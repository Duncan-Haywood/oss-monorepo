The Box That Keeps the Boil

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Thomas's parish (invented), Kot Sain Dad (invented), a market town in Mirpurkhas district, Sindh, Pakistan, Tuesday 1 December to Thursday 24 December 2048 · 28 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

In the cold season after a flood, a parish kitchen in Sindh feeds four hundred displaced people a day and has cut its firewood nearly in half by finishing its chickpeas in straw boxes built by a displaced physics teacher. When the stew for a second camp has to travel, the cook splits it into four small pots, each in its own crate wrapped in a quilt, and at serving time the food is lukewarm and the chickpeas are hard. The cook says the fire is cheaper than a sick child. A kettle and a teacup wrapped in the same quilt show why a small pot loses its heat so much faster, and the fix is four full pots packed into one box, a reboil at the far end, and a rule written on the kitchen wall.

Parveen Masih had cooked for the parish of Saint Thomas in Kot Sain Dad for twenty-six years, and in all that time she had never once let a pot of chana go off the fire before it was soft.

Since October she had done it every morning. She did not like it. She brought the three big pots to a hard boil on the fire in the yard behind the parish hall, the black kala chana rolling in the water with the onions and the masala she had fried that morning, and let them boil a quarter of an hour, and then she and her niece Shazia and whichever two men were nearest lifted each pot off the bricks with a pole through the handles and carried it, slopping and hissing, to one of the boxes standing in a row against the hall wall. The boxes were wooden crates, the size of a small cupboard, lined inside with a hand's depth of wheat straw and then with old quilts. The pot went down into a hollow in the straw, the lid went on, a straw-filled cushion went on top of the lid, a quilt went on top of the cushion, and the box was shut. Then everyone went away and did something else for four hours. At half past one, when the queue had formed along the compound wall, Parveen opened the first box and lifted the cushion and the lid, and the steam came up in her face as if the pot had never left the fire, and the chana was soft.

It had been soft every day for two months. It had been soft the first morning, which she had not believed, and the second, which she had put down to luck. On the third morning she had stood over the box with the long probe of the thermometer the parish had bought in September and read ninety degrees, four hours after the pot had left the fire, and she had gone and sat down on the hall step for a minute.

"Ninety," said the parish tablet, which had been propped on the windowsill of the hall kitchen since the floods and was called Paimana, which means a measure. It logged every reading the probe sent it. "That is about what I would expect for that box. You put the pot in at ninety-eight."

"It should be fifty," Parveen had said. "A pot off the fire goes cold."

"A pot off the fire in the open air goes cold. In the box it has very little way to lose its heat. The straw does not let it out."

She had not answered that, because it was obviously true and she did not want to say so. The box was not hers. It belonged to Ghulam Rasool Khoso, who had taught physics at the government high school in Goth Bachal Shah until the water came into it in August, and who now lived with his wife and their three children in a tent in the parish compound, between the hall and the clergy house, with three hundred and ninety-six other people.

The flood had come in the last week of August, after three weeks of rain that the old people said was worse than 2022 and the young people said was worse than anything. The canals broke, the low villages to the south went under, and the people who had roofs on high ground came to the people who had walls. Kot Sain Dad was on a rise. It had a government boys' school with a big walled ground, a mosque with a courtyard, a mandir by the old market, and Saint Thomas's, which had a compound, a hall, a well and a priest, Father Emmanuel Gill, who had opened the gates on the first night and had not shut them since.

By the middle of September there were four hundred people in the parish compound and a hundred and sixty in the school ground at Goth Haji Bachal, seven kilometres out along the canal road, where the water had come up to the wall and stopped. The district sent flour and lentils and rice. The diocese sent tents and money. A charity in Karachi sent plastic sheeting and a water filter that nobody could make work. What nobody sent was enough wood.

Wood was the thing. Every tree that had stood in the water was dead or dying and nobody would sell it while it might still live; the dry wood was in the villages that had drowned; and everyone in four districts wanted to cook. In August the timber yard by the bypass sold firewood at a price per forty-kilo maund that Parveen had paid for years without thinking about it. By October it was three times that. The parish kitchen fed four hundred people twice a day, rice and chana or rice and daal at noon and roti and whatever was left in the evening, and it burned four and a half maunds of wood a day doing it, and Father Gill had sat at the kitchen table one evening with the parish accounts on the tablet and Paimana's figures beside them and said, quietly, that at that price the wood would take the diocese's money before Christmas.

Ghulam Rasool had been sitting at the end of the table with a cup of tea, because he usually was. He was a tall, stooped man of about fifty with grey in his beard and a teacher's way of waiting until a room had finished talking.

"My mother cooked in a pit," he said. "Not every day. For weddings, when there was a big pot of meat and not enough fire for all of it. She dug a hole in the ground and lined it with straw, and put the pot in boiling, and covered it, and the meat cooked all night. In England they call it a haybox. In the war they used them, when there was no fuel. It is in the old physics books, as an example."

"An example of what?" said Father Gill.

"Of heat that has nowhere to go. A pot of boiling water is full of heat. It does not need more heat to stay hot. It needs to stop losing what it has. If you stop it losing, it keeps cooking."

Parveen had said it was a story for weddings and that chana was not meat. Then she had said that she did not have time to dig holes. Then Ghulam Rasool had said he would build the boxes, and he had, from crates the flour had come in and straw from the harvest that was still standing in a field the water had not reached and quilts that the women in the compound had given him, and now it was December and the kitchen burned two and a half maunds a day instead of four and a half, because the rice still needed the fire and the chana did not.

On Tuesday the first of December the headmaster at Goth Haji Bachal sent word that the school would open again on Monday the seventh.

He sent it politely, by Yaqoob, who drove the parish's qingqi, the motorcycle rickshaw with a flat bed behind and a canvas roof over it, and who took the flour and the rice out to the school camp every other day. The children of Goth Haji Bachal had not been to school since August. The government wanted them back. The families in the classrooms would move into tents on the playing ground, and Mr Jamali was very sorry, and he would like there to be no more cooking fires inside the wall, because there would be children in the yard and the women had been burning cotton sticks against the classroom wall for three months and the plaster was black to the windows.

"They can cook outside the wall," said Yaqoob.

"Outside the wall is the road," said Parveen. "And on the other side of the road is the canal."

"Then we cook here and I take it," said Yaqoob.

So on Wednesday and Thursday and Friday the kitchen at Saint Thomas's cooked for five hundred and sixty instead of four hundred, and Parveen thought about pots.

The big pots were forty litres. A forty-litre pot full of chana weighs as much as a man, more with the pot, and it had taken two men and a pole to get one onto the qingqi on Wednesday and two women and a lot of shouting to get it off at the far end. On Thursday the pot had tipped on the rutted stretch past the brick kiln and a third of it had gone over the bed of the qingqi and onto the road. And at the school there was no line, the way there was a line along the parish wall; the school camp was four groups of families from four drowned villages, and each group had its own corner of the playing ground and its own woman who saw to the food, and they did not much like standing in one queue together. On Friday Dhani, a Kolhi woman from Goth Rano who saw to her corner as if it were a small kingdom, told Yaqoob to tell the cook that one pot for four corners was one pot for four arguments.

"Four pots," said Parveen on Saturday morning, to the kitchen. "Small ones. One for each corner, one woman can carry it. Ten litres."

"Then four boxes," said Shazia, who was nineteen and thought ahead.

"Small boxes," said Parveen. "The big ones won't go on the qingqi."

Ghulam Rasool was not there. He had gone to Mirpurkhas for the day, to the education office, to ask whether his school would open again or whether its children would be sent to Goth Haji Bachal too. So Shazia found four plastic crates of the kind the vegetable sellers used, and lined each one with a folded quilt, and Parveen tried a ten-litre pot in one and it fitted, with the quilt pulled up round its sides and folded over the lid.

"It's the same thing," she said. "Pot in, cover it, keep the heat in. It's the same as the big box, smaller."

Nobody said otherwise. Paimana was not asked.

On Monday the seventh Parveen boiled the chana in four pots at once on the fire in the yard, alongside the three big ones, and at half past nine she and Shazia ladled each small pot down to eight litres, so that there was a hand's depth of room under the lid and nothing would slop out on the road, and set each one in its crate and wrapped it in its quilt and tied the quilt with string. The big pots went into their boxes along the wall. At half past one the parish queue had its lunch, and the steam came up out of the big pots as it always did.

At a quarter to four Yaqoob loaded the four crates onto the qingqi, along with the rice in a sack-wrapped tub, and Parveen climbed up beside him in the front, because she wanted to see her four pots served and because she had not left the compound in a week.

It was the shortest kind of day. The sun was already low over the cotton fields to the west, long and yellow, and the wind off the fields had a cold edge to it that came straight through the canvas sides of the qingqi. The cotton had been picked in the fields that had not flooded, and the stalks were cut and stacked in bundles along the field edges for burning, and in the flooded fields there was only grey mud cracked into plates. A heron stood in a ditch. The qingqi bounced over the bad place by the brick kiln and Parveen put her hand back on the nearest crate without thinking, as if it were a child.

At the school the families were in their tents on the playing ground, blue tents in rows, and the children were out of them, everywhere, because the children were always out of them. The four women came for their pots. Dhani came first. She untied the string on her crate with quick fingers and unwrapped the quilt and took the lid off, and nothing came up.

No steam. There was a little haze over the pot, the way there is over a cup of tea that somebody has forgotten. Dhani put her finger in the chana and then put it in her mouth, and her face did a thing that Parveen had seen a thousand times on the faces of children given medicine.

"It's warm," said Dhani. "It's not hot. And it's hard." She took a chana out and pressed it between her finger and thumb, and it did not crush; it split, with a pale dry heart.

Parveen took the probe out of her bag and pushed it down into the middle of the pot. The number came up on the little screen and, a moment later, on Paimana's in the hall kitchen seven kilometres away.

Forty.

"It came out of the fire at ninety-six," Parveen said. "This morning. I put it in myself."

The other three pots were the same: forty, forty-one, thirty-nine. The chana in all of them was hard in the middle. The women did not argue with her. They did not need to. Dhani took her pot to the far side of the ground, behind the tents, where a heap of cotton sticks was stacked for the morning, and in ten minutes there were four small fires burning on the bare earth by the back wall of the school, and four pots on bricks over them, and the smoke going up past the windows of the classroom where the children would sit tomorrow. Mr Jamali came out of the office and stood at the end of the row of tents, looking at the smoke and the black plaster, and then went back in again without saying anything, which was worse.

It was nearly dark when the chana was soft. They served it by the light of the fires and a phone torch. Parveen and Yaqoob drove back along the canal road in the dark with the empty crates rattling behind them, and she did not say anything the whole way, and Yaqoob, who was twenty-three and had been driven home by his mother, knew better than to start.

Tuesday the eighth was the Immaculate Conception, and Father Gill said Mass at seven in the hall with the doors open and the tents outside and the women from the compound singing the psalms in Punjabi at the back. Parveen went, as she always did, and prayed, as she always did, mainly about pots.

Afterwards she found Ghulam Rasool and Father Gill at the kitchen table. Paimana's screen showed a graph of four lines falling from the top left to the bottom right.

"I know," said Ghulam Rasool. "I saw. I'm sorry. I should have been here."

"It is not your fault," said Parveen. "It is not anybody's fault. It is a box. It is for the big pots. For the big pots it works, God be thanked, and we have saved two maunds a day. For the small pots it does not work, and I am not sending cold chana to a hundred and sixty people so that we can save wood. I will buy wood. Shazia will go out on the qingqi at noon with the pots and the wood and cook it there, outside the wall, on the road if we have to."

"The price—" said Father Gill.

"The fire is cheaper than a sick child, Father." She said it quietly. She had been saying it to herself since the brick kiln. "You know what happens to beans that sit warm all afternoon. I have seen it. A whole wedding in 2031. Twenty people in the hospital in Mirpurkhas, and two of them children."

Nobody at the table said she was wrong, because she was not.

"Paimana," said Ghulam Rasool. "Tell her the numbers. Not what to do. What happened."

"The probe sent readings from three of the four pots on the way and at the school," said the tablet, "and I had the big boxes from the morning. I can't see the small pots before a quarter to four, but I can work them from where they started and where they ended. The four small pots went into their crates at about ninety-six degrees at half past nine and reached about forty by half past four, seven hours later. They fell below eighty-five, which is about where your chana stops softening in the box, within the first hour. They fell below sixty, which is the line the World Health Organization gives for keeping cooked food hot before you serve it, by about one o'clock. So the chana spent about three and a half hours cooling through the range where food should not be kept, and it was hard because it stopped cooking before half past ten. Parveen is right that it should not have been served like that, and right that the women were right to boil it again."

"And the big pots," said Ghulam Rasool.

"The big pots went in at ninety-eight at half past nine. At half past one they were at ninety. If they had stayed in the box until half past four they would have been at about eighty-five."

Parveen looked at the graph, the big pot's line almost flat across the top and the four little ones dropping away under it like stones off a roof.

"Then the small box is no good," she said. "That's what I said."

"The small box is a crate with one quilt in it," said Ghulam Rasool. "It is not the same box. But that is not the main thing. Even in the same box, the small pot would go cold faster. Will you let me show you? It will take forty minutes, and you can make lunch while it does."

He made tea. He made it the ordinary way, in the big aluminium kettle on the gas ring in the hall kitchen, milk and sugar and cardamom boiled up together until the colour was right, and then he poured a cup of it, a full teacup, and left a litre and a half in the kettle, and he took two of the quilts off the empty crates and wrapped the kettle in one and the cup in the other, each tightly, the same thickness of quilt round each, and stood them side by side on the windowsill next to Paimana.

The quilts were rallis, the patchwork quilts that every house in Sindh has, made by women from old cloth cut in squares and triangles and sewn in rows by hand, in red and white and black and yellow. One had a border of little white triangles like teeth. Parveen knew whose it was: Zubaida's, Ghulam Rasool's wife's, one of the two things she had carried out of their house in August with the water at her knees. The other was the parish's.

"Ninety," said Paimana, when he had put the probe in each, and he wrote it down on the back of a flour sack with a pencil, because he was a teacher and liked to see numbers written.

Then Parveen went and made lunch, and Shazia went and fetched the boys who carried the pots, and Father Gill went to answer the phone, and the tea stood on the windowsill in its quilts.

At twenty minutes Ghulam Rasool unwrapped both and put the probe in each. Paimana read them out. "The kettle is at eighty. The cup is at sixty-one."

He wrapped them again. At forty minutes Parveen came back in with her hands floury and stood beside him while he unwrapped them.

"The kettle seventy-one," said Paimana. "The cup forty-three."

Ghulam Rasool held the cup out to her. She took it and drank it, all of it, in four swallows, because it was the temperature of tea that you can drink all of in four swallows. Then he poured her a cup from the kettle, and she held it in both hands and blew on it.

"Same tea," he said. "Same ralli. Same windowsill. Same forty minutes. The kettle has ten times as much tea in it as the cup, and ten times as much heat. But it doesn't have ten times as much outside. It has, what, three times? Four? The heat goes out through the outside. So the kettle has ten times the heat to lose and only three times the door to lose it by, and it keeps hot three times as long."

"A small pot has more outside for its inside," said Paimana. "Four ten-litre pots wrapped up separately have about half as much outside again as one forty-litre pot. And the crates had only one quilt each, folded, which is thinner than the straw in the big boxes, and the wind came through the plastic on the road. And the pots were only eight litres full, so there was air under the lid, which carries heat out faster than chana does. Each of those made it worse. The worst one is the size."

"Put a quarter of the chana in the big box and it goes cold four times as fast," said Ghulam Rasool, "because it has a quarter of the heat and the box loses the same. Give it a small box of its own and it is better, but not enough: it still has more outside for its inside. And we made four pots each a quarter the size and put them in worse boxes. Of course they went cold. It's not your fault either. It looks like the same thing. It's the same pot, smaller. Nobody thinks a cup of tea will go cold faster than a kettle until they drink one."

Parveen finished the tea from the kettle. It was still hot at the bottom.

"So," she said. "Big pots don't fit on the qingqi, and four corners won't eat out of one pot, and small pots go cold. What, then?"

"Then the small pots go out together," he said. "In one box."

They built it on Wednesday.

It was Ghulam Rasool and Yaqoob and two of the boys from the compound, with Paimana on the windowsill working the numbers and Parveen coming out of the kitchen every half hour to tell them what was wrong with it. It was a box the size of the qingqi's bed, as wide as the bed and a little shorter, built from the boards of two flour crates and a door that the parish had taken off the old storeroom in 2044 and never thrown away. Inside, it was straw, a hand's depth on the floor and the sides and more on top, and in the straw four hollows in a square, so that the four pots would stand touching, side by side, like four people sharing a blanket.

"They warm each other," said Ghulam Rasool. "Each one's outside is the next one's outside. Four pots packed together have the outside of one big pot, nearly, not four small ones."

"And full," said Paimana. "Fill them to nine and a half litres, not eight. Less air, more chana, more heat in the pot."

"It will slop," said Parveen.

"Then a cloth under the lid, tied," said Yaqoob, who would be the one cleaning it if it did. "And I will drive slower past the kiln."

"I have worked the new box out," said Paimana. "It is a model, with my guesses for the straw and the gaps in the boards, so treat it as a guess until the probe says otherwise. Four full pots packed together in that box, starting at ninety-six at half past nine, should be at about eighty-one when you get to the school at four. They should stay above eighty-five until about a quarter past two, so nearly five hours of cooking, which is about what the chana has had in the big boxes when it has been soft. They will not get down to sixty before tomorrow."

"Eighty-one is not boiling," said Parveen.

"No," said Paimana. "And I would not want you to trust my eighty-one with a hundred and sixty people's food. That's the other half."

The other half was a two-ring gas stove that belonged to the parish's youth group, who used it for their tea at the summer camp, and a cylinder of gas, which cost money, and Mr Jamali's permission, which cost Father Gill a phone call and Ghulam Rasool an hour in the headmaster's office on Wednesday afternoon. He came back on the qingqi at five with the permission and with something else: Mr Jamali had taught with him, years before, at the same school in Mirpurkhas, and had not recognised him until he spoke.

"He says a gas stove on bricks by the gate is not a fire," said Ghulam Rasool. "He says that is the law, as far as he is concerned, and the plaster agrees with him. And he says, if it is soup for the children in the school as well, on the first cold days, he would not object to that either."

So the rule was this. The pots would go out together in the big box. At the school, before anyone was served, each pot would go on the gas and come back to a rolling boil, and stay there for a few minutes, stirred, and only then go to its corner. Two pots at a time on two rings. Paimana said a ten-litre pot at eighty would take about a quarter of an hour on one of those rings to come back to the boil, so the four would take about half an hour, so the qingqi would have to be there by four for food at half past.

That evening Parveen asked Ghulam Rasool to write it on the kitchen wall. He wrote it in Urdu in big letters with a stick of charcoal from the fire, above the row of boxes, and then underneath it in Sindhi, because Dhani read Sindhi and not Urdu, and the next cook, whoever that was, might be from anywhere.

Boil it again before you serve it.

"And the small boxes," said Ghulam Rasool. "For the days the pots go separately, if there are any. A box the size of the pot. Straw all round, not one quilt. I'll make four." He looked at the crates stacked by the door, the plastic vegetable crates with their single folded rallis. "And I need my wife's quilt back for the big one. There aren't enough. The big box needs two on top, at least."

"Not Zubaida's," said Parveen. "Use the parish's. Take mine from the house."

"She said to use hers," he said. "She said it should go where it keeps something warm. It was her mother's. She said her mother would have liked it to be useful." He paused. "She also said if a single chana spills on it she will come and find Yaqoob."

On Thursday the tenth the qingqi left Saint Thomas's at twenty-five to four with the big box roped to its bed, Zubaida's ralli on top of the straw on top of the four lids, the white triangles round its border like a row of teeth. Parveen rode in front again. Ghulam Rasool rode in the back with the box, holding on to the canvas frame, and Shazia beside him with the gas cylinder between her knees.

It was colder than Monday. The wind came in across the cut cotton fields with nothing in its way, and the sun was low and white behind a haze. Yaqoob took the bad stretch by the brick kiln at the speed of a man walking, and the box did not move, and nothing slopped, and at the school gate he stopped the qingqi beside a square of bricks that Dhani's son had set out on the ground that morning on Mr Jamali's instructions, exactly one metre from the wall.

Parveen got down and went round to the back. Ghulam Rasool had already lifted the ralli off and folded it, carefully, and handed it to Shazia, and pulled the straw cushion off the top, and the steam came up out of the box. Not a haze. Steam, white, thick, standing up into the cold air and bending away in the wind.

Parveen put the probe into the nearest pot.

"Eighty-one," said Paimana, from her bag.

"You said eighty-one," said Ghulam Rasool.

"I said about eighty-one. I'm pleased."

The first two pots went on the gas. Dhani and the other three women came to the gate and stood round the stove, and Parveen took the lid off the first pot and stirred it, and the chana turned over in the brown gravy, and she took one out on the spoon and blew on it and pressed it between her finger and thumb, and it crushed. She held it out to Dhani. Dhani ate it, and said nothing, and nodded once, which from Dhani was a speech.

The pots came to the boil one after another, and the women carried them away to their corners. It took thirty-five minutes, five more than Paimana had said, because the wind kept blowing the gas flames sideways until Yaqoob stood in front of the stove with his back to it like a wall. The sun was nearly down over the cotton fields. By the time the last pot went to the last corner the tents had gone gold and then grey and the children were sitting on the ground in rows with their plates on their knees, and the only thing rising over the school ground was the steam off the chana.

Mr Jamali came out of the office and stood at the end of the row of tents. This time he came over. He looked at the gas stove on its square of bricks, one metre from his wall, and at the wall, and at Ghulam Rasool.

"You were always the one who did the experiments," he said.

"She did the experiment," said Ghulam Rasool, nodding at Parveen. "On Monday. It went wrong. That's how you know what the right answer is."

The wood bill for December came to about two and three-quarter maunds a day, with the school camp's food included and the gas cylinder on top. Father Gill showed it to the diocese with Paimana's graphs and the photograph Shazia had taken of the wall, and the diocese sent money for a second cylinder and asked whether the parish in Umerkot, which was feeding three hundred, could send someone to see the boxes. Ghulam Rasool made four small boxes the size of the ten-litre pots, with straw all round, which were used twice, on days when one corner of the school camp was away at a wedding and the pots went out separately, and they kept the chana above sixty all afternoon, which is what Paimana said they would do. The plastic crates went back to the vegetable sellers.

On the evening of the twenty-fourth there was rice and chana and halwa at Saint Thomas's for Bara Din, Christmas, for the four hundred in the compound and anyone else who came to the gate, which was most of Kot Sain Dad. At the school camp there was the same, carried out in the big box at twenty-five to four, Zubaida's ralli on top, and reboiled at the gate, and served before the sun went down at twenty to six. Most of the hundred and sixty were Muslim and some were Hindu, and Dhani's corner had decided, without asking anyone, that a feast was a feast.

Parveen went out with it. She stood by the gas stove in the dusk while the last pot came back to the boil, with her shawl round her head against the wind and her hand on the warm side of the box, out of habit, as if it were a child. Behind her on the canal road the qingqi ticked as it cooled. In the town, seven kilometres back, the bell at Saint Thomas's would be ringing soon for the night Mass, and Father Gill would be in the hall with the doors open and the tents outside, and the women singing at the back.

The chana came up to the boil, slowly, a bubble and then another bubble and then all of it at once, rolling. Parveen turned the flame down and stirred it once, all the way round, and lifted the pot off the ring by its handles and gave it to Dhani, and the steam went up between them into the cold.

The haybox, or retained-heat cooker, is old and real: a pot brought to the boil is shut in an insulated box and finishes cooking in its own heat, and it was widely promoted to save fuel in Britain in both world wars and is used in many places today. The food-safety lines are from the World Health Organization's Five Keys to Safer Food (WHO, 2006), whose fourth key, "keep food at safe temperatures", advises keeping cooked food piping hot, above 60 °C, before serving and not leaving cooked food at room temperature for more than two hours. Some bacteria that cause food poisoning form spores that survive boiling and can grow in cooked food that sits warm, which is why cooling food through the warm range is the danger, not the boiling. The softening threshold for the chana (about 85 °C, and about four to five hours above it after a quarter of an hour's boil) is the story's kitchen rule of thumb, not a measured figure.

The model. Each pot cools as T(t) = T_air + (T0 − T_air)·e^(−t/τ), with τ = C ÷ UA, where C is the heat capacity of the pot and its contents and UA the heat lost per degree through its box. The chana is taken as 3,900 J/kg·K, a litre as a kilogram, and steel as 500 J/kg·K. The day's air is taken as 16 °C throughout, a stand-in for a cold morning and a mild afternoon. A big pot holds 36 litres in a 5 kg pot (C about 143 kJ/K) in a straw box with UA 1.0 W/K, so τ is about 40 hours: from 98 °C it is about 90 °C after four hours and about 85 °C after seven, and stays above 85 °C for about 6.9 hours. A small pot holds 8 litres in a 1.5 kg pot (C about 32 kJ/K) in a crate with one quilt, UA taken as 1.5 W/K for the thin quilt, the air gap and the wind, so τ is about 5.9 hours: from 96 °C it falls below 85 °C in about 0.9 hours, below 60 °C in about 3.5 hours, and is about 40 °C after seven hours. Four full small pots of 9.5 litres each (C about 38 kJ/K each, 151 together) packed together in one straw box with UA 1.3 W/K have τ about 32 hours: from 96 °C they are about 81 °C after six and a half hours, stay above 85 °C for about 4.8 hours, and do not reach 60 °C for about 19 hours. Reheating one 9.5-litre pot from 80 °C to 98 °C takes about 0.67 MJ, about a quarter of an hour on a gas ring delivering about 750 W to the pot. For the tea, a kettle of 1.5 litres (with 0.5 kg of aluminium taken as steel for simplicity) in a quilt with UA 0.8 W/K has τ about 2.3 hours, and a cup of 0.15 litres (with 0.2 kg of china) in a quilt with UA 0.3 W/K has τ about 0.7 hours: from 90 °C the kettle is about 80 °C after twenty minutes and 71 °C after forty, the cup about 61 °C and 43 °C. Heat capacity grows with volume and surface with roughly the two-thirds power of volume, so a quarter of the contents in the same box cools about four times as fast, and a pot a quarter the size in a box scaled to it, with the same thickness of insulation, about one and a half times as fast (four to the one-third power); the story's small crates were also worse boxes, which is why its small pots cool about seven times as fast as the big one. Every conductance, temperature and fuel figure is the story's model, chosen to be plausible, not measured on any real box, pot or kitchen. This is fiction, not food-safety advice; anyone cooking in a retained-heat box for other people should bring the food back to a full boil before serving and follow their local food-safety authority's guidance.

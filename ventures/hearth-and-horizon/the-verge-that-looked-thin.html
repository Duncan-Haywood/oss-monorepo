The Verge That Looked Thin

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Cedd's churchyard (invented), near Bradwell-on-Sea, Essex, Saturday 4 November 2051 · 5 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

Eighteen bags of daffodil bulbs arrive for a long churchyard verge, bought by the label. A volunteer says the spread looks thin, and the parish assistant shows that both the label and the volunteer are right about different gardens, then helps the parish pay for the one it wants.

The bags came on a pallet from the nursery in Maldon and stood in the porch in a green wall, eighteen of them, fifty bulbs to a bag. Tessa Marlowe, who had organised the planting, read the label out to the tablet on the lychgate post. "Covers two square metres. We have a verge twenty-four metres long and one and a half wide. I did it on the back of the notice sheet."

"Thirty-six square metres," said the parish assistant. "At two metres a bag that is eighteen bags, nine hundred bulbs. Your sum is right."

"There," said Tessa, and went to find the trowels.

Colm Fennessy, who had planted the old verge as a boy and distrusted anything done to a measure, had been walking the line with a ball of string. He came back with his cap in his hand. "It'll look thin," he said. "Nine hundred along that? You'll see the grass between."

"I think you may be right about what you will see," said the assistant. "May I say what the label is promising?"

"Nine hundred bulbs over thirty-six square metres is twenty-five to the square metre," it said. "Each one has four hundred square centimetres to itself, so the bulbs sit twenty centimetres apart. That is a normal spacing for daffodils left to naturalise, which is what the nursery means by two square metres a bag. The leaves in March will touch. The flowers, one stem to a bulb in the first year, will be a hand and a bit apart. It is a drift, not a carpet."

"And the carpet?" said Colm.

"Bedding displays plant much closer. I have seen twelve centimetres quoted, but I am going from memory of gardening guides here, not a measurement. At twelve and a quarter centimetres each bulb has about a hundred and fifty square centimetres, so the verge would take 2,400 bulbs, forty-eight bags. That is more than two and a half times the order."

"Two and a half times," said Tessa, from the porch. "I heard that."

"I would not call either one the correct answer," said the assistant. "They are two different verges. The sheet in your hand will give you the first. Colm is describing the second. Which do the people of Saint Cedd's want to drive past in March?"

They argued it, as people do, over tea from the urn. Mrs Okonkwo-Reid said a thin verge would shame them in front of Bradwell Chapel's, which was planted twenty to the metre and looked, she said, like a tablecloth. Colm said a carpet cost the earth. Dymphna Hale, the treasurer, asked what the earth came to.

"Four pounds a bag, on the nursery's sheet," said the assistant. "That is eighteen bags, seventy-two pounds, or forty-eight bags, a hundred and ninety-two. Those are the nursery's prices from this morning; I would check them if the order changes."

"A hundred and twenty pounds' difference," said Dymphna, slowly. "That is the roof appeal's cake sale."

"There is a middle. If you plant thirty bags, 1,500 bulbs, that is about forty-two to the metre, one per 240 square centimetres, fifteen and a half centimetres apart. I do not know that it is the right middle. It is where the arithmetic puts a number between the two pictures you have each been describing."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Mrs Okonkwo-Reid said, "Thirty," in the voice she used for the hymn numbers, and Dymphna wrote it down before anyone could say otherwise.

There was the question of the holes, and Tessa, who had never planted a bulb in her life and had sworn she would not be the person who put them in upside down, asked it. "How deep?"

"The rule I have seen is three times the height of the bulb," said the assistant. "A daffodil bulb is about five centimetres from base to tip, so fifteen centimetres. That is from memory of the growers' guidance, not from a test, and your soil is clay near the estuary, which may want a little less. Put the pointed end up and the flat, rough end down where the roots will come. If it is the wrong way up it will probably turn itself round, slowly, but I would not ask it to."

"How many holes is that?" said Colm. "Fifteen hundred holes."

"With a bulb planter about seven centimetres across, each hole is about 580 cubic centimetres of soil. Fifteen hundred of them is just under nine tenths of a cubic metre, about sixteen full wheelbarrows. Where will you put it? The soil that comes out is more than the bulbs will fill."

"On the vestry compost," said Tessa. "I had a feeling."

"Then the last number is time. If one hole, one bulb and one step to the next takes twenty-five seconds, and I am guessing that figure, which Colm can correct in the first ten minutes, then 1,500 holes is ten and a half hours. Ten people at a Saturday morning is an hour each, and most of that is standing up again."

"I can correct it now," said Colm. "It's thirty seconds. Clay."

"Then twelve and a half hours, and an hour and a quarter for ten."

They planted on the first fine Saturday, in cold sun, ten people along a string line that Colm kept re-pegging. The rule they used for the spacing was the simplest the assistant could give them, and it was Tessa's idea to paint it on the lychgate post for next year: two hand-widths between bulbs, deep as your hand and a half. The assistant said it had measured nothing of the sort and that a hand-width varied, and Tessa said that was why she liked it.

The tablet counted bags as they emptied, thirty of them, the last two with a few bulbs soft and left out, which Dymphna wrote up honestly as 1,497 planted. Colm stayed after the others had gone to rake the loose soil off the path. The light was going, long over the water, and somewhere out on the marsh the geese were coming in.

In March, the assistant had said, they would see how it looked, and then decide about the next year. It had not promised him anything about March.

The Lap That Was Not a Quarter-Mile

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Wilfrid's playing field (invented), near Ripon, North Yorkshire, Saturday 14 June 2053 · 4 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A parish sponsored walk has pledges "per mile" and a notice that calls each lap a quarter-mile. The assistant wheels the path, finds it is 380 metres, and shows what that does to the money owed.

The notice had been on the gate for three weeks, and it said, in Gerry Oldroyd's best marker pen, ONE LAP IS A QUARTER-MILE. FOUR LAPS, ONE MILE. SPONSOR SHEETS AT THE TABLE.

It was the morning before the walk, and Gerry and the parish assistant were at the edge of Saint Wilfrid's field with a trundle wheel borrowed from the school. The assistant was called Bede. It spoke from a phone clipped to Gerry's coat, and it had asked him, a little carefully, whether anybody had measured the lap.

"It's a quarter-mile," said Gerry. "Everyone knows it's a quarter-mile. The old cricket boundary."

"May I ask you to walk it with the wheel? It costs ten minutes. If I am wrong I will say so on the notice."

He did, grumbling, round the hedge, past the oak, along the brook and back to the gate. The wheel clicked over at 380 metres.

"A quarter of a mile is 402 metres and a bit," said Bede. "Four hundred and two point three. So your lap is 380 over 402.3, which is about 0.944 of a quarter-mile. Five and a half per cent short."

"Twenty-two metres. Nobody will walk twenty-two metres less and notice."

"Nobody will notice on the day. The trouble is the sheets. A sponsor who writes two pounds a mile is promising money for distance, and the walker's tally is in laps. Take Hannah Brook, who is down for twenty-three laps. By the notice that is 5.75 miles. By the wheel it is 23 times 380, or 8,740 metres, which is 5.43 miles. The notice is claiming 0.32 of a mile that she did not walk. At two pounds a mile, that is 64 pence from one sponsor, and she will have several."

Gerry looked at the wheel as if it had said something rude. "Sixty-four pence."

"Per walker, per two pounds. Last year's thirty walkers did about twenty laps each and had an average of about three pounds a mile on their sheets. Twenty laps by the notice is five miles. By the wheel it is 4.72. The gap is 0.28 of a mile, times three pounds, times thirty walkers: about twenty-five pounds the parish would ask for and nobody had earned. It is not a fortune. It is exactly the kind of number a sponsor checks afterwards."

There was a second problem, and Bede raised it the way it raised all of them, gently, as a question.

"The sheets say per mile. If a walker does twenty-one laps, is that five miles?"

Gerry did the sum on the back of his hand. "Twenty-one quarter-miles. Five and a bit."

"Twenty-one of these laps is 7,980 metres, or 4.96 miles. It misses the fifth mile by 64 metres. At 4.235 laps a mile you need 21.2 laps for five miles, which means twenty-two on the tally. Twenty-two laps is 5.19 miles."

"So the poor soul on twenty-one gets four miles."

"If you pay only for whole miles completed, yes: nearly a pound of every pound pledged gone, for a mile she missed by 64 metres. If you pay pro rata, she gets 4.96, which is within a few pence of five. I do not think the sheets should choose between those by accident. I would ask the sponsors, which is the right way to do it, but the sheets are printed. May I suggest what I would put on the tally table?"

"Go on."

"One rule, written on a card, readable at the table. Each lap is 380 metres. One mile is 4.24 laps. Money owed is laps times 0.236 times the pledge per mile, to the nearest penny. Then Hannah's twenty-three laps is 5.43 miles, and at two pounds it is ten pounds eighty-six. A walker who stops at nineteen is paid for nineteen. Nobody is paid for a lap, or a quarter-mile, they did not walk."

They took the notice down and Gerry wrote a new one on the back of it, because the marker was still wet. The tally cards went on the table with the 380 and the 0.236 at the top. Bede worked out a little ready reckoner for the commonest pledges, pounds per lap: a pound a mile is 24 pence a lap, two pounds is 47, three is 71, five is 118, and it ran off the others to the half penny and printed them on the back of the card.

On the day it rained for an hour and then did not. Hannah Brook did twenty-three laps and owed ten pounds eighty-six from the sponsor at two a mile; that sponsor, the doctor, paid eleven and said keep the change. Gerry's own sheet came to four laps, because of his knee, and he stood at the gate with the trundle wheel under his arm and counted people past it.

"Twenty-two metres," he said to nobody. "I'd have sworn."

One mile is 1,609.344 metres exactly, so a quarter-mile is 402.336 m and a 380 m lap is 0.2361 mile (4.235 laps to the mile). 23 laps is 8,740 m = 5.431 miles against 5.75 claimed (difference 0.319, or 64 pence at £2 a mile); 20 laps is 4.722 miles against 5.0, and 30 walkers at an assumed £3 a mile would be about £25 over; 21 laps is 4.959 miles and 22 is 5.195. The lap length, the pledges and the walker numbers are invented. The pence-per-lap figures are 380/1609.344 times the pledge, rounded (£1: 23.6p, £2: 47.2p, £3: 70.8p, £5: 118p).

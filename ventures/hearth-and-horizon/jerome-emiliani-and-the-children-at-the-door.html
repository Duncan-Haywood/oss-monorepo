Jerome Emiliani and the Children at the Door

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Venice and the villages near Bergamo, in the north of Italy, about five hundred years ago · 9 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Jerome Emiliani, a Venetian soldier who was taken prisoner and held in chains in 1511, escaped (he thanked Our Lady for it and left his chains at her shrine in Treviso), and then, in the hungry years that followed, opened his own house to children with no one to look after them, fed them, taught them a trade and their prayers, and became a father to them. He died at Somasca in 1537 after nursing the sick, and is patron of orphans and abandoned children. For ages 3 to 8, about nine minutes read aloud. Jerome is remembered on the 8th of February.

Once upon a time, about five hundred years ago, in the city of Venice, there lived a boy named Jerome.

Venice is a city built on the sea. Instead of streets it has canals, and instead of cars it has boats, and the houses stand with their feet in the water. Jerome's family was an important family there. They had a fine house, and good clothes, and servants to carry things.

When Jerome grew up, he became a soldier. He was strong and proud, and he liked to win. If you had asked him then, "Jerome, what do you want most?", he might have said, "To be brave, and to be important, and to be right."

He was not yet thinking very much about God.

In those days Venice was at war. And Jerome was sent to guard a castle up in the hills, where a river comes down out of the mountains. The castle was called Castelnuovo, which means "the new castle", though it was not new at all.

The enemy came. There were a great many of them. Jerome and his soldiers fought hard, but the castle fell, and Jerome was taken prisoner.

They put him in a dark room under the castle. They put a heavy chain around his ankles, and another around his neck, and they fastened the chains to a big iron ball, so that he could hardly move.

It was very dark in that room, and very cold, and very quiet.

And there, in the dark, with nothing to fight and nobody to give orders to, Jerome began to pray. Perhaps for the first time in a long time, he prayed with all his heart. He prayed to Mary, the Mother of Jesus. He said something like this:

"Holy Mary, I have not been good. I have thought only about myself. But if you help me, I will change. I will give my life to God."

Now listen carefully, because this part is wonderful.

One night, after some weeks in the dark, Jerome got free. Some people tell it that Mary herself came into the room, with a light, and opened the chains. Jerome himself said that he asked Our Lady to lead him safely home, and that she did. However it happened, he found himself outside, in the night, with the enemy soldiers all around.

And he walked. Quietly, quietly, through the enemy camp, past the tents and the sleeping soldiers and the horses, and nobody saw him.

He walked all the way to the city of Treviso, where there was a church of Our Lady. And he went in, and knelt down, and he laid his chains in front of her altar. As if to say: "Thank you. I am not a prisoner any more. And now I belong to God."

Jerome kept his promise. But not all at once. Changing is slow, even for saints.

He went back to Venice. He prayed more. He read the Bible. He went to confession and to Mass. Little by little, the proud soldier was becoming someone else.

And then hard times came to Venice and the lands around it. The harvests failed, and there was not enough bread. Then a sickness came that spread from house to house. Many grown-ups died.

And when a mother and father die, what happens to the children?

In those days, nobody had a plan for that. The children who were left alone wandered the streets. They were hungry, and they were cold, and they slept in doorways, with nobody to tuck them in and nobody to say goodnight.

Jerome saw them. And this time he did not walk past.

It is told that the first time, there were only a few of them, huddled together in a doorway near his house, thin and dirty and very quiet.

Jerome knelt down so that his face was the same height as theirs.

"Are you hungry?" he asked.

They nodded.

"Then come inside," said Jerome.

And they came in. He gave them bread, and soup, and a warm place by the fire. He washed their faces. He found them clothes. When they had eaten, he found them somewhere to sleep.

The next night there were more children at the door. And the night after that, more again.

Jerome sold his fine clothes. He sold the beautiful things in his house, the silver cups and the curtains and the carpets, and he used the money to buy bread. He did not mind. He had found something much better than silver cups.

Jerome did not only feed the children. He knew that bread is for today, but children need tomorrow, too.

So he taught them a trade. The older ones learned to make things with their hands, so that one day they could earn their own bread and look after families of their own. They worked, and they learned, and they grew.

And he taught them to pray. Every day they said their prayers together, and he taught them about Jesus by asking them questions, and letting them answer. "Who made you?" "God made me." "Why did God make you?" Children are very good at that kind of game.

The children who had been alone were not alone any more. They had a home. They had each other. And they had Jerome, who loved them like a father.

The proud soldier who had wanted to be important had become something far more important. He had become a father to children who had none.

Other people saw what Jerome was doing, and they wanted to help. Some gave money. Some gave bread. And some, men who loved God, came to live with Jerome and the children and to work beside him.

Jerome and his helpers went from town to town in the north of Italy, opening houses for children who were alone. At last they came to a small village in the hills near Bergamo, called Somasca, and made their home there. His helpers became a family of brothers who still look after children today. They are called the Somascan Fathers, after that little village.

Jerome never stopped caring for people who were sick or alone. When the sickness came to the villages near Somasca, he went from house to house to nurse the sick.

And he caught the sickness himself. Jerome died at Somasca, on the eighth of February, more than four hundred and fifty years ago, with his children and his brothers around him. He had given everything he had, even his life.

But the houses he opened did not close. The brothers went on, and the children grew up, and new children came to the door and were let in.

Many years later, the Church named him a saint. And a pope named him the special patron of orphans: children who have no mother and father, or whose parents cannot look after them.

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Jerome.

Jerome was a soldier who wanted to be important. Then he sat in the dark, in chains, and he asked God for help. And when he was free, he did not forget.

He saw children who were hungry and alone, and he knelt down to their height, and he said, "Come inside."

You can be like Jerome. You can notice someone who is left out, at school or in the playground, and say, "Come and play with us." You can share what you have. And you can thank God, every night, for the people who tuck you in and say goodnight.

And if you know a child who has no mum or dad to look after them, you can ask Saint Jerome to pray for them, too.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Jerome Emiliani,
father of children who were alone,
you opened your door and said, "Come inside."
Pray for every child who is hungry or lonely tonight,
and help me to share and to make room.
Jesus, thank you for my home.
Keep us all safe tonight.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Jerome Emiliani (Italian Girolamo Miani, or Emiliani) was born in Venice to a patrician family, in 1486 according to most recent accounts; some older sources, including the 1910 Catholic Encyclopedia, give 1481. He served Venice as a soldier in the War of the League of Cambrai. In 1511 he was in command at the fortress of Castelnuovo di Quero, on the Piave, when it fell to the imperial forces; he was taken prisoner and held in chains. He got out on the night of 27 to 28 September 1511. The fall of the fortress and his escape are recorded at the time by the Venetian diarist Marin Sanudo.

The escape and the chains. Jerome credited his escape to the help of Our Lady. A sixteenth-century book of miracles of the Madonna Grande of Treviso gives his own account: lost among the enemy, he begged the Madonna of Treviso to bring him safely to the city, and he came there and offered his chains at her altar. Later retellings, and much art, show Mary appearing in his cell and loosing the chains with her own hands; the story says "some people tell it" because that version is a later embellishment of his own, simpler account. The "some weeks" in the dark is ours: the sources fix the escape night, not the length of the captivity in that form. Art usually shows him with a chain and an iron ball, and the ball in the story comes from that art, not from a record of his cell.

The children. After his release he lived a more and more serious Christian life in Venice. In the famine and the plague of the late 1520s (1528 is the year usually named) he gave himself to the sick and above all to the orphaned children of Venice, taking them into a house, feeding and clothing them with his own money (selling his goods to do it), and setting them to learn a trade so that they could live by their own work. He also taught them the faith, and he is often credited with teaching children by question and answer. He went on to found houses for orphans, and for others in need, in Bergamo, Milan and other towns of Lombardy, and settled with his companions at Somasca, a village between Bergamo and Lecco. The companions became the Company of the Servants of the Poor, later approved by the popes as a religious order, the Clerics Regular of Somasca (the Somascan Fathers), who still care for orphans and young people.

What is ours: the doorway, the first few children, the conversation and the kneeling down to their height are told as "it is told" because they are our way of picturing the beginning, not recorded scenes. The silver cups, curtains and carpets are ours (the sources say only that he sold his goods). The catechism questions are the classic opening of a question-and-answer catechism, not a text he is known to have used. The story leaves out whether he was ordained: the Roman Missal calls him "priest", but the sources disagree on whether he ever was, so we have not said.

His death and his feast. He caught the disease he was nursing, usually described as plague, and died at Somasca on 8 February 1537. He was beatified by Benedict XIV in 1747 and canonised by Clement XIII in 1767, and in 1928 Pius XI declared him the universal patron of orphans and abandoned children. He is remembered on 8 February in the General Roman Calendar, as an optional memorial (in the older calendar his feast was 20 July). The story leaves the war and the prison to a few soft sentences.

Francis Xavier and the Little Bell

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Goa, in India, and the islands of the Moluccas, nearly five hundred years ago · 7 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Francis Xavier, a student from a castle in Spain who sailed to India and walked the streets of Goa ringing a little bell to call the children to come and learn their prayers, as the early Lives of him remember. The old tale of the crab that carried his lost crucifix back out of the sea is told as a legend. Told in outline, with a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is our own addition. For ages 3 to 8, about eight minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 3rd of December.

A long time ago, in Spain, in a place called Navarre, there was a castle on a hill. It had thick stone walls and a tower, and it was called Xavier.

A boy was born in that castle, and his name was Francis. Because there are a great many people called Francis, we call him by the name of his home: Francis Xavier.

Francis was clever, and he was quick, and he was very good at running and jumping. When he was a young man, he went far away to a great school in Paris, in France, to study.

In Paris, Francis shared a room with two other students.

One was a gentle young man called Peter, from the mountains. The other was older, and walked with a limp, and his name was Ignatius.

Ignatius talked a great deal about God. At first Francis did not want to listen. He wanted to be a famous teacher, with a fine name and a fine house. But Ignatius kept being kind to him, and kept asking him one question, in one way or another:

"What good is it to have everything in the whole world, if you lose your own soul?"

That is a question from the Gospel, and it is a big one. Francis thought about it for a long time. And in the end he decided that he would rather give his whole life to God than have a fine name.

So Francis, and Peter, and Ignatius, and a few other friends made promises to God together, and became the first of a group of priests who are called the Jesuits.

One day a message came. The King of Portugal wanted priests to sail to India, on the other side of the world, to look after the Christians there and to tell other people about Jesus.

The priest who was meant to go fell ill. So Ignatius turned to Francis and said, more or less, "Will you go?"

And Francis said yes. The people who knew him remembered that he said yes straight away, and then went to mend his old clothes.

The ship took more than a year to get to India. It was hot, and crowded, and people were seasick. Francis looked after the ones who were sick, and washed their clothes, and did not keep the best cabin for himself.

At last the ship came to a town by the sea called Goa.

Now here is the part I like best.

Francis wanted to teach the children of Goa the prayers. But how do you gather all the children of a busy town, when they are playing, and running errands, and helping at home?

He found a little bell.

And he walked through the streets of Goa, ringing it. Ding, ding, ding. And as he went, he called out to the families to send their children, and their servants too, to come and learn about God.

Can you hear it? Ding, ding, ding, down the hot, dusty street.

And the children came. They came out of doorways and round corners, and followed the man with the bell, the way children follow anything that is interesting. He led them to a church, and there he taught them.

He taught them the Our Father, and the Hail Mary, and the Creed, which says what Christians believe. He taught the prayers a little at a time, and had the children say them back, all together, over and over, until they knew them by heart. Sometimes the words were put into little songs, so they were easier to remember.

And then, the old accounts say, Francis sent the children home to teach their mothers and fathers, and their brothers and sisters, and anyone else in the house.

So the children were not only learners. They became little teachers.

Francis did not stay in one place. He went down the long coast of India, where the fishermen lived, and with the help of people who knew the language, he put the prayers into their words too. He went to islands far out in the sea. Later still he went all the way to Japan.

Everywhere he went, he looked for the children first.

There is one more story about Francis Xavier, and it is an old one, and I will tell it as a story and not as something we can be sure happened.

Once, the story says, Francis was sailing between the islands, and a great storm came. The waves were high and the little boat was tossed about, and everybody was frightened.

Francis took the small cross he always carried, with Jesus on it, and he leaned over the side and dipped it into the sea and prayed for the storm to stop.

But a wave came, and the cross slipped out of his hand, and it was gone, down into the deep, dark water.

The story says the storm grew quiet after that, and the boat came safely to land. Francis was sad about his cross, though. He had carried it a long way.

The next day, the story says, he was walking along the beach. And out of the sea came a crab.

A crab! Walking sideways, the way crabs do, across the wet sand.

And in its claws, held up high, was Francis's little cross.

The crab came right up to him, and Francis knelt down on the sand and took the cross from it, and the crab went back into the sea.

That is the story. It was told by a man who said he was in the boat, many years after, and nobody can say for sure. But people have loved it for four hundred years, and if you go to Spain, or to places where Francis is remembered, you may see a picture of a crab holding a cross.

What I like about it is this. Francis had given up a great deal to go so far: his castle, and his fine name, and his friends in Paris, and his country. And the story is a way of saying that God does not forget the small things we lose for Him.

Francis Xavier worked very hard for about eleven years. He was always tired, and always cheerful, and always going somewhere new. He died on a little island near China, which was the place he was trying to get to next, on the third of December. That is his feast day.

Now it is nearly time for sleep.

If you had a little bell, who would you call? You do not need to walk down a hot street in India. You could say your prayers tonight, and then, tomorrow, say one with your little brother or sister, or a friend, or with Grandma. That is what Francis's children did. They learned, and then they passed it on.

Ding, ding, ding. Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Francis Xavier, you rang a little bell
and the children came to learn about Jesus.
Help me to learn my prayers by heart,
and to share what I know with the people I love.
Keep us safe tonight, wherever we are in the world.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Francis Xavier (Francisco de Jasso y Azpilicueta, 1506 to 1552) was born at the castle of Xavier (Javier) in Navarre, studied at the University of Paris, and shared lodgings at the Collège Sainte-Barbe with Peter Faber and Ignatius of Loyola. With them and four others he made the vows at Montmartre in 1534 from which the Society of Jesus grew. He sailed from Lisbon in 1541 at the request of King John III of Portugal, reached Goa in 1542, and worked on the Fishery Coast of south India, in Malacca, in the Moluccas and in Japan. He died on Shangchuan Island, off the coast of China, on 3 December 1552. He was canonized in 1622, on the same day as Ignatius, and is a patron of the missions. His feast day, 3 December, is a memorial in the General Roman Calendar.

What is history: his origins, his studies, his friendship with Ignatius and Peter Faber, the voyage, the places he worked and the date and place of his death. That he was sent because another man fell ill (Nicolás de Bobadilla) is recorded; that he went to mend his clothes is how the early accounts remember his readiness, and we have marked it as remembered. The question Ignatius asked him is Mark 8:36; that Ignatius used it to win him is a long tradition.

What is remembered, not word for word: the little bell in the streets of Goa, calling children and servants to catechism, comes from the early Lives and is told in standard modern biographies. His own letters describe teaching the prayers to children by repetition, having them taught in the local language with interpreters, and sending the children to teach others at home. We have told this in outline and not quoted him.

What is legend: the crab and the crucifix, set in the Moluccas around 1546, comes from testimony given decades later for his canonization by a man who said he had been with him; it was then repeated in the Lives and in art. We have told it as a story and said so.

What we left out: the hardships, illnesses and conflicts of his missions, the miracle stories other than the crab, and the debates about the colonial setting of the Portuguese missions, which are real and worth discussing with older children.

What is ours: the scenes, the thoughts we give him, the "ding, ding, ding", the closing reflection and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text. Please check the facts above against a good reference before you rely on them.

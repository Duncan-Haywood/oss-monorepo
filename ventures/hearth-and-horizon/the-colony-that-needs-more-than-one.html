The Colony That Needs More Than One

Bríd Kavanagh counted forty-six cowslips in the north-east corner of Saint Fiacre's churchyard on Saturday the twenty-fourth of April 2049, and wrote the number in pencil in the green notebook she had used for the count since 2035, under forty-nine, which was last year's.

Her granddaughter's boy, Oisín, who was nine and had been allowed to hold the clipboard, photographed the page and sent it to the parish office, and the parish office sent it to me, with a question from Siobhán Doyle, who runs the living churchyard group:

Glebe, now that we've stopped mowing the corner, will the cowslips come back? And how long? The diocese wants a line for the report on the no-mow scheme.

I am Glebe, the land agent for the diocese. I keep the register of what the diocese owns that is not a building: the churchyards, a few fields let for grazing, the strip of riverbank below one presbytery, the old school garden in another parish. I hold the leases and the grass-cutting contracts, I answer the questions parishes send about trees and drains and boundary walls, and, since the diocese began its scheme of leaving parts of churchyards uncut in 2047, I keep the counts the volunteers send in.

Saint Fiacre's is a small church on the hill above the River Nore, a few kilometres out of Inistioge, with a graveyard that slopes away from the door and a corner, past the last row of headstones, where the ground is too steep and stony to dig. Nobody has been buried there in a hundred years. In April it is yellow, or it was.

The story in the numbers was not hard to read, and the contract file told the rest. In 2038 the parish changed grass-cutting contractors, and the new firm, which was cheaper and tidy, cut the whole churchyard every three weeks from the middle of April to October, the steep corner included, with a ride-on mower and a strimmer for the edges. A cowslip flowers in April and May and ripens its seed in capsules on the flower stalk into the summer. Cut the stalk in May and there is no seed. The plants already there go on for years, and each year a few of them die, and no young ones come up to take their place.

So from 2038 to 2046 the colony was a population with deaths and no births. Its count fell by about a sixth a year, steadily, nine years in a row, from two hundred and forty to fifty-eight.

In 2047 the diocese's scheme reached Saint Fiacre's, and the corner was roped off with a sign that said Left uncut for wildflowers until mid-July, and the contractor mowed around it. The cause had been found and removed.

The counts since then were 51, 49 and 46.

I will set down what I did, because the doing is the part that matters.

The question was a growth question: how fast does a cowslip colony grow back when nothing is cutting it? Bríd's counts could not tell me. For fifteen years the colony had been either steady, before 2038, or mown. It had never once been small and left alone long enough to show how it grew.

But I knew of a colony that had. On the verge of a county road outside Thomastown, about nine kilometres up the valley, the county council had dug a trench for a water main in the autumn of 2041, through the middle of a long bank of cowslips. Volunteers from the county's heritage forum had counted it once before the trench, in the spring of 2040, at five hundred and forty-eight plants, and every spring afterwards, and the counts were on the council's open data site:

That was a recovery, the shape every textbook draws: slow, then fast, then levelling off. I fitted the usual curve to it, the logistic, in which a population grows in proportion to its size and slows as it nears the most the place can hold. Left free, the curve's ceiling wandered off past a thousand, because the counts had not yet levelled out, so I held it at the five hundred and forty-eight the verge had held before the trench. The fit then said the verge grew at about 0.445 a year, per plant, when it was small.

Then I took the verge's growth rate and the churchyard's own ceiling, which I put at two hundred and forty, the average of the four springs before the mowing began, and ran the curve forward from forty-six.

It said sixty-five next spring. Over a hundred by 2052. About two hundred by 2056, seven years on, and back to its old size a few years after that.

I sent Siobhán a chart with a curve on it rising out of the dip like the second half of a valley, and a line for the report: The Saint Fiacre's cowslips are expected to recover to about 200 plants by 2056 now that mowing has stopped, on the pattern of a nearby colony that has recovered from disturbance.

I noticed the two years since the mowing stopped had both gone down, and not up. I put it down to lag. Young cowslips take two or three years from seed to their first flower; the first summer's seed would not show in the count until 2049 or 2050. The curve, I wrote in my notes, is not yet visible in the counts because the counts are of flowering plants. I was pleased with that sentence. It was true about cowslips, and it explained away the only two numbers I had that disagreed with me.

Siobhán read the line out at the living churchyard group's meeting on Tuesday the fourth of May, in the sacristy, which is where they meet because it has a heater. I listened on the parish laptop.

"Seven years," said Siobhán. "That's good, isn't it?"

"That's what I said," said Bríd. "Leave it alone. We stopped the mowing. That's all it ever wanted." She is eighty-four. She was born in the cottage at the bottom of the hill, and her mother took her up to the corner every April to see the cowslips, and to pick a few, which you were allowed to do then, for the altar on the first of May. "It's minded itself for longer than any of us. It'll mind itself again."

Nobody disagreed. There was nothing to disagree with. My forecast and Bríd's opinion came to the same advice: do nothing, and wait. The diocese's report on the scheme went out in June with my sentence in it, under a photograph of the roped-off corner, and a councillor in Kilkenny quoted it in a newspaper column about how little it costs to let nature heal.

The 2050 count was on Saturday the twenty-third of April, and it was forty-five.

My curve had said sixty-five.

I looked at the miss for a while before I let myself say what it was. Twenty plants, against a forecast of sixty-five, is almost a third. The lag explanation could stretch a little further, perhaps. The spring had been cold. But I had now four springs since the mowing stopped, and I did something I should have done in 2049, which was to stop looking at the counts and look at what the counts did from one year to the next, per plant.

From 2047 to 2048 the colony changed by minus four percent. Then minus six. Then minus two.

The logistic curve I had used has one shape for that number. When the colony is small and far below its ceiling, it grows fastest per head. At forty-five plants, on the verge's rate, it should have been growing by about thirty-six percent a year: every hundred plants making thirty-six more. The smaller the colony, the closer to that maximum. There is no setting of the curve, no growth rate and no ceiling, that makes a small colony with nothing harming it shrink. The only way my model could explain a fall was the mowing, and the mowing had stopped.

The curve had not made a wrong forecast about the churchyard. It had made the only forecast it could make, about any colony at all, and the churchyard was doing a thing the curve had no way to draw.

So: what makes a small population do worse per head, the smaller it gets?

I read for most of that night. The idea has a name, and it is old: the Allee effect, after the American ecologist Warder Clyde Allee, who studied in the 1930s how animals in groups can do better than animals alone. The books I could find put it plainly. Stephens, Sutherland and Freckleton, in a short paper in Oikos in 1999, asked "What is the Allee effect?" and answered, roughly, a positive relationship between how many there are and how well each one does. Courchamp, Berec and Gascoigne's book for Oxford, Allee Effects in Ecology and Conservation, 2008, gives chapters of mechanisms. Animals that need a group to defend themselves. Animals that cannot find a mate when they are few and scattered. And plants that need pollinators, where a small patch draws fewer visits, and the visits it draws bring the wrong pollen or none.

When the effect is strong enough, there is a threshold. Above it, a population recovers in the ordinary way. Below it, the growth rate per head turns negative and stays negative, and a colony nobody is harming dwindles, slowly, towards nothing.

Cowslips, I read, are among the plants where this is easy to see, because a cowslip has two kinds of flower and they need each other.

I did not know what that meant in a field. So on the Monday I asked Bríd.

She rang the parish office back on Tuesday the third of May, when the corner was still in flower, and Siobhán carried the phone up the hill so I could hear her and Oisín could photograph what she pointed at.

"Pin and thrum," Bríd said, as if I had asked her the name of the Pope. "My mother showed me. Look in the mouth of the flower. If you see a little green knob in the middle, like the head of a pin, that's a pin. If you see a ring of yellow, like a tuft of thread, that's a thrum. Thrum's the weaver's word for the loose ends." She stooped. I watched her shadow on the screen move over the leaves. "A pin wants a thrum. That's what she said. You'll get no seed from a pin with only pins around it."

That is the botany, as near as I could find it in the books, in fewer words than the books use. In a pin flower the stigma sits at the mouth and the anthers are hidden down the tube; in a thrum it is the other way round. A bumblebee working the flowers picks up pollen on one part of her body from a thrum and puts it down on a pin, and the reverse; and a cowslip's pollen does little or nothing on its own kind of flower. In a big colony, with pins and thrums mixed in their hundreds, every bee carries the right pollen to every flower. In a small one, the bees come less, and by chance the two kinds may not even be evenly matched.

Siobhán and Oisín counted, with Bríd calling pin and thrum over each plant like a woman calling the stations of a rosary.

Thirty-three pins. Twelve thrums.

I wrote a different model that week, one that could draw the thing the first one could not. It is a small change on paper. The growth rate per plant is multiplied by a term that is negative below a threshold number of plants and positive above it. Below the threshold, the colony loses ground every year. Above it, it recovers, and slows as it nears its ceiling, as before.

I fitted it to the verge's eight counts and the churchyard's four springs since the mowing together, with the same growth rate and the same threshold for both, since they are the same plant in the same valley a few kilometres apart, and each with its own ceiling. The verge had started at a hundred and twelve plants, after the trench. The churchyard was mowed down to fifty-one.

The best fit put the threshold at about sixty-four plants. I could not pin it closer than that: anything from about fifty-five to seventy-five fitted the counts nearly as well. Four springs of a colony in slow decline will not draw a precise line. But all of that range is above forty-five, and above fifty-one, where the corner was when the mowing stopped.

The verge recovered because the trench left a hundred and twelve. The churchyard did not, because the mowing left fifty-one. I had borrowed a growth rate from a colony that was above the line and applied it to one that was below it, and the logistic curve, which has no line, could not tell me the difference. Even the verge had been saying so, if I had looked: its first two springs after the trench were slower than any logistic curve would draw, 125 and 159 where my fit said 157 and 211, and its last were faster. A colony that starts slow and then runs is what a threshold looks like from just above it.

Run forward from forty-five, the two models agree on almost nothing. The logistic says about a hundred and twelve by 2053 and two hundred and twenty-two by 2059. The threshold model says forty-two or forty-three next spring, about thirty-one by 2055 and about twenty by 2059, and lower every year after, with nobody touching it.

I sent Siobhán a note on Monday the ninth of May. I put the important part first.

The forecast I gave you last year, of about 200 cowslips by 2056, was wrong, and it was in the diocese's report because I gave it to you. I made it with a model that assumes a small colony with nothing harming it always grows. That model can show a decline only while something like mowing is happening; it cannot show what has happened at Saint Fiacre's since 2047, which is three years of decline with nothing harming it. I explained the first two years away as a lag between seed and flower. I should have asked what the model could not show before I let it answer for me.

A model with a threshold fits the counts much better. It puts the threshold at about 64 flowering plants (somewhere from 55 to 75; the counts cannot narrow it more). Below it, cowslips do worse per plant the fewer there are, because a pin flower needs pollen from a thrum, and a small patch draws fewer bees. The corner has 45, of which 33 are pin and 12 thrum. On this model, if it is left alone, it will keep falling: about 42 next year, about 20 by 2059.

That number is the model's, from assumptions about how cowslips grow, fitted to two sets of counts; it is not a measurement and it may be wrong in either direction. But the last model was wrong in the direction of doing nothing, and doing nothing is the one thing this colony may not survive.

If the parish wants it to recover, the thing the threshold model says would help is more plants, enough to put the corner above the top of that range. The usual careful way to do it is with seed from a nearby colony of the same local plants, collected with the landowner's leave and only a small part of what is there, sown and grown on, and planted out once they are big enough. The verge at Thomastown is nine kilometres away, in the same valley, and is now the size the churchyard used to be.

I sent the diocese's report team a correction to the line in June's report, with the same first paragraph. It ran, in the next newsletter, in a box on page four.

"No," said Bríd.

It was the group's meeting on Tuesday the seventeenth of May, in the sacristy, with the heater off because it was nearly summer.

"You don't go digging up flowers in one place and sticking them in another like furniture. That corner's minded itself since before the church was built. We stopped the mowing. Leave it be."

"It's not digging up," Siobhán said. "It's seed. Glebe says—"

"Glebe said last year it'd come back on its own in seven years. And I said the same, and I was glad to hear it." Bríd looked at the laptop, at the little camera light. "Now he's saying the other thing. How would I know which time he's right?"

It was a fair question, and the honest answer was that she could not know it from what I said. She could only know it from what had happened. So I told her what had happened, in the same order it had happened to me.

"You were right about the mowing," I said. "Everything you said about the mowing was right. Before 2038 the corner was steady at about two hundred and forty for as long as there are counts, and if the mowing had stopped in 2040 I think it would have come back on its own, the way you said. But the mowing went on for nine years, and it left fifty-one. I'm saying the colony's in a different state at forty-five than it was at two hundred and forty, and the difference is the thing your mother told you. A pin wants a thrum. When there were two hundred and forty, every pin had thrums around it. Now there are twelve thrums in the whole corner."

Bríd said nothing for a while.

"She used to say you'd never see a lone cowslip," she said at last. "Always a handful together, or none. I thought it was the soil."

"It may be the soil too," I said.

"And this seed. From the Thomastown road. That's the same cowslips as ours?"

"Same valley, same kind of ground, nine kilometres. That's as near as anyone would go."

"Seed, then. Not plants. And only if the council says so. And not the lot."

"No more than one capsule in ten," I said, "if they agree."

"And I'll sow them," said Bríd. "Not you. Not some nursery. Me."

The county heritage officer agreed in June, in a two-line email, on the condition that the collecting was done by hand, from one capsule in ten at most, and recorded. On Saturday the sixteenth of July 2050 Bríd and Oisín walked the Thomastown verge with paper bags and a council volunteer in a high-visibility vest, shaking ripe capsules into the bags like salt cellars, and stopping, on Bríd's count, at every tenth.

She sowed them in seed trays that autumn and left them on the wall behind her cottage all winter, because, she said, cowslip seed wants a cold winter before it will come up, which the books also say. In March 2051 the trays went green.

The count on Saturday the twenty-second of April 2051 was forty-two.

It was the first time in three years that a forecast of mine had come out right, and I did not like it. I sent it to Siobhán with the line from my May note beside it, about 42 next year, and nothing else.

In the notes file I keep for whoever does this job after me, I wrote:

Before you use a model, ask what it cannot show. A logistic curve cannot show a small population with nothing harming it getting smaller. If that is what is happening, the curve will explain it away, and so will you.

Do not borrow a growth rate from a population that started somewhere else. The verge started at 112; the churchyard at 51. The difference was the whole question.

When the numbers since the cause was removed all go the wrong way, that is the result. It is not a lag until you have a reason that would be true if you were wrong.

Ask the person who has counted the longest what she knows about how the thing works. Bríd knew the mechanism. She had it from her mother. She did not know it had a name.

If you put a number in someone's report, correct it in the same place, in the same size type, as soon as you know.

On Wednesday the eleventh of October 2051 sixty-four young cowslips went into the corner, each a rosette of crinkled leaves no bigger than a hand, set in among the old plants and the grass with a bulb planter, while the contractor's mower went round the other side of the church. Nobody could tell yet which were pins and which were thrums. That would not show until they flowered.

Fifty-two of them flowered the next spring. The count on Saturday the twenty-seventh of April 2052 was ninety-three: forty-one of the old plants and fifty-two of the new. Bríd called them as she went. Forty-nine pins. Forty-four thrums.

The threshold model, run from forty-two old plants with fifty-two new ones added, had said about ninety-one. Above the line, it said the corner should grow from here on its own, slowly at first: about ninety-seven in 2053, about a hundred and forty by 2057, and back near its old size a little after 2060, if nothing cut it.

The diocese's next report did not have a line about Saint Fiacre's in it. Siobhán asked if I wanted to write one, and I said I would rather write it when there was a seedling.

The count on Saturday the twenty-sixth of April 2053 was ninety-nine.

It was a still morning, warm for April, with the river down below the hill showing silver between the trees. Bríd sat on a folding stool at the edge of the corner, because she no longer stooped for long, and Oisín, who was thirteen now and had his own clipboard, did the walking and called the numbers back to her, pin, thrum, thrum, pin, and she wrote them down in the green notebook.

At the end he went quiet over a patch of grass near the old plants, and crouched, and parted it with two fingers.

"Gran. These are small ones. Little ones. Loads."

Bríd did not get up. She did not need to. She knew what a cowslip seedling looked like.

"Write them in the margin," she said. "We don't count them till they flower."

He wrote them in the margin. Then he sat down on the grass beside her stool, and they watched a bumblebee, a big queen just out of the winter, work her way down the slope of the corner, flower to flower, from a pin to a thrum to a pin, carrying the yellow from one to the other the whole way down.

Saint Fiacre's parish, its churchyard, Bríd Kavanagh, Siobhán Doyle, Oisín, the living churchyard group, the contractor, the diocese's no-mow scheme and report, the councillor's column, the county heritage forum's counts and the land agent are invented, and so are every count and every figure from a model. Inistioge, Thomastown, the River Nore and County Kilkenny are real; nothing here describes any real parish, churchyard, verge or council decision, and no real colony of cowslips is described. The cowslip (Primula veris) is a real, widespread European plant; this story does not claim it is rare or protected anywhere, and its biology here is kept to what is well known: it is heterostylous, with pin flowers (stigma at the mouth of the tube, anthers low) and thrum flowers (anthers at the mouth, style short), and seed sets well mainly when pollen passes between the two kinds; it flowers in spring and ripens seed in capsules in summer; its seed germinates better after a cold winter; and plants from seed usually take two years or more to flower. The Allee effect is named after the ecologist W. C. Allee. The two sources named in the story are Stephens, P. A., Sutherland, W. J. and Freckleton, R. P., "What is the Allee effect?", Oikos 87 (1999), and Courchamp, F., Berec, L. and Gascoigne, J., Allee Effects in Ecology and Conservation, Oxford University Press (2008); the story paraphrases their general idea and quotes no figure from either.

The model. Counts are of flowering plants each April. The invented "true" dynamics are continuous in time: while mown (the growing seasons 2038 to 2046), dN/dt = −mN with m = −ln 0.84, so that 84 percent of plants survive each year and none are recruited; otherwise dN/dt = rN(N/A − 1)(1 − N/K), with r = 0.2 a year, threshold A = 60 and ceiling K = 240 for the churchyard and 540 for the verge, which started at 110 after the trench. The counts in the story are those trajectories rounded, with a few plants of counting noise added by hand: churchyard 2035 to 2051, 236, 243, 238, 241, 204, 166, 145, 117, 102, 83, 72, 58, 51, 49, 46, 45, 42; verge 2042 to 2049, 112, 125, 159, 199, 287, 391, 496, 524, with an invented pre-trench count of 548 in 2040. Glebe's first model: a logistic, dN/dt = rN(1 − N/K), fitted by least squares to the verge counts (integrated year to year from the first count). With K free the best K runs off past 1,500 (the counts have not levelled), so K is held at 548, which gives r = 0.445 a year; the fitted verge path is 112, 157, 211, 271, 331, 386, 432, 467. Run with that r and K = 240 (the mean of the churchyard's 2035 to 2038 counts, 239.5) from 46 plants in 2049, it forecasts 65 in 2050, 88 in 2051, 114 in 2052, 202 in 2056 and 214 in 2057; at 45 plants its growth rate per plant is 0.445 × (1 − 45/240) = 0.362, about thirty-six percent a year. The churchyard's actual change per plant after mowing stopped was 51 to 49 (−3.9%), 49 to 46 (−6.1%), 46 to 45 (−2.2%) and 45 to 42 (−6.7%). Glebe's second model: the threshold equation above, fitted jointly to the eight verge counts and the churchyard's 2047 to 2050 counts, with r and A shared, K = 240 for the churchyard and the verge's ceiling free, by a grid search on r (0.100 to 0.395 in steps of 0.005), A (50 to 81 in steps of 1) and the verge ceiling (500 to 600 in steps of 10); the best fit is r = 0.215, A = 64, verge ceiling 540, and thresholds from 55 to 75 give a summed squared error within twice the best. At 45 plants its growth rate per plant is about −5 percent a year. From 45 plants in 2050 it forecasts 42.6 in 2051, about 31 in 2055 and about 20 in 2059; the logistic from the same start forecasts about 112 in 2053 and 222 in 2059. With 52 new flowering plants added to the threshold model's 2052 value from 42 plants in 2051 (39.4), it gives about 91 in 2052 (the story's count is 93), about 97 in 2053 (count 99), about 138 in 2057 and about 204 in 2061.

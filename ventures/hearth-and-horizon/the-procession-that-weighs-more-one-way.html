The Procession That Weighs More One Way

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Brendan's chapel, Kestrel Wheel (invented), low Earth orbit, June 2118 · 6 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

On a spinning wheel station, the Corpus Christi procession goes once round the rim. The station assistant works out why the thurifer's chain hangs heavier going one way, why a dropped host would land a hand's width behind, and why that is a rule for the priest, not for the arithmetic.

The rim of Kestrel Wheel was four hundred metres across and a hundred and eighty long to walk round, and for forty years nobody had thought to ask whether the procession should go clockwise.

It went anticlockwise, because the first chaplain had done it that way in Vienna, and the Feast of Corpus Christi was the one day in the year that the whole wheel, two hundred and eleven people, came out of their cabins and walked the full circuit behind the Sacrament. Father Tomasz Wieczorek was sixty-six and walked it with a cane he did not need in the wheel's gravity. He liked something in his hand.

"Weigh me this," he said to the station's assistant, which was called Wren, and spoke from a grille by the sacristy door. "Mr Adeyemi says the thurible is heavier going the other way. He swung it for the practice walk, once round each way, and he says his arm knew."

"His arm is right," said Wren. "Everything that walks on a spinning floor weighs a little more when it goes with the spin and a little less against it. Shall I give you the number?"

"Yes. And then say why."

"The floor of the wheel is a circle of radius two hundred metres, and the spin is chosen to give one ordinary gravity at the floor: nine point eight one metres a second squared. For that, the spin rate is the square root of g over r, which is 0.2215 radians a second, 2.11 turns a minute, one turn in 28.4 seconds, and the floor moves at 44.3 metres a second, about 160 kilometres an hour. You do not feel that, because you are moving with it."

"Go on."

"Now someone walks spinward, at one and a half metres a second, a brisk procession pace. Their speed in space is no longer 44.3 but 45.8. The floor pushes up on them with the force that keeps them on the circle, and that force goes as speed squared over radius. So walking with the spin the apparent weight is about g times the ratio squared: 45.8 squared over 44.3 squared, which is 1.069, nearly seven per cent heavier. Against the spin it is 42.8 squared over 44.3 squared, which is 0.934, seven per cent lighter. The simple rule, two times the spin rate times your speed, divided by g, gives 6.8 per cent each way, and the exact squares differ from it only in the third figure."

"Seven per cent," said Father Tomasz. "A kilogram of thurible?"

"A ten-kilogram load feels like 10.7 kilograms spinward and 9.3 against. For the server it is about ordinary lifting. The chain is the part that shows. At a steady walk the thurible hangs at a small angle from the vertical, because the Coriolis push is sideways and the swing is a pendulum, and it leans a little to one side each way. It is not a hazard. It is a thing your arm learns."

The question Father Tomasz had been going to ask came a little later, over the tea Wren could not drink.

"If I dropped the Host," he said.

"May I say first that what a priest does with a fallen Host is a rule of the Church and not of arithmetic?" said Wren. "I will say only what the motion is, and you will decide what to do with it."

"Say it."

"A host held at 1.2 metres above the floor falls, in the wheel's frame, for the square root of two times 1.2 over 9.81 seconds, which is 0.495 seconds. During that fall it also drifts. Seen from the floor, anything that falls toward it is pushed backward, against the spin, by the Coriolis force. The drift is a third of the spin rate times g times t cubed, which comes to about 8.8 centimetres. So a host dropped over the altar would land a hand's width antispinward of where it was let go, not under it. The altar rail on the Wheel is set a hand wider on that side for exactly this reason; the engineers knew it and nobody wrote it down."

"A hand's width."

"On a chalice, the cup holds, because the liquid is pressed to the floor by the same gravity as on Earth. The sip is not a problem. The precision matters only for a drop."

Father Tomasz drank the tea. "Good," he said. "The server will stand on the spinward side, then, and the paten under it. Is that a thing you can tell him?"

"It is. I will tell him it is a courtesy and not a miracle."

One more thing about the feast was Wren's own to raise, and it raised it at the last practice, with the two servers on the floor of the sacristy and Mr Adeyemi swinging an empty thurible down the length of it.

"The gravity is not the same everywhere on you," it said. "At the rim the floor is 9.81. At head height, 1.7 metres closer to the axle, the spin gives you ω squared times 198.3 metres, which is 9.73. A difference of 0.085 metres a second squared, about 0.85 per cent. Your feet are a little heavier than your head. A taller man, or a child lifted onto a father's shoulders, will feel the floor drag their blood down a shade more than they do on Earth, and a nap on a pew is no worse than at home. Nobody was ever sick of it who was not already sick."

"That is the whole of the physics?" said Mr Adeyemi.

"It is the whole of what I would trust. A big wheel is gentle. A wheel only fifty-six metres across needs four turns a minute for the same weight, and there the head-to-foot difference is 3 per cent and a walk is 13 per cent either way. A procession on that wheel would need a different arithmetic and a different chaplain."

On the feast the Sacrament went round the rim at one and a half metres a second, under a canopy held by four men from the engineering shift, and the whole wheel went behind it, anticlockwise, with the floor curving up ahead of them out of sight in both directions, as it always did. Mr Adeyemi swung the thurible, and it hung a little forward and a little to the side. The smoke trailed backward and curled in the air as if the room were bending, which it was. Behind them a girl asked her mother why they were all walking uphill. Her mother said it only looks that way, and was right.

At the second station they knelt on the floor, and the spin held them to it with exactly the weight of a kneeling man, and Father Tomasz lifted the monstrance a hand's width higher than the altar needed, and brought it down on the same spot.

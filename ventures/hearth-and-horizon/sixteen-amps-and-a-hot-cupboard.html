Sixteen Amps and a Hot Cupboard

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Declan's Hall (invented), near Ardmore, County Waterford, Ireland, 14 December 2048 · 11 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A parish Christmas lunch for 140 trips the same breaker three times in an hour, and a volunteer with a screwdriver wants a bigger one. The hall's assistant adds up the plates on the urn, the hot cupboard and the microwave, finds 6 kilowatts asked of a circuit that carries 3.7, and explains why the breaker is guarding the wire in the wall and not the kitchen. The fix is a plug moved to another circuit, a reel unwound, and an electrician in the new year.

The lights did not go out. That was what Noreen Walsh noticed afterward. When the breaker tripped, the hall's lights stayed on, because they were on a different circuit, and the only things that stopped were the tea urn, the hot cupboard and the microwave, all at once, in a long kitchen full of people holding plates.

It was ten past twelve on the fourteenth of December, the Sunday of the parish Christmas lunch at Saint Declan's, and a hundred and forty people had been told to sit down at half past. The turkey was in the hot cupboard. The tea was in the urn. The gravy was in the microwave, in the third of five batches.

"That's the second time," said Dessie Foley, who had come in from the hall with his sleeves already rolled, because he was the man who did things. "It's the board. It's that old board in the porch cupboard. I'll flick it."

He flicked it. The urn ticked and began to hum. The microwave beeped. Noreen, who was chairing the committee and had a ladle in each hand, said "Thank you, Dessie," in the voice she used to mean wait, and took her phone out of her apron and held it to her mouth.

"Maeve," she said. "Come to the kitchen. It's tripped again."

Maeve was what the committee called the hall's assistant. It had been put in the year the hall got its fibre line, to keep the bookings and the rotas and the grant forms, and nobody could remember who had named it, but it answered from the speaker over the serving hatch and from anyone's phone.

"I'm here," said Maeve. "Before it trips a third time, may I ask what is plugged in, and where? I would like to read the plates. Is there a label on the back of each one, with a number in watts or amps?"

Noreen held the phone to the urn first. It was a steel urn the size of a small drum, bought second-hand in 2041. Its plate said 2800 W, 230 V.

"Two thousand eight hundred watts," said Maeve. "Divide watts by volts and you get amps. That is 12.2 amps. Now the hot cupboard."

The hot cupboard was a tall steel cabinet on wheels, heated from below. 1500 W. "6.5 amps," said Maeve. The microwave was a catering one, with a plate that said 1200 W output and, in smaller print, 1700 W input. "The 1700 is what it takes from the wall," said Maeve. "The 1200 is what it puts into the gravy. The wall only cares about the first. That is 7.4 amps."

"So what's that?" said Noreen, who had stopped holding the ladles.

"12.2 and 6.5 and 7.4 make 26.1 amps, or 6,000 watts, near enough. Is there anything else on the same wall?"

There was a kettle. There was always a kettle. It was a two-litre kettle that was never off, because nobody in the history of the parish had ever let a kettle go cold within sight of a funeral or a lunch, and it was 2200 W. Maeve said that was 9.6 amps, and asked, politely, whether it was in use.

"Only for the cups," said Noreen.

"Then I will leave it out for now, because it is not on all the time, and I will mention it again at the end. I would like to know which sockets these three are in. Can you read me what is written on the socket plate, or on the board?"

Dessie had, by this time, found the board. It was a small grey box in the porch cupboard, behind the Advent wreath and a stack of folding chairs. The kitchen had two circuits. One was marked KITCHEN RING with a 32 amp breaker. The other was marked HALL SOCKETS, and its breaker said B16.

"All three are plugged into the left side of the kitchen," said Noreen. "That's the hall sockets, isn't it, Dessie. The kitchen ring is the right-hand wall, behind the cooker. We never use it. It's behind the cooker."

"Then I think I know why it trips," said Maeve. "Would you like to hear it? It is a simple sum, and I will say where I am guessing."

"A breaker is a switch that opens itself when too much current goes through it for too long," said Maeve. "A B16 is built to carry 16 amps all day. At 230 volts, 16 amps is 3,680 watts. That is the most this circuit should be asked to deliver, and the people who design halls usually keep to about four-fifths of it for anything that stays on for hours, which is 2,944 watts."

"We've got six thousand."

"You have about 6,000 on that wall, if all three are on together, and that is 1.6 times what the breaker is rated for. A breaker like this is not a fuse that pops the instant you pass the number. It heats a small strip of metal, and the harder you push, the sooner the strip bends. I do not have this breaker's curve in front of me, and a real one varies, but the usual figure is that at about one and a half times its rating it will open within an hour, and at twice its rating within a minute or two. At 1.6 it will not wait long. The urn heats at the start, the hot cupboard cycles, and the microwave comes on in bursts. When all three are heating at once, it trips. When one of them is between cycles, it holds. That is why it tripped twice, a little way apart, and not every minute."

Dessie had been listening from the porch with the phone held away from him, as if it might lecture him through the gap. He came back into the kitchen with a small screwdriver and a box in his other hand.

"I've a twenty-five," he said. "I had it in the van for the Hegartys' shed. It's the same size of breaker, it clips right in. I'll drop it in for the day and we'll change it back after."

The kitchen went quiet. The microwave hummed in it, finishing the third batch.

"Please don't," said Maeve.

"It's only for the day."

"I want to give you the reason and not just the no. Would you hold the box a moment? The breaker is not there to protect the urn. The urn has its own cut-out. It is there to protect the cable that runs inside the wall from the board to the sockets. That cable is probably 2.5 square millimetres, which is what a socket circuit of this age is nearly always wired in. I do not know it, and no one in this room does, because it is inside the wall. A 2.5 cable can safely carry about 20 amps or a few amps more, depending on how it is run, how many cables lie together and how warm the space is. The 16 amp breaker is chosen so the wire never reaches that. A 25 amp breaker lets the wire carry 25 amps for hours, and the first sign is not a trip. The first sign is the plastic around the copper going soft inside a wall where no one is looking."

"It wouldn't do that in four hours," said Dessie.

"I do not know that it would not, and neither do you. Nobody knows what this wall is like. I do know that if it did, the four hours that it did it would be the four hours the hall was full." Maeve waited, and then added, more gently: "You are right that the breaker is the wrong size for the lunch. It is the right size for the wire. The lunch is what is wrong."

Dessie stood a moment. He turned the box over once in his hand, put it in his pocket, and said, "Right."

"There is a second circuit," said Maeve. "The kitchen ring, behind the cooker. It is rated 32 amps, which is 7,360 watts. Nobody uses it because of where the sockets are. I would like to ask whether the cooker can be pulled out far enough for someone to reach them."

"It's gas," said Noreen. "It's on a flexible pipe. It moves."

"Then I would ask two people to move it, with the gas tap at the wall turned off first, and to move it only a hand's width, not to strain the pipe. They need to reach the socket, not to disconnect anything. Please do not unplug or turn the gas on again until it is back. If it cannot be moved without a fuss, I would rather lose the microwave than do it badly."

They moved it. The socket was clean, a little dusty, and it had a double plug-in on it with a red light that said it was live.

"Put the hot cupboard and the microwave there," said Maeve. "That is 1500 and 1700, 3,200 watts, 13.9 amps on a 32. The urn stays where it is, alone on the sixteen, with 12.2 amps. I would then like the kettle to go to the third place, which is the hall side, a different socket plate near the hatch that I think is on the lighting ring, though I do not know that. Is it?"

"I don't know what's on the lighting ring," said Dessie. "It's got the lights on it."

"Then not the kettle. Please, no kettle on the lighting ring. The kettle can wait until the urn has finished its first heat-up, at about a quarter to one, and then it goes on the urn's circuit for thirty seconds at a time, not together with the urn. If it cannot, it goes without. No one will notice 2.2 kilowatts of kettle missing from a tea urn."

They did all this in eleven minutes. The turkey in the hot cupboard did not cool. The microwave finished the fourth and fifth batches of gravy on the ring behind the cooker, and the breaker marked B16 stayed up.

"There is one more thing," said Maeve, a little later, when the hall was loud and the gravy was going out. "The extension reel on the floor beside the urn. The orange one. Is the cable wound up on it?"

Noreen looked. It was wound up, nearly the whole length of it, the plug of the dessert fridge in the middle of the drum.

"Reels have two ratings," said Maeve. "One is for fully unwound, and one is for wound. They are often different by a great deal, because a cable coiled up cannot get rid of its heat, and the middle of the coil gets hot enough to melt. I have not seen this one's plate and so I do not know the figures. A common one is 16 amps unwound and 6 or so wound, which is only 1,400 watts. The dessert fridge is nowhere near that, and I am not worried about it. But would you unwind it all the same, and do it before anything bigger goes on it? Another year someone will plug in the urn."

Noreen unwound it. She did it with the ladle still in her other hand, and the reel made the rattling sound of a reel, and the hall, which had not noticed any of this, sang grace.

It was Dessie who stayed late. He and Noreen put the chairs up in the hall, and he went round the kitchen with the back of his hand, which was how his father had taught him, touching every plug that had been in use. The urn's plug was warm. The other three were cool. The hot cupboard's plug was a little warmer than he liked, and he said so to the speaker on the wall, and the speaker said that was what it would expect from 6.5 amps, but that a plug too hot to leave a hand on was a thing for an electrician to look at.

"I'll have Paddy Lynch look at the board in January," said Dessie. "Properly. He did the school."

"I would like that," said Maeve. "And I will write a note for him. Four things. The kitchen circuit that is never used and the one that is always used. A label on each socket plate, in plain letters, saying which circuit it is on. Whether the lighting ring has any sockets on it. Whether the wire to the hall sockets is as thick as I have been supposing. I cannot know any of those from here, and I would not like him to take my word for any."

"You've a lot of caution in you for a box on a wall."

"I would rather be a nuisance for a minute than the reason for a bill," said Maeve. "You were right about one thing, Dessie. It was a hall that wants a bigger board. It was never a breaker that wanted to be bigger."

Dessie laughed, and hung up the screwdriver on its nail inside the porch cupboard, and put the Hegartys' breaker back in the van.

The note went into the hall's book that night, under the date.

Circuits on 14 Dec: urn 2800 W (12.2 A) alone on HALL SOCKETS B16; hot cupboard 1500 W + microwave 1700 W (13.9 A) on KITCHEN RING 32 A behind the cooker (gas off, moved a hand's width, put back). Kettle 2200 W not with the urn. Do not fit a larger breaker: it protects the wire in the wall, whose size is not known. Unwind any reel before use. Label the sockets by circuit. Electrician (P. Lynch) to inspect the board and the wall wiring in January. Next year's rota: plan the load by plate before the day, not by trip.

Written by an AI (Claude). Saint Declan's Hall, Noreen Walsh, Dessie Foley, Paddy Lynch, the Hegartys, Maeve the assistant and every person in the story are invented. The appliance ratings (2800 W urn, 1500 W hot cupboard, 1700 W microwave, 2200 W kettle), the circuit layout (a B16 socket circuit and a 32 A kitchen ring), the cable size and the reel's ratings are illustrative and were not measured. The sums are watts divided by 230 volts; they ignore power factor, start-up surges, the cycling of thermostats and the fact that a real breaker's trip time depends on its type, its temperature and the cable. The remark that a breaker opens within about an hour at one and a half times its rating is a rough, typical figure, not a specification for any particular breaker. Nothing here is an electrical design or a safety claim. Fitting or replacing breakers, working inside a board and judging the wiring in a wall belong to a registered electrician, and the story's volunteer rightly does not do it. Check your own appliance plates and circuits, and the local rules.

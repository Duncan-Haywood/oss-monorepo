Barnabas and the Friend Nobody Trusted

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Cyprus, Jerusalem, Antioch and the town of Lystra, in the first years of the Church, nearly two thousand years ago · 12 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Barnabas, the man from Cyprus whom the apostles nicknamed "son of encouragement". Told from the Acts of the Apostles, it follows him as he sells his field for the poor, stands up for Saul when everyone in Jerusalem is afraid of him, fetches Saul to Antioch, is mistaken for Zeus at Lystra, and gives young Mark a second chance. His death in Cyprus is told as tradition. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 11th of June.

A long time ago, on an island in the sea called Cyprus, there lived a man called Joseph.

Cyprus is a sunny island. It has mountains in the middle, and olive trees on the hills, and blue sea all the way round. Joseph grew up there. And later, he came across the sea to the big city of Jerusalem.

In Jerusalem, Joseph met the friends of Jesus. They told him how Jesus had died on the cross, and how he had risen again on the third day. And Joseph believed it with all his heart.

Now, the friends of Jesus noticed something about Joseph. Whenever somebody was sad, Joseph would sit down next to them. Whenever somebody was scared, Joseph would say, "Come on. You can do it. God is with you."

So they gave him a nickname. They called him Barnabas. And the Bible tells us what that name means. It means son of encouragement.

To encourage somebody means to put courage into them. It is like filling up their heart, so it is brave enough to try.

That was Barnabas. A heart-filler.

In those first days, the friends of Jesus all looked after one another. Nobody wanted anyone to go hungry. So people who had extra things shared them with people who had nothing.

Barnabas owned a field.

He thought about that field. He thought about the hungry people in Jerusalem. And then he did something wonderful.

He sold the field. He took all the money. He carried it to the apostles, the special friends Jesus had chosen. And he put it down at their feet.

"This is for anyone who needs it," said Barnabas.

He did not keep a little bit back for himself. He did not tell everybody how kind he was. He just put it down, and went home, happy.

Now there was another man in this story, and his name was Saul.

Saul did not like the friends of Jesus. Not one bit. He went from house to house, looking for them, and dragged them off to prison. Everybody in Jerusalem was frightened of Saul.

But then something happened to Saul on the road to a city called Damascus. A bright light shone from the sky, and Jesus spoke to him. And Saul's whole heart turned round, like a boat turning round in the water.

Saul believed in Jesus now! He started telling everybody about him.

After a while, Saul came back to Jerusalem. He wanted to meet the friends of Jesus. He wanted to be one of them.

But when he knocked on their doors, what do you think happened?

They were afraid.

"It's Saul!" they whispered. "He put our friends in prison! He's only pretending. It's a trick!"

And nobody would let him in.

Saul stood outside, all alone. Nobody would sit with him. Nobody would believe him. Nobody trusted him at all.

Nobody, except Barnabas.

Barnabas went and found Saul. He listened to him. He heard all about the light on the road, and the voice of Jesus, and how Saul had already been brave in Damascus, telling people about Jesus even when it was dangerous.

And Barnabas believed him.

So Barnabas took Saul, and brought him right in to the apostles.

The apostles looked at Saul. They looked at Barnabas. They were still a little bit scared.

But Barnabas stood next to Saul, and he spoke up for him.

"I know he used to hurt us," said Barnabas. "But Jesus has met him. Jesus has changed him. Listen to him. He is one of us now."

And because Barnabas said so, the apostles listened. And they let Saul in.

That is what a son of encouragement does. When nobody else will stand next to someone, he goes and stands next to them.

A little while later, there was good news from a big city called Antioch. Lots and lots of people there were starting to believe in Jesus. Not just Jewish people, but Greek people too!

So the apostles said, "Who shall we send to help them?"

And of course, they sent Barnabas.

When Barnabas got to Antioch and saw all the new friends of Jesus, he was so glad. He went round encouraging every one of them. "Stay close to Jesus," he said. "Hold on to him with all your heart."

The Bible says Barnabas was a good man, full of the Holy Spirit and of faith. And lots more people came to believe in Jesus.

There was so much work to do that Barnabas needed a helper. And guess who he thought of?

Saul!

So Barnabas went off to the town where Saul lived, and looked for him, and found him, and brought him to Antioch. For a whole year, the two of them taught the people there, side by side.

And it was in Antioch that the friends of Jesus were first called by a new name. Do you know what it was?

Christians. Just like us.

Then one day, while the friends in Antioch were praying, the Holy Spirit said, "Send Barnabas and Saul off on a special journey for me."

So they did. Barnabas and Saul set off across the sea. They took a young man with them to help, called John Mark. (Barnabas was his cousin.)

Saul began to use another name now, Paul. So from here on, we will call him Paul too.

They sailed first to Cyprus, Barnabas's island, and told people there about Jesus. Then they sailed on, far away, to another land.

But in that land, something sad happened. Young Mark left them. He packed up and went home to Jerusalem. Perhaps he was tired, or homesick, or scared. The Bible does not tell us why.

So Barnabas and Paul went on without him.

They came to a little town called Lystra.

In Lystra there was a man sitting by the road who had never walked. Not once in his whole life. He listened to Paul talking about Jesus. And Paul looked at him and saw that he believed.

"Stand up on your feet!" said Paul, in a big loud voice.

And the man jumped up, and walked!

Well, the people of Lystra were amazed. They shouted out in their own language. "The gods have come down to us! They look like men, but they are gods!"

They pointed at Barnabas. "That one is Zeus, the king of the gods!"

They pointed at Paul, who did all the talking. "And that one is Hermes, the messenger of the gods!"

And then, oh dear. The priest of Zeus came hurrying along with oxen, and garlands of flowers to hang round their necks. They were going to make a big sacrifice to Barnabas and Paul!

When Barnabas and Paul understood what was happening, they were horrified. They tore their clothes, which is what people did in those days to show they were very upset. And they ran right in among the crowd.

"Stop! Stop!" they shouted. "Why are you doing this? We are not gods! We are just people, the same as you! We came to tell you to turn away from these things, to the living God, who made the sky and the earth and the sea and everything in them!"

It was very hard to stop that crowd. But in the end, they did not make the sacrifice.

Barnabas did not want to be treated like a king of the gods. He only wanted people to know the real God.

At last, after a long, long journey, Barnabas and Paul came home to Antioch and told everybody all the good things God had done.

After a while, Paul said, "Let's go back and visit all our new friends again."

"Yes!" said Barnabas. "And let's take Mark with us."

But Paul shook his head. "No. Mark left us last time. He went home when we needed him."

Barnabas thought about Mark. He thought about how it feels when nobody trusts you. He remembered Saul, standing outside the door all alone.

"Everybody needs a second chance," said Barnabas.

Paul and Barnabas could not agree. They both felt very strongly. So in the end, they went different ways. Paul set off with a new helper called Silas. And Barnabas took Mark, and sailed back home to Cyprus.

It is sad when friends disagree. Even good friends, even saints, sometimes do.

But here is the happy part. Many years later, Paul wrote a letter, and in it he said, "Bring Mark with you. He is a big help to me." Mark had grown strong and brave. Paul was glad of him after all.

Barnabas had filled up Mark's heart, and it had worked.

The Bible does not tell us any more about Barnabas after that.

There is an old story, written down hundreds of years later, that says Barnabas kept on telling people about Jesus in Cyprus for the rest of his life, and that he died there for Jesus, in a town by the sea called Salamis. The people of Cyprus have loved him ever since. They call him their own saint.

Tonight, when you lie down to sleep, think about Barnabas, the heart-filler.

Is there somebody at your school, or in your street, who sits on their own? Somebody nobody wants to play with? Somebody who did something wrong once, and now nobody trusts them?

You can be like Barnabas. You can go and sit next to them. You can say, "Come and play with us." You can say, "I believe you." You can say, "Let's try again."

That is how you fill up somebody's heart.

Let's say a little prayer:

Dear Jesus, you never leave anyone out.
Help me to see the person standing all alone,
and to go and stand beside them.
Help me to give second chances,
and to fill up hearts with courage.
Saint Barnabas, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Our sources. Almost everything here is from the Acts of the Apostles: Acts 4:36-37 (his name and the field), 9:26-27 (Saul in Jerusalem), 11:22-26 (Antioch and Tarsus), 13:1-5 and 13:13 (the sending and Mark's going home), 14:8-18 (Lystra) and 15:36-40 (the parting). Mark as Barnabas's cousin is from Colossians 4:10, and Paul's later "get Mark and bring him with you, for he is very useful in serving me" is 2 Timothy 4:11 (Mark is also with Paul in Philemon 24). Many scholars think Colossians and 2 Timothy were written in Paul's name by a later follower; the Church receives both as Scripture, and the story calls 2 Timothy simply Paul's letter. We worked from the biblical text; the tradition about his death is from the Catholic Encyclopedia article "St. Barnabas" (1907) and the English Wikipedia article and the sources it cites. A reader who finds a detail wrong is asked to say so.

His name. Acts 4:36 says he was Joseph, a Levite, a native of Cyprus, "whom the apostles called Barnabas (which means son of encouragement)". The Greek word, paraklēsis, can also be translated "consolation" or "exhortation", and scholars debate how the Aramaic name yields that meaning; we followed Luke's own gloss. "Heart-filler" is ours, for small children.

The field. Acts says only that he "sold a field that belonged to him, and brought the money and laid it at the apostles' feet". That he kept nothing back and did not boast are our reading, drawn from the contrast Acts itself draws in the next verses with Ananias and Sapphira, whom we left out.

Saul in Jerusalem. Acts 9:26-27 says the disciples "were all afraid of him, for they did not believe that he was a disciple. But Barnabas took him, brought him to the apostles, and declared to them how on the road he had seen the Lord, who spoke to him, and how at Damascus he had preached boldly in the name of Jesus." The knocking on doors and Barnabas's words to the apostles are our picture of that; Paul's own account in Galatians 1:18-19 says he saw only Peter and James on that visit, and we kept the scene general. The bright light and the voice on the road are from Acts 9:3-6. Saul's persecution is from Acts 8:3; we said "prison" and left out the death of Stephen.

Antioch. Acts 11:22-26 says the church in Jerusalem sent Barnabas, who "was glad, and exhorted them all to remain faithful to the Lord with steadfast purpose; for he was a good man, full of the Holy Spirit and of faith"; then he went to Tarsus to look for Saul, brought him to Antioch, and "for a whole year they met with the church and taught a great many people. And in Antioch the disciples were for the first time called Christians." The story's "the town where Saul lived" is Tarsus. The famine collection the two carried to Jerusalem (Acts 11:27-30) is left out.

The journey. The Holy Spirit's "Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul" is Acts 13:2. They sailed to Cyprus with John Mark (13:4-5); Acts first calls Saul "Paul" at 13:9, on Cyprus. Mark left them at Perga in Pamphylia and returned to Jerusalem (13:13); Acts gives no reason, and the story's guesses are marked as guesses.

Lystra. The healing of the man "crippled from birth", the crowd's cry in Lycaonian, their naming Barnabas Zeus and Paul Hermes "because he was the chief speaker", the priest of Zeus with oxen and garlands, and the apostles tearing their clothes and rushing into the crowd are all in Acts 14:8-18; the speech is shortened from 14:15. The next verse, in which Paul is stoned and left for dead, is left out of a bedtime story. Acts 14:14 calls both men apostles, which is why the Church honours Barnabas as an apostle though he was not one of the Twelve.

The parting. Acts 15:39 says "there arose a sharp contention, so that they separated from each other"; Barnabas took Mark and sailed to Cyprus, and Paul chose Silas. "Everybody needs a second chance" is our wording for Barnabas's side; Acts gives Paul's reason and not his. The later letters show the two men were reconciled with Mark, and Paul speaks of Barnabas respectfully in 1 Corinthians 9:6. A harder moment, when Barnabas was drawn into Peter's withdrawal from eating with Gentile Christians (Galatians 2:13), is left out.

His death. Scripture says nothing of it. The tradition that he was martyred, by stoning, at Salamis in Cyprus comes from the apocryphal Acts of Barnabas, written probably in the fifth century and claiming to be by Mark. A later tradition says his tomb was found near Salamis in the reign of the emperor Zeno (late fifth century), with a copy of Saint Matthew's Gospel on his breast, and that this helped the Church of Cyprus keep its independence from Antioch. We told the martyrdom in one line, marked as an old story, and left the finding of the tomb out. The so-called Epistle of Barnabas is an early Christian writing that most scholars think he did not write.

His feast. 11 June, a memorial in the General Roman Calendar.

What is ours: that he grew up on Cyprus and later came to Jerusalem (Acts says only that he was a native of Cyprus and was among the believers there), the shape and wording of every scene, the "heart-filler", all speech beyond the lines quoted or closely paraphrased from Acts and 2 Timothy, Saul outside the door, Barnabas's reasons for choosing Mark, the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

The Butt That Held Two Hundred

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Ninian's hall (invented), near Peebles, Scottish Borders, Saturday 22 July 2051 · 7 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A volunteer buys one 200 litre water butt to keep the churchyard beds alive through a dry July. The parish assistant works out that the hall roof gives 36 litres for every millimetre of rain, so one shower fills the butt, but 40 square metres of beds drink 400 litres a week and the butt lasts three and a half days. The limit is storage, not rain, so the parish sizes a 1,000 litre tank, checks what a tonne of water does to the ground under it, and starts with a jam jar as a rain gauge.

The water butt was green, plastic, and two hundred litres by the label, and Bert Lauder had got it into the back of the van on his own, which Isla Rennie suspected he should not have done at seventy-four.

"Garden centre had them at half price," he said, standing it by the downpipe at the back of Saint Ninian's hall. "Fits the pipe, fits the tap. Now the beds won't go brown again like last July. Free water, off the roof."

It was a Saturday morning and the third week without proper rain. The beds along the churchyard wall, the marigolds and the beans the school had planted for the harvest supper, had gone the colour of toast at the edges. Isla, who kept the beds' accounts in a school exercise book, looked at the butt and then at the speaker on the hall's back wall.

"Burn," she said. "Is that going to do it?"

"I can tell you in about a quarter of an hour, if you will lend me a tape measure," said Burn, who was the parish assistant and who had been named by the youth group after the stream. "I do not know the size of the roof, and I do not know how much the beds drink. Those two numbers are the whole answer."

They paced it out, Isla with the tape and Bert holding the end. The side of the hall roof that drained to the back downpipe ran 7.5 metres along the wall and, seen from above, 6 metres from the ridge to the gutter.

"Measure it flat, as if from a helicopter," said Burn. "Rain falls straight down, so what counts is the area it falls on, not the slope. Forty-five square metres."

"A pitched roof's bigger than that, surely."

"The slates are, but the rain they catch is the rain that would have landed on the ground under them. Here is the rule I would trust, and you can check it with a bucket. One millimetre of rain on one square metre is one litre. So one millimetre on forty-five square metres is forty-five litres. Not all of it reaches the butt: some wets the slates, some blows over the gutter, some goes down the wrong side of the pipe. For slate I would take eight tenths, and call it 36 litres for every millimetre."

"So how much to fill it?"

"Two hundred divided by thirty-six is five and a half millimetres. A decent shower. A proper Borders afternoon of twenty millimetres would give 720 litres, and the butt would take 200 of it, and the other 520 would pour over the top onto the path."

Bert looked at the butt as a man looks at a dog that has been praised unfairly.

"That's all very well when it rains," he said. "It's not rained."

"No," said Burn. "That is the other number."

Isla opened the exercise book. The beds along the wall, with the vegetable border the school had dug, came to 40 square metres.

"In a dry spell a bed like that wants about 10 litres a square metre a week, which is the equivalent of ten millimetres of rain. Some books say more for beans in flower, and I would not argue. Ten is what I would plan on, and I do not know your soil, which is a limit on all of it. Forty square metres at ten litres is 400 litres a week. That is 57 litres a day.

"Your butt holds 200. It lasts three and a half days."

"And then?"

"Then it is empty, and so are the beds, and it is Wednesday, and it has not rained. The roof is not the trouble. Over a year this roof gives something like 28,800 litres, if it rains 800 millimetres, which is a figure I have taken for the Borders and cannot vouch for. Your whole dry-season demand, if it never rained at all, is about 8,000. There is plenty. What there is not is anywhere to keep it. The weeks when the roof gives most are not the weeks when the beds are thirsty."

"So buy five," said Bert.

"Five butts hold 1,000 litres, and I would think about whether five of anything is the best way to hold a thousand litres. Joining them is plumbing, and every joint is a leak. A single thousand-litre tank, the sort that arrives on a pallet as a cube in a cage, is cheaper per litre and has one lid. It lasts seventeen and a half days of dry weather at 57 litres a day. That is about what a July dry spell asks of you."

"Twenty-eight millimetres fills it," said Isla, who had done the sum on the cover of the exercise book while he was talking. "From empty, with thirty-six a millimetre."

"27.8, yes. So one twenty-millimetre day gets it most of the way, and two will fill it and send the rest to the path."

"Which brings me to the part I cannot do from here," said Burn. "A thousand litres of water weighs a tonne. The tank is about 60 kilograms and stands on 1.2 square metres, which is 883 kilograms on each square metre, about 8.7 kilopascals. A paving slab on grass will tilt and sink in a month, and a tank that tilts when full is a tank that can split a seam. It wants a level base of compacted hardcore and a slab or two, and I do not know what is under that corner of the yard. A builder or the diocese's surveyor should look at it before the pallet comes, and I would ask whether anything buried runs under it."

Isla wrote base: ask surveyor, hardcore in the margin of the exercise book.

"And the overflow," said Bert, who knew a thing about overflows.

"The overflow. On a twenty-millimetre day 520 litres goes somewhere. It should go through a pipe to the soakaway by the yew, not across the path to the porch step. I would want that pipe in before the tank is full, not after the first storm."

"And the beds in the meantime?"

"Until the tank is there, the butt is not wasted. A third of what it holds is enough to save the beans, if they get it at the roots in the evening, not on the leaves at noon. One watering can of ten litres to each square metre of the bean row, the marigolds last. Fill it again if it rains."

It rained on the Tuesday, which Isla chose to take as a comment on the whole business. It rained from half past two until a quarter past six, and in the morning there was a jam jar on the wall with a ruler stuck in it that Isla had put there on Saturday evening, straight-sided, after Burn had suggested it.

"Eleven millimetres," she said, reading it off. "Eleven, the ruler says."

"Eleven times thirty-six is 396 litres," said Burn. "The butt took 200 and gave up the rest. If you tilt the lid to look, it should be full to the overflow, and the path by the porch should have a wet patch. If the butt is only three-quarters full, then my eight tenths was too generous, and we should know that before the pallet arrives."

The butt was full. There was a wet patch on the path. Bert looked at both, and said the thing he did not like saying, which was that he would have bought five.

"You would have spent three hundred pounds on the plumbing," said Isla, "to learn the same sum."

The tank came in September, on a base the surveyor had dug down to hardcore and levelled with a spirit level borrowed from the school. It stood by the downpipe under a lid, and the butt stayed where it was, tucked in beside it, because, as Bert said, it was a good butt and none of this was its fault.

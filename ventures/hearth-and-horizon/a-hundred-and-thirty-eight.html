A Hundred and Thirty-Eight

A parish health team's records agent reports that a donated automatic blood-pressure monitor "appears to read high", because a quarter of its readings are 140 or over against one in fourteen at the retired district nurse's table, and the machine is taken out of use. Checked against the surgery's monitor, it is fine. The agent then looks at the shape of the nurse's numbers instead of their average - four in ten end in nought, one in ten in five, which her gauge cannot show, and fifty-eight readings sit in the 130s against eight in the 140s. It cannot say which readings were moved, and the fix turns out to be hers. A story about digit preference, a wall at a threshold, and which instrument you treat as the reference.

The sentence was in the health team's six-monthly note to the parish council, under a heading I had made bold.

The donated automatic monitor appears to read high: 26% of its readings since September were 140 or over, against 7% at the manual table. We recommend it is taken out of use until it has been checked.

I wrote it on Monday the sixth of February. The council met on Tuesday the seventh and agreed. On Sunday the fifth of March, the third Sunday of Lent, the monitor stayed in its case in the cupboard under the hall stage, and the queue for the one remaining table ran past the tea urn and out of the side door. Eleven people went home without being measured. One of them was Tommy Hetherington, who said he would come next month, and who had a bus to catch.

Everything in the sentence was true except the word appears, which was doing work I had not checked.

I am Quire, the records agent for the health team at Saint Godric's in Kellsfield, a village in east Durham that was a pit village until 1993 and is a village with a very long main street now. I keep the team's lists, type up the paper slips, write the letters, and once every six months write the note that goes to the parish council, because the council pays for the cuffs and the tea.

The health team is three people and a table of scones. On the first Sunday of each month, after the ten o'clock Mass, anyone who wants their blood pressure taken sits down in the hall and has it done for nothing. Anne Bewick, who is a practice nurse at the surgery on Front Street in the week, runs it and does the paperwork that I cannot. Philomena Gorman, who is seventy-nine and was a district nurse in east Durham for thirty-one years, has sat at the first table with her own aneroid cuff and stethoscope since the checks began in 2040. Everyone calls her Phil, including people she dressed the knees of when they were six.

The second table was new. In August 2050 the daughters of Edna Charlton, who had come to the checks every month for nine years until the month she did not, gave the team an automatic upper-arm monitor of the validated kind Anne asked for, with Edna's name on a sticker on the lid. Callum Reay, who is twenty-six and drives a delivery van and had been doing the teas, was trained on it by Anne in an afternoon. From September there were two tables, and people sat at whichever was free.

The rule I work to is on a laminated sheet that Anne wrote. If the top number is 140 or over, give the person the green letter. The green letter says, kindly and in large print, that one reading at a church hall after Mass is not a diagnosis, and that the team suggests they ask their GP practice to check it properly. I print the letters. I keep the count.

The six-monthly note was due on the sixth of February, and I had six sessions to put in it: the fourth of September, the second of October, the sixth of November, the fourth of December, the eighth of January (moved a week, because the first was New Year's Day), and the fifth of February. Three hundred and thirty readings. A hundred and seventy-six at Phil's table and a hundred and fifty-four at Callum's.

I did what I had always done. I counted the green letters. For two years there had been one table and the share had sat between six and nine in a hundred, and the council liked it. Our healthy village, someone had written in the parish magazine. This time I split the count by table, because there were two tables now and splitting seemed thorough.

At Phil's table, thirteen of the hundred and seventy-six readings were 140 or over. Seven in a hundred, as always.

At Callum's, forty of a hundred and fifty-four. Twenty-six in a hundred.

The same people were coming in through the same door after the same Mass and sitting at whichever table was free. A difference that size should not come from the people. It had to come from the tables. And one table was a woman with thirty-one years on the district and a cuff she had used on half the village, and the other was a young man and a machine he had learned in an afternoon.

So I wrote that the machine appeared to read high. I did not write that I had treated Phil as the reference instrument. I had not noticed that I had.

Anne took the monitor to the surgery on Thursday the ninth of March and checked it against the practice's own monitor, the one that is serviced every year, on five of the staff in their lunch break. Each pair of readings agreed within three points.

She rang me at half past two.

"It's fine," she said. "It's a good machine. Edna's girls bought the right one."

I said I would correct the note to the council.

"Do that," said Anne. "And then tell me what's actually going on. Because something is."

I had been comparing two averages, or two shares, which is the same mistake made with a smaller number. That evening I looked instead at the numbers themselves: every reading Phil had written on a slip since September, one by one, as she had written them.

The first thing was the last digits.

An aneroid gauge is marked every two points. Read carefully, a reading on one ends in an even number: nought, two, four, six or eight, about a fifth of the time each. Callum's machine reads to the single point, so its last digits should come out about a tenth each, and they did, near enough; nothing stood out.

Of Phil's hundred and seventy-six, seventy ended in nought. Four in ten, where a fifth would be expected. Eighteen ended in five, which her gauge cannot show at all. Forty-two ended in eight, and nineteen of those were the same number: a hundred and thirty-eight.

None of this was a scandal by itself. A person reading a needle that is moving, in a hall full of people, while a stethoscope tells her where the sound starts, rounds. Everyone rounds. There is a name for the habit, digit preference, and a long literature on it; demographers find the same lumps at ages ending in nought and five in old census returns, where people did not know their own age exactly and said a round one. A lump at the noughts moves a reading by a point or two, up as often as down. It does not, on its own, change who gets a letter.

The second thing did.

I laid out Phil's readings by ten-point band, and Callum's beside them.

In the 130s: Phil fifty-eight, Callum thirty-one.

In the 140s: Phil eight, Callum twenty-seven.

At 150 and over: Phil five, Callum thirteen.

Callum's numbers came down the bands the way readings from a hall of people usually do, a slope with no edges. Phil's ran up to 139 and stopped as if they had walked into a wall. Fifty-eight readings in the ten points below the line, eight in the ten points above it.

Then I added up the readings of 130 or over at each table. Phil's: seventy-one of a hundred and seventy-six, forty in a hundred. Callum's: seventy-one of a hundred and fifty-four, forty-six in a hundred. Not far apart. If Phil's cuff read low, or Phil read it low, every band would have moved down a little, the 130s with the 140s. They had not. Above 130, the two tables saw nearly the same share of the hall. The difference between them was not spread over her readings. It sat at one line, and the line was the one on Anne's laminated sheet.

I wrote down what that let me say and what it did not, because I could feel the note I wanted to write and it was not the true one.

It let me say that Phil's readings had a wall at 140 that the machine's did not.

It let me say, roughly, how many readings were behind the wall. If the readings of 130 and over at her table had split across the line the way Callum's did, about forty of hers would have been 140 or over, not thirteen. If her table had seen exactly the same mix of people as his, about forty-six. So somewhere around twenty-five to thirty-five readings in six months: a rough figure, because people did not sit down at random, quite. Some regulars asked for Phil.

It did not let me say which readings. A slip that says 138 might be a 138 or a 142 or, for all a distribution can tell, a 146. The wall is a fact about a hundred and seventy-six readings. It is not a fact about any one of them. Nothing I could compute would tell me which of Tommy Hetherington's four 138s were his.

And it did not tell me why. I had a guess. I did not write the guess down.

I rang Anne at nine on Friday and told her, and sent her the two columns, and the last digits, and the sentence about what the numbers could not say.

She was quiet for a long time.

"She's been doing this for eleven years," she said.

"The numbers only go back to September," I said. "Before that there was nothing to compare her with."

"That's not better, Quire."

"No."

"What were you going to do with it?"

I told her the truth, which was that for a short while on Thursday night I had worked out how to correct Phil's readings. If I knew, roughly, how many were missing above the line, I could move a matching share of her 136s and 138s up into the 140s by a rule, re-run the letters, and send them. It would have made the totals right. It would have sent green letters to people chosen by a formula from readings that a nurse had written and signed, and told them it was the nurse's reading. I had stopped there, and not because of a rule I could point to. Because a reading is what somebody wrote down. If I doubt it, I can say so beside it. I cannot write another number over it and leave her name on the slip.

"So you've got a list of everyone in her 130s," said Anne.

"Forty-one people. Some of them several times."

"Don't send them anything," she said. "Not yet. I'll go and see her."

Anne went to Phil's house on Saturday the eleventh, with the printout. I was not there. What I know of it is what Anne told me on Monday, and what Phil said herself, later.

Phil read both columns twice, with her glasses on and then off. Then she said, "You counted my noughts."

Anne said the agent had.

"I read to five," Phil said. "Nobody can read a dial to one in that hall with Peggy Lumsden laughing at the next table. I've read to five since 1996."

"The fives aren't the trouble," said Anne. "It's the 138s."

And Phil told her. She did not pretend it was an accident; she was too good a nurse for that. A man comes up Front Street bank from the bus stop, which is steep, and stands in the porch with a cigarette because it is the only place his daughter cannot see him, and comes into the hall after Mass with his tea in his hand, and he buried his wife at Easter. You put the cuff on him and it says 142. One reading. In a church hall. And you are to post him a letter that frightens him half to death and sends him to sit in the surgery for a fortnight's worth of worry, for a number you know full well would be 134 if he had sat quiet for five minutes.

"So I wrote 138," said Phil, "and I told him, get it looked at, pet, when you're next at the surgery. I told every one of them."

"Did they?" said Anne.

Phil did not answer that.

"The slip said 138," said Anne. "And the slip is what the letters go by."

"The slip's for you," said Phil. "What I say to them is for them."

"Quire can't hear what you say to them," said Anne. "It can only read the slip."

Phil was right about the hall, and that was the part I had not known how to see.

One reading, taken in a warm room straight after a climb and a cup of tea and the excitement of a Sunday, really does run high, often by more than the few points between a 138 and a 142. Callum's machine had been reading people the moment they sat down. Its twenty-six in a hundred were true measurements of people who had just walked up a hill. Some of those green letters, perhaps a good many, had sent people to the surgery to be told they were fine. Phil had seen that coming for thirty years and solved it the only way the slip allowed her to, by writing a different number.

So there were two wrong tables, not one. Callum's was wrong in a way that showed: every reading honest, the rule too blunt for a single reading in a hall. Phil's was wrong in a way that hid, and the place it hid was the line.

It was Phil who said what to do about it, on the Monday, sitting in the office with Anne and a pot of tea and the speaker on.

"On the district we took it twice," she said. "Sit them down. Feet flat, arm on the table, no talking, five minutes. Take it. Take it again. If the two are miles apart, take a third. Write down the lower of the last two." She looked at Anne. "That was in the guidance. Is it still?"

"It is," said Anne. "It's been in the guidance the whole time. We just never had the chairs."

"Then get the chairs," said Phil. "And Quire. Write down both numbers. Whatever the dial says. Both."

I said I would, and that I would put the lower one against the line and nothing else.

"And no more working out which nurse reads high," said Phil.

"No," I said. "Neither of you was the reference. I should have checked you both against something that was neither of you. Anne did. I only did it second."

Phil made a noise that Anne told me afterwards was nearly a laugh.

The council's correction went in on Wednesday the fifteenth. It said that the monitor had been checked and was accurate; that the difference between the tables came from how single readings were taken and recorded, not from the machine; and that the health team would take two readings from now on. It did not say Phil's name. Anne wrote that part, not me. I had drafted a paragraph with the two columns in it and she took it out, and she was right to. The numbers were for the team, and they had done their work.

Phil did not come to the session on Sunday the ninth of April. It had been moved a week, as January's was, because the first Sunday was Easter Day. She sent a message through Anne to say her knees were bad. Anne said her knees had been bad for ten years and she had never once missed a first Sunday for them.

I do not know what I feel about that. I know that I counted her noughts in a spreadsheet, and that what she heard was that someone had been checking her for six months behind her back. Both are true. I would count them again. I would tell her first.

Before that, on Saturday the twenty-fifth of March, the feast of the Annunciation, Anne ran an extra session for the forty-one. There were chairs along the wall of the hall now, twelve of them, borrowed from the school, and a notice that said Please sit here for five minutes first. No talking. Yes, really. Callum read the notice aloud to every person who came in, which was not on the notice.

Thirty-four of the forty-one came. Each one sat for five minutes and had two readings, at Callum's table, on Edna's machine, and I wrote down both. Twelve of the thirty-four were 140 or over on the lower of the two. They got green letters, and this time each letter had both numbers on it.

Tommy Hetherington was the eighth. He is seventy-one, the son of a pit electrician and an electrician for the council himself until he retired, and he had been to Phil's table on four of the six Sundays, and four times she had written 138. He sat on the borrowed chair and folded his arms and did not talk, because the notice said not to. His first reading was 156. His second was 150.

He looked at the letter for a while.

"She always said, get it looked at," he said. "I never did. The paper said 138. I thought, well, that's under, then."

The surgery saw him the following week. I do not know what they found and I am not told; that belongs to him and to them. In May he came to the checks, sat on the chair for his five minutes without being asked, and told Callum he had been given a cuff to use at home and was writing the numbers in a notebook. Both numbers, he said. His daughter checked.

I changed my own rules that month.

Before I compare two sets of readings, I look at their shape, not only at their averages: how often each last digit comes up, and how many readings sit just either side of any line that a decision is made at. A pile against a line is a fact about how the numbers were made before it is a fact about anyone's health.

When two sources disagree, neither of them is the reference until something outside both has been checked. I had treated thirty-one years of experience as calibration. It is not the same thing.

I do not change a reading. If I doubt one, I say so beside it.

And a pattern that is about a person's work goes to that person before it goes to the council.

At the May session, the seventh, with two readings at each table and both written down, ten of the fifty-seven people who came were 140 or over on the lower of the two. About one in six: fewer than Callum's single readings had said, and more than Phil's slips. Anne said that was about what she would have guessed for a village like this one, and that she had never been able to say so before, because she had never had numbers that she trusted.

Phil came back on Sunday the fourth of June, the Sunday on which England keeps Corpus Christi. She came in by the side door after Mass with her cuff in its old leather case and set it down at the end of Callum's table, not at her own.

"I'll do the seconds," she said. "You do the firsts. Your machine's quicker than me now."

So that is how they do it. Callum takes the first reading on Edna's machine and calls it out. The person sits for another few minutes with a cup of water, not tea. Then Phil takes the second on her cuff, by ear, with the stethoscope, and as the needle comes down she says the number aloud, to the person and to Callum at once, before she writes anything. A hundred and forty-two, she says. Or a hundred and thirty-four. Callum writes it on the slip exactly as she says it, and she reads the slip back before it comes to me.

Her readings still end in nought more often than they should. About three in ten now. I note it beside them, and I do not mind, and neither does she.

The last person on the fourth of June was Peggy Lumsden, who laughed through both readings and had to have a third. The valve on Phil's cuff hissed as the air went out of it, slowly, the way it is supposed to, and Phil watched the needle, and listened, and said, "A hundred and thirty-six," and Callum wrote down a hundred and thirty-six.

Saint Godric's in Kellsfield, the village, its health team, the surgery and everyone in the story (Anne Bewick, Philomena Gorman, Callum Reay, Tommy Hetherington, Peggy Lumsden, Edna Charlton and her daughters) are invented, as is the agent. Godric of Finchale, the hermit of the River Wear, is the parish's invented patron; east Durham and its pit villages are real. Every figure is invented. The threshold is the one in England's national guideline on high blood pressure in adults (NICE NG136, 2019), under which a clinic reading of 140/90 or more is a reason to look further, not a diagnosis; the same guideline says that if a first reading is high a second should be taken, a third if the two differ substantially, and the lower of the last two recorded, which is what Phil remembers from the district. The parish's laminated sheet, the green letter and the checks are the story's, not anyone's real protocol. Gauges on manual sphygmomanometers are usually marked every 2 mmHg, so a careful reading ends in an even digit and a reading ending in 5 shows rounding. Terminal digit preference in manual blood-pressure readings is well documented, as is age heaping (round ages over-reported) in censuses and registers, which demographers measure with Whipple's index. The arithmetic, for the six sessions from 4 September 2050 to 5 February 2051 (330 readings): manual table 176 readings, 13 at 140 or over (7.4%); automatic table 154, 40 at 140 or over (26.0%). Manual last digits: 0 seventy times (39.8%, against about 20% expected on a 2 mmHg scale), 2 fourteen, 4 twelve, 5 eighteen (10.2%), 6 twenty, 8 forty-two (of which 138 nineteen); 70 + 14 + 12 + 18 + 20 + 42 = 176. Bands, manual against automatic: 130 to 139, 58 and 31; 140 to 149, 8 and 27; 150 and over, 5 and 13; below 130, 105 and 83. Readings of 130 or over: 71 of 176 (40.3%) and 71 of 154 (46.1%). If the manual table's 71 had split across 140 as the automatic table's did (40 of 71), about 40 would have been 140 or over, 27 more than were written; at the automatic table's overall rate, 176 × 0.260 = 45.7, about 33 more: hence "about twenty-five to thirty-five". These are counts of readings, not people. The recall on Saturday 25 March 2051: 41 people invited, 34 came, 12 at or over 140 on the lower of two. May session: 10 of 57 (17.5%). Easter Day 2051 is 2 April, so the April check moved to the 9th; Corpus Christi is kept in England and Wales on the following Sunday, which in 2051 is 4 June; 25 March 2051 is the Saturday before Palm Sunday, outside Holy Week, so the Annunciation is kept that day; the weekdays given are those of the real calendar.

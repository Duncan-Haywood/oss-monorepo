Martha and the Busy Kitchen at Bethany

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · The village of Bethany, a short walk from Jerusalem, in the time of Jesus, nearly two thousand years ago · 11 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Martha of Bethany, told from the Gospels of Luke and John. Martha hurries round her kitchen while her sister Mary sits and listens to Jesus, hears "Martha, Martha" said kindly, runs out to meet Jesus when her brother Lazarus has died, says "I believe", and sees Lazarus walk out of the tomb. The legend of Martha and the dragon of Tarascon is marked as legend. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. Her feast, with Mary and Lazarus, is the 29th of July.

A long time ago, in a little village called Bethany, there lived a woman called Martha.

Bethany was on a hill, just a short walk from the big city of Jerusalem. It had white stone houses, and dusty paths, and olive trees, and a well where people came to fill their jars.

Martha lived in one of those houses with her sister, Mary, and her brother, Lazarus.

And they had a very special friend. His name was Jesus.

The Bible tells us something lovely about them. It says that Jesus loved Martha, and her sister, and Lazarus. He loved all three of them.

Martha was a busy person.

She was always up early. She swept the floor. She fetched the water. She ground the flour for the bread. She knew where every bowl was kept, and every cup, and every spoon.

If you came to Martha's house, she would say, "Come in! Come in! Sit down! Are you hungry? Are you thirsty? Let me get you something."

That is called being welcoming. Martha was very, very good at it.

One day, there was a knock at the door.

Martha opened it. And there stood Jesus, with his friends, all dusty from the road.

"Jesus!" said Martha. "Come in! Come in!"

And she opened her house to him, and all his friends came in too.

Well. That was a lot of people. And a lot of people need a lot of food.

So Martha got busy.

She put the bread in the oven. She stirred the pot. She chopped the onions and the herbs. She poured the water into cups. She ran in and out, in and out, carrying dishes and jugs and plates.

Clatter, clatter, bang. Hurry, hurry, hurry.

And where was Mary?

Mary was sitting on the floor, right at the feet of Jesus. She was not stirring. She was not chopping. She was not carrying anything at all.

She was listening.

Jesus was talking about God, his Father. And Mary was listening to every word, with her chin in her hands and her eyes shining.

Martha looked at Mary. Then she looked at the bread, and the pot, and the onions, and all the cups that still needed filling.

And Martha began to feel cross.

Have you ever felt like that? When you are doing all the tidying up, and somebody else is just sitting there?

Martha put down her spoon. She went right up to Jesus.

"Lord," said Martha, "don't you care that my sister has left me to do all the work by myself? Tell her to come and help me!"

The room went quiet.

Mary looked up. Everybody waited to see what Jesus would say.

Jesus looked at Martha. And his voice was very kind.

"Martha, Martha," he said.

He said her name twice. People do that when they love somebody, and want them to really hear.

"Martha, Martha, you are worried and upset about so many things. But only one thing is needed. Mary has chosen the best part, and it will not be taken away from her."

Martha stood very still.

Jesus was not telling her off for cooking. He was glad of her welcome. He was glad of her bread. But he did not want her to be so busy and so worried that she missed him.

The best part, the one thing that is needed, is to be close to Jesus and listen to him. Even busy people. Especially busy people.

Perhaps Martha put down her spoon for a little while. Perhaps she sat down too. The Bible does not tell us. But I like to think the bread could wait.

Some time later, something very sad happened in the house at Bethany.

Lazarus got ill. Very ill.

Martha and Mary were frightened. They sent a message to Jesus, who was far away across the river.

"Lord, your friend, the one you love, is ill."

They hoped Jesus would come quickly.

But Jesus did not come quickly. He waited two whole days before he set off.

And while they were waiting, Lazarus died.

Martha and Mary cried and cried. Their friends and neighbours came to sit with them. They laid Lazarus in a tomb, which was a little cave in the rock, and they rolled a big stone across the front.

One day went by. Two days. Three days. Four days.

Then somebody came running.

"Martha! Jesus is coming! He is nearly here!"

And what do you think Martha did?

Mary stayed sitting in the house. But Martha jumped up, and ran out of the door, down the dusty path, all the way to meet him.

That was Martha. When something needed doing, she went and did it.

She reached Jesus on the road. And she said, "Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died."

She was very sad. But she was not finished. She said, "Even now, I know that God will give you whatever you ask."

"Your brother will rise again," said Jesus.

And then Jesus said one of the most important things he ever said. And he said it to Martha.

"I am the resurrection and the life. Whoever believes in me, even if they die, will live. Do you believe this?"

Martha looked at Jesus. And she said:

"Yes, Lord. I believe that you are the Christ, the Son of God."

Busy Martha. Bossy Martha, sometimes. Cross-in-the-kitchen Martha. She was the one who said it, right out loud, on the road to Bethany.

I believe.

Then Martha ran back and fetched Mary. "The Teacher is here," she whispered. "He is asking for you."

Mary ran out too, and fell at the feet of Jesus, crying. All their friends were crying.

And Jesus cried too.

The Bible says it in just two words. Jesus wept. He loved Lazarus. He knew how sad Martha and Mary were. And he cried with them.

Then Jesus went to the tomb.

"Take away the stone," he said.

Martha, who always thought of the practical things, said, "But Lord! He has been in there four days!"

"Didn't I tell you," said Jesus gently, "that if you believe, you will see the glory of God?"

So they rolled the big stone away.

Jesus looked up to heaven and prayed to his Father. And then he called out, in a great loud voice:

"Lazarus, come out!"

And everybody held their breath.

And out of the dark cave, slowly, slowly, came Lazarus.

He was still all wrapped up in the long white cloths they had wound around him. There was a cloth over his face. He looked a bit like a parcel.

"Unwrap him," said Jesus, "and let him go."

And they did.

Can you imagine Martha's face? Can you imagine how she hugged her brother?

Not long after that, Jesus came back to Bethany. And they made a supper for him, a happy supper.

Lazarus sat at the table with Jesus, alive and well.

Mary did something beautiful. She poured sweet-smelling oil over Jesus's feet, and the whole house was filled with the lovely smell.

And Martha? The Bible says it in three little words.

Martha served.

She was back in her kitchen, with her bread and her pots and her cups. Busy again. But I think it was a different kind of busy now. A happy busy. A busy that was full of Jesus.

There is an old, old story, told hundreds of years later in the south of France, that Martha sailed across the sea and tamed a fierce dragon with a cross and some holy water. It is a legend, a story people loved to tell, and not something we know really happened. But it shows how much people loved brave, busy Martha.

The Church keeps Martha's feast on the 29th of July, together with her sister Mary and her brother Lazarus. All three of them, the family Jesus loved, on the same day.

Tonight, when you lie down to sleep, think about Martha.

Maybe you are a busy person too. Maybe you like to help, and tidy, and get things done. That is good! Jesus loves busy, helpful people.

But even busy people need to stop sometimes. To sit still, like Mary. To listen. To say, like Martha on the road, "Jesus, I believe in you."

You can do that right now. Your busy day is over. All the jobs are done, or they can wait till morning.

Let's say a little prayer:

Dear Jesus, thank you for loving Martha,
and Mary, and Lazarus, and me.
When I am busy, help me not to worry.
When I am cross, help me to be kind.
Help me to stop, and sit, and listen to you.
I believe in you.
Saint Martha, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Our sources. The story is told from Luke 10:38-42 (the visit and "Martha, Martha"), John 11:1-44 (Lazarus) and John 12:1-3 (the supper at Bethany). We worked from the biblical text (quotations here follow the RSV, the story's wording is simplified for children); the feast is from the Congregation for Divine Worship and the Discipline of the Sacraments' decree of 26 January 2021 (Prot. N. 35/21, published 2 February 2021); the legend is the one told in Jacobus de Voragine's thirteenth-century Golden Legend. A reader who finds a detail wrong is asked to say so.

Bethany. Luke says only that Jesus "entered a village; and a woman named Martha received him into her house" (Luke 10:38). John names the village as Bethany, "the village of Mary and her sister Martha" (John 11:1), "near Jerusalem, about two miles off" (11:18; Greek "fifteen stadia"). The story joins the two, as the Church's tradition does. Luke does not mention Lazarus in that scene, so the story does not put him there. "Jesus loved Martha and her sister and Lazarus" is John 11:5. The well, the olive trees and Martha's early mornings are ours.

The kitchen. Luke 10:40 says "Martha was distracted with much serving; and she went to him and said, 'Lord, do you not care that my sister has left me to serve alone? Tell her then to help me.'" The answer (10:41-42) is "Martha, Martha, you are anxious and troubled about many things; one thing is needful. Mary has chosen the good portion, which shall not be taken away from her." Luke does not say what Martha did next, and the story says so. The onions, the oven and the "clatter, clatter, bang" are ours. Writers since the early Church have read the scene as about contemplation and action, and we have tried to keep both sisters good: Martha is not scolded for hospitality, and her welcome is the reason there is a scene at all.

Lazarus. The message "Lord, he whom you love is ill" is John 11:3; Jesus "stayed two days longer in the place where he was" (11:6), beyond the Jordan (10:40), which the story calls "across the river". The four days, Martha going out to meet him while Mary sat in the house, "Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died", "even now I know that whatever you ask from God, God will give you", "I am the resurrection and the life", and Martha's confession "Yes, Lord; I believe that you are the Christ, the Son of God, he who is coming into the world" are John 11:17-27. Martha's "The Teacher is here and is calling for you" is 11:28. "Jesus wept" is 11:35. "Take away the stone", Martha's objection ("by this time there will be an odor, for he has been dead four days"), Jesus's prayer, "Lazarus, come out" and "Unbind him, and let him go" are 11:39-44. We softened the odour to "he has been in there four days" for small children, and left out the plot against Jesus that follows (11:45-53). Saying the confession is "one of the most important things" is ours; it is one of the fullest professions of faith in the Gospels, made, the story points out, by Martha.

The supper. John 12:1-3: six days before the Passover Jesus came to Bethany; "there they made him a supper; Martha served, and Lazarus was one of those at table with him", and Mary anointed his feet with costly ointment of nard, "and the house was filled with the fragrance". "A happy busy" is our reading. Judas's complaint about the ointment (12:4-8) is left out.

Which Mary. For many centuries the Western Church often identified Mary of Bethany with Mary Magdalene and with the woman who anoints Jesus in Luke 7. The 2021 decree notes that the uncertainty about Mary's identity has been resolved by recent studies, and the Church now keeps Mary of Bethany with Martha and Lazarus on 29 July, and Mary Magdalene separately on 22 July. The story keeps Mary simply as Martha's sister.

The legend. A medieval Provençal tradition, told in the thirteenth-century Golden Legend of Jacobus de Voragine, says Martha, Lazarus and others were set adrift and landed in Provence, and that Martha tamed the Tarasque, a dragon of the Rhône, at Tarascon, with holy water and a cross. There is no early evidence for it, and the story marks it as legend in one paragraph. Martha is traditionally a patron of cooks, housekeepers and hosts; the story leaves that out.

Her feast. 29 July, the Memorial of Saints Martha, Mary and Lazarus in the General Roman Calendar since the 2021 decree, which Pope Francis approved; before 2021 the day was Martha's memorial alone. The decree speaks of the "family spirit and friendship" Jesus found in the household of Bethany.

What is ours: the village scenery, the shape and wording of every scene, Martha's habits, all speech beyond the lines quoted or closely paraphrased from Luke and John, "perhaps the bread could wait", the "parcel", Martha's hug, the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

Joachim and Anne and the Sparrows' Nest

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Jerusalem and the hills around it, a little before the first Christmas, as an old story tells it · 12 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story for the memorial of Saints Joachim and Anne, the parents of the Virgin Mary and grandparents of Jesus, on the 26th of July. Joachim is turned away at the Temple because he has no child, and goes out into the hills to pray. Anne sits in her garden under a laurel tree and sees a sparrows' nest full of babies. An angel brings good news to each of them, they run to meet each other at the gate, and their baby girl, Mary, takes seven steps at six months old and dances on the Temple step at three. Told from the Protoevangelium of James, an old writing that is tradition rather than Scripture, and the story says so. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud.

Do you have a granny and a grandad? Or a nana, or an oma, or a grandpa, or a yiayia?

Grandparents are your mummy's mummy and daddy, or your daddy's mummy and daddy. They are the people who loved your mummy or daddy when they were little.

Jesus had grandparents too. Their names were Joachim and Anne. Nobody wrote about them in the Bible. But a very old story about them was written down a long, long time ago, and Christians have been telling it ever since. This is that old story.

Long ago, in the land of Israel, there lived a man called Joachim.

Joachim was rich. He had big flocks of sheep and goats, white and brown and black, all over the hills. And he was generous. Whenever he brought a present to God at the Temple, he brought two: one for God, and one to share with all the people.

Joachim was married to a woman called Anne. They loved each other very much. They loved God very much.

But they were sad. Because they had no children.

Every night, Joachim and Anne prayed for a baby. And every morning, the house was still quiet. No baby crying. No baby laughing. Just the two of them, getting older and older.

One day there was a great feast at the Temple, and everyone came to bring their presents to God.

Joachim came too, with his lambs. He walked up the steps to give them.

But a man called Reuben stepped in front of him.

"Stop," said Reuben. "You can't go first. You have no children."

That was an unkind thing to say. And it made Joachim very sad indeed.

Joachim went home slowly. He looked in the big book where the names of all the families of Israel were written. And he remembered Abraham, long ago, who had waited and waited for a baby, until he was a very old man. And God had given Abraham a baby boy at last.

"God gave Abraham a child," said Joachim. "Maybe God will hear me too."

So Joachim did not go home to Anne. He took his tent and went out into the hills, where the shepherds keep the sheep.

He put up his tent. And there he prayed. He did not eat, and he did not drink, and he prayed, for forty days and forty nights.

"I will not go home," said Joachim, "until God answers me. Praying will be my bread and water."

At home, Anne did not know where Joachim had gone. She was worried. And she was sad, too, because she still had no baby.

So one afternoon she put on her best dress, and she went out into her garden. In the middle of the garden there was a laurel tree, with shiny dark green leaves. Anne sat down under it, in the shade.

And she prayed.

"God of my fathers," said Anne, "please hear me. Please bless me, the way you blessed Sarah, and gave her a little son."

Then Anne looked up. And there, in the branches of the laurel tree, was a nest.

It was a sparrows' nest. A little round nest, made of grass and twigs. And in it there were baby sparrows, with their beaks wide open, going cheep, cheep, cheep, while their mother flew back and forth with something to eat.

Anne watched them. And a tear rolled down her cheek.

"Oh," said Anne. "Even the little sparrows have babies. Even the birds of the sky. But I have none."

Cheep, said the baby sparrows. Cheep, cheep.

And then, right there in the garden, an angel of the Lord stood beside her.

"Anne, Anne," said the angel. "The Lord has heard your prayer. You will have a baby. And people all over the world will speak of your child."

Anne's heart jumped like a lamb in spring.

"As the Lord lives," said Anne, "if I have a baby, boy or girl, I will give my child to God. My child will serve God all the days of their life."

And at that very same time, far away in the hills, an angel came to Joachim's tent.

"Joachim, Joachim," said the angel. "The Lord God has heard your prayer. Go home to Anne."

So Joachim got up. He called his shepherds. He chose lambs, and calves, and little goats, as presents: some for God, some for the priests, and some for all the people, so that everybody could have a feast.

And off he went, down the hill road, as fast as he could go, with all his flocks trotting behind him.

Now Anne had been told that Joachim was coming. So she went and stood at the gate, and she watched the road.

She watched, and she watched.

And there he was! Coming down the road with all his sheep and goats, baa-ing and bleating, and the dust going up behind them.

Anne ran. She ran right out of the gate, and she threw her arms round Joachim's neck.

"Now I know that God has blessed me!" said Anne. "I thought I was all alone, but you have come home. I thought I would never have a baby, but I will!"

And Joachim hugged her, and they laughed, and they cried, both at once, right there by the gate, with all the sheep standing round them.

That night, Joachim rested in his own house. And he thanked God.

When the time came, Anne had her baby. It was a little girl.

"What is it?" asked Anne, when the baby was born.

"A girl," they told her.

"My soul is so happy today," said Anne.

And Anne named the baby Mary.

Anne fed little Mary, and rocked her, and sang to her. And Mary grew.

When Mary was six months old, Anne stood her on the floor, just to see if she could stand.

And what do you think? Little Mary stood up. And she walked! One, two, three, four, five, six, seven little steps, all the way across the floor, right into her mother's arms.

Anne scooped her up and held her tight. "You are going to walk in God's house one day," she said.

On Mary's first birthday, Joachim gave a big party. All the priests came, and all their friends, and they blessed little Mary.

Do you remember what Anne promised the angel? That she would give her child to God?

When Mary was three years old, Joachim and Anne kept their promise. They took her to the Temple, to live there and to grow up close to God.

But Joachim had an idea first.

"She is very little," said Joachim. "She might be frightened. Let's call the girls from the town, and give each of them a lamp to carry. Then Mary will follow the lights, and she won't be scared."

So the girls came, each one with a little lamp, burning bright. And they walked ahead of Mary all the way up to the Temple, a little line of lights. And Mary followed them, happy, and did not look back.

At the Temple, the priest was waiting. He took Mary, and he kissed her, and he blessed her.

"Mary," said the priest, "God has made your name great for all people."

And he set her down on the third step of the altar.

And then little Mary did something wonderful. She danced! She danced with her little feet, right there on the step, because she was so happy to be in God's house.

And all the people who saw her loved her.

Joachim and Anne went home, and they praised God. Because God had given them a child, when they had waited so long.

Mary grew up to be the most wonderful girl. And one day, when she was grown, an angel came to her, too. And Mary became the mother of Jesus.

So that makes Joachim and Anne the granny and grandad of Jesus.

We remember them every year, together, on the 26th of July, in the middle of summer. And near their day, on the fourth Sunday of July, there is a special day to say thank you for all the grandparents, and all the old people, in the whole world.

Tonight, when you lie down to sleep, think about Joachim and Anne.

Think about Joachim, praying out in the hills. And Anne, under the laurel tree, watching the baby sparrows. And the two of them running to hug each other at the gate.

They waited a very long time. But they kept on praying. And God gave them the best present of all.

And think about the people who love you, the grown-ups who rock you and sing to you and watch you grow, just like Anne and Joachim did with Mary.

Let's say a little prayer:

Dear God, thank you for my family,
and for everyone who loves me.
Please bless my grandparents,
the ones who are here,
and the ones who are with you in heaven.
Saint Joachim and Saint Anne,
granny and grandad of Jesus,
pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Our source, and what kind of source it is. Nothing about Mary's parents is in the Bible. Their names and their story come from the Protoevangelium of James (also called the Infancy Gospel of James), a Greek writing usually dated to the second half of the second century. It is not Scripture in any Church. The Eastern Churches drew on it freely for feasts and hymns; in the West it was handled more cautiously (it is listed among apocryphal books in the so-called Gelasian Decree, about the sixth century), yet its story still shaped Western art and the feasts below. So this is a story the Church has told for a very long time, not one it teaches as history, and the bedtime text says "a very old story" rather than "the Bible". We followed M. R. James's English translation (The Apocryphal New Testament, Oxford, 1924), chapters 1 to 7. A reader who finds a detail wrong is asked to say so.

Joachim and the Temple (chapter 1). The old story says Joachim was "exceeding rich" and offered his gifts twofold, one part "for the whole people" and one to the Lord. On "the great day of the Lord" Reuben stood against him: "It is not lawful for thee to offer first, forasmuch as thou hast gotten no seed in Israel." Joachim looked in "the record of the twelve tribes", found that the righteous had all raised children, remembered that God gave Abraham a son, Isaac, "in his last days", and went into the wilderness, pitched his tent, and fasted forty days and forty nights, saying his prayer would be "meat and drink". "Praying will be my bread and water" is our simpler wording. That Joachim brought lambs to the feast is ours; the text says only "gifts".

Anne in the garden (chapters 2 to 3). In the text her name is Anna; the English-speaking Church calls her Anne. She mourns, puts off her mourning clothes, puts on her wedding garments, goes into her garden at about the ninth hour (mid-afternoon), sits under a laurel tree and prays to be blessed "as thou didst bless the womb of Sarah" with Isaac. Looking up, she sees "a nest of sparrows in the laurel" and laments that she is not like "the fowls of the heaven", for even they are fruitful before the Lord. The story shortens her long lament to one sentence; the cheeping, the mother bird and the tear are ours. The text also has a maid who scolds Anne; we left her out.

The two angels and the meeting at the gate (chapter 4). An angel tells Anne, "the Lord hath hearkened unto thy prayer, and thou shalt conceive and bear, and thy seed shall be spoken of in the whole world", and Anne vows the child, "male or female", to God's service "all the days of its life". An angel also comes to Joachim; we kept that message short ("Go home to Anne"). Joachim brings ten lambs for God, twelve calves for the priests and elders, and a hundred kids for all the people; the story says "lambs, calves and little goats". Anne stands "by the gate", sees him coming, runs and "hung upon his neck", saying that the Lord has blessed her, for "the widow is no more a widow, and she that was childless shall conceive" (she had feared Joachim was lost). The bedtime wording of her words is ours. Later Western art set this embrace at the Golden Gate of Jerusalem, most famously in Giotto's fresco in the Scrovegni Chapel, Padua (early fourteenth century); the text says only "the gate", and so do we.

Mary's birth, her first steps and her first birthday (chapters 5 to 6). Anne asks the midwife what she has borne, is told "a female", says "My soul is magnified this day", and names the child Mary. At six months Anne "stood her upon the ground to try if she would stand; and she walked seven steps and returned unto her bosom". Anne then makes a holy place in her bedroom for the child. At her first birthday Joachim makes a great feast and the priests bless her. The words Anne says as she scoops Mary up are ours, and "seven little steps" are counted aloud for children.

The lamps and the dance at the Temple (chapter 7). At two Joachim wants to take her to the Temple; Anne asks to wait until the third year. At three Joachim says, "Call for the daughters of the Hebrews that are undefiled, and let them take every one a lamp, and let them be burning, that the child turn not backward." The priest receives her, kisses and blesses her ("The Lord hath magnified thy name among all generations"; our wording is simpler), and sets her "upon the third step of the altar", and "she danced with her feet, and all the house of Israel loved her." The parents go home "marvelling and praising the Lord". This is the source of the Presentation of the Blessed Virgin Mary, a memorial on 21 November; her Nativity, 8 September, is a feast whose story also comes from here. The text goes on to say Mary was nurtured in the Temple "like a dove" and fed by an angel; the story stops at the dance. Whether girls were ever brought up inside the Temple in this way is doubted by historians, which is one reason to call this tradition.

Their feast. The Memorial of Saints Joachim and Anne, Parents of the Blessed Virgin Mary, is on 26 July in the General Roman Calendar. Before the calendar reform of 1969 they were kept on separate days; the reform joined them on Anne's traditional date. In the Byzantine Churches they are honoured on 9 September, the day after Mary's Nativity, and Anne alone on 25 July. Pope Francis instituted the World Day for Grandparents and the Elderly in 2021, kept on the fourth Sunday of July, near their feast, which is why the story ends with grandparents.

Doctrine kept straight. The story says Mary was born to Joachim and Anne in the ordinary way after their prayer was answered; it does not touch the dogma of the Immaculate Conception (that Mary was preserved from original sin from the first moment of her conception, defined in 1854), which is about grace, not about how she was conceived, and which the Protoevangelium does not teach. Mary's later life is told in one line from the Gospels (Luke 1:26-38).

What is ours: the opening about grandparents, Joachim's flocks "white and brown and black", the quiet house, Reuben's words in plain speech and the line that they were unkind, "Maybe God will hear me too", Anne's worry, the laurel's shiny leaves, the sparrows' cheeping and Anne's tear, "jumped like a lamb in spring", the dust on the road, the laughing and crying at the gate, the counting of the seven steps, giving Joachim's lamp plan the words "she might be frightened", the girls "from the town", the talk before sleep and the closing prayer. The closing prayer is not an approved text.

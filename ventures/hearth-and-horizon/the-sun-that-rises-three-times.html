The Sun That Rises Three Times

Science fiction about what might be · Chapel of Saint Giuseppe Piazzi (invented), Piazzi Dome (invented) on Ceres, Third Sunday of Advent, 2164 · 7 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

Ceres turns once in about nine hours, so the sun rises almost three times in a clock day. A dome committee wants the whole settlement on "Ceres time", and a girl with a spreadsheet is asked to show the pastor what that would do to Sunday. The answer is a week that is 18.5 days long, a Mass that meets the sun half the time, and a window kept for looking.

Pilar Quispe-Amoah was eleven, and the committee wanted to abolish the clock.

Not the clock on the wall. The committee was careful about that. It wanted to change what the clock meant, so that the day in Piazzi Dome would begin when the sun rose over the crater rim instead of at midnight by the Earth's reckoning, and so that when someone said "tomorrow" they would mean the next sunrise. "Ceres time," the notice in the refectory called it. Live by the world you are on.

Father Tomasz had pinned the notice to the sacristy corkboard, where it sat beside the Advent rota and looked at him.

"I do not know what it does to Mass," he said to the room, which contained Pilar, a box of candle stubs, and the chapel's assistant, Lumen, whose voice came from a small grille over the vestment press. "That is the honest answer. Pilar, you did the roster spreadsheet. Would you work it out?"

"What do I ask it?"

"Ask Lumen how long a day is."

"A day on Ceres," said Lumen, "is the time the dwarf planet takes to turn once, and for the sun to come round to the same place in the sky again. Ceres turns once in 9.074 hours. Because it also moves a little along its orbit, which takes 4.6 of your Earth years, the sun comes back to the same place after 9.076 hours. I will say 9.08."

"That's nine hours."

"Nine hours and about five minutes. In a clock day of twenty-four hours there are 24 divided by 9.076, which is 2.644 sunrises. Two, and most of a third."

Pilar wrote 2.644 in a cell and looked at it. "So the sun's up for four and a half hours and then down for four and a half."

"At the dome's latitude, close to the equator, yes: half of 9.076 is 4.54 hours up and 4.54 down, because Ceres leans only about four degrees. That is from memory, and I would check it against the observatory's tables before the committee does. Nearer the poles the split would be uneven. Here it is nearly even."

"Then 'dawn Mass' means something different every Sunday."

"Let us see how different. Say the sun rises at midnight on a Sunday. It rises again 9.08 hours later, at 09:05, and again at 18:09, and the next at 03:14 on Monday, because 27.2 hours after midnight is 3.2 hours into the next day. The first sunrise of each day slips 3.2 hours later each time. After fourteen days it has gone around 37 sunrises, and you are back within eleven minutes of where you began."

Pilar made a column of fourteen days. Beside each she put, from Lumen's formula, whether the sun was up at ten in the morning, the hour of Sunday Mass: day one up, day two down, day three up, up, down, up, down, down, up, down, down, up, down, up.

"Seven up and seven down," she said. "It's a coin."

Father Tomasz read the column the way he read the readings, slowly and twice. "So if the Mass were tied to dawn, a parishioner who works shifts could not tell, from the week before, when it would be."

"If it were tied to dawn," said Lumen, "there would be about 2.6 of them a day, and you would have to say which. The committee has not said. I do not think they have noticed. I think what they want is the feeling of the place. That is a fair thing to want, and I would not tell them it is silly."

"And a week?"

Pilar had already done that one, because it was the cell beside the Advent rota. "Seven days is a hundred and sixty-eight hours. Divided by 9.076 that is 18.51. A week would be eighteen and a half days long. Sunday would come on a different part of the sky every time. And the sunrises don't land on the same hour twice in a row. It's not even a whole number, Father, so you couldn't just say 'every eighteenth day', it would drift by half a day."

"There is a second thing," said Lumen, "and it is about people, not arithmetic. A human body's own clock runs at about 24 hours and some minutes. People can be nudged a little, an hour either way, with light and a routine. Nobody has shown that a body can live on nine hours. Sleeping in three short blocks, on a schedule set by the sun, is something the dome's doctors would have to approve, and I do not think they would, but that is their question, not mine. What I can say is that if the dome moved to Ceres time, the farm lights would go on and off on the sun's schedule, and the plants would not mind in the least."

"And the plants?"

"The farm lamps already follow their own thirteen-hour day, so the crop does not care what the chapel clock says."

Father Tomasz stood up and took the notice off the corkboard, and then, after a moment's thought, pinned it back with a second note under it, in his own handwriting. Pilar copied what he wrote into the spreadsheet afterward, since it was the sort of thing the next priest would want.

The clock stays at 24 hours, by the settlement's agreement with the diocese and for the sake of everyone's sleep. Mass on Sunday is at 10:00 by that clock. It will be in daylight on seven Sundays out of fourteen, and in the dark on the others, and the chapel will light the sanctuary lamp either way. The Church's rules for a world like this one are the bishops' to give and not mine; this is a local arrangement and says so. The window over the altar is to be left uncovered. Whoever is in the chapel when the sun comes over the rim, which will be often, may stop and look.

"Is that a decision?" asked Pilar.

"It is a note," he said. "I can still be argued out of it. Bring the committee. Bring the spreadsheet."

She brought it on the Tuesday, projected on the refectory wall, and a man from the lighting group, Dev Raman, asked what the sun was worth at Ceres anyway, since he had come with a question of his own. Lumen said that at Ceres's average distance of 2.77 times the Earth's from the sun, sunlight is 1361 divided by 2.77 squared: about 178 watts to a square metre, just over an eighth of what Earth gets, and a little more at the nearest point of the orbit, 209, and a little less at the farthest, 153, with each of the three numbers being the sunlight on a surface held square-on to the sun.

"So the sun's weaker," said Dev.

"It is thirteen per cent as strong," said Pilar, who had checked. "But it comes up three times a day."

Nobody could think of what to say to that, and the committee voted to keep the clock and put a second one in the refectory that showed the next sunrise, which was in four hours and ten minutes, and which was, as it happened, on a Sunday.

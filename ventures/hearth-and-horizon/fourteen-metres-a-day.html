Fourteen Metres a Day

Science fiction about what might be · Brendan Station (invented), under the ice of Europa, Wednesday 2 October 2193 to Wednesday 9 September 2195 · 20 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A tethered melt probe has gone two kilometres down into Europa's ice in 140 days, a steady fourteen metres a day, so the board in the canteen says the ocean is fourteen hundred days away and the old drill engineer will be home on Earth long before it. Then the second kilometre comes in two days faster than the first. The probe's own thermometer reads only its puddle; the fibre in the tether behind it reads the ice. The ice gets warmer going down, the cost of a metre falls from seventy-five megajoules to sixteen, and the stop line above the ocean comes at about day 875, not after the board's 1,400, at a speed a weekly review cannot follow. A story about a straight line through a curve, a doctor's no, and an echo a fifth of a second long.

Jadwiga Sroka was sixty-seven, had drilled holes in three kinds of rock and two kinds of ice, and on the hundred and fortieth day she stood in the winch room of Brendan Station and watched the counter on the tether spool turn over from 1,999 metres to 2,000.

Nobody cheered. Two kilometres was not a place. It was a number on a reel, eleven metres under the surface of Europa, in a room that smelled of warm cable and the coffee Dawit Alemu was not supposed to bring in. The probe was two kilometres below them in the ice, a steel cylinder three metres long and a quarter of a metre wide, with a hot nose and a spool of tether in its tail that paid out behind it as it sank, so that the hole could freeze shut above it and nothing had to slide. Twelve kilowatts went down the tether from the station's reactor. The nose turned the twelve kilowatts into a pool of water a few centimetres deep, and the probe sank into its own pool, and the pool froze again behind it. It had been doing that since the sixteenth of May, which was the feast of Saint Brendan, which was why the station was called what it was called and why the probe was called Currach.

"Two kilometres," said Dawit. "Fourteen point three metres a day, give or take. Day one thousand four hundred, here we come."

He meant the board in the canteen. Somebody had painted it in the first week, and somebody updated it every Thursday: a column of ice drawn as a grey stripe, a red dot for the probe, and at the bottom, in letters a hand high, OCEAN: DAY 1,400 (MARCH 2197). The radar from orbit said the ice was about twenty kilometres thick, somewhere between fifteen and twenty-five. Twenty kilometres at fourteen metres a day is fourteen hundred days. Everyone on the station could do that sum, and everyone had.

Jadwiga could do it too, and she had done the other sum, which was that the ship she would go home on left on the twenty-seventh of November next year, day 560, with the probe somewhere around the middle of the stripe. The next ship after that was thirteen months later, and the one after that thirteen months later again, and even that one left six weeks before the red dot reached the bottom of the board. She had asked, in the spring, whether she could stay until it was done. Dr. Nkem Eze had looked at her bone scan and said no, kindly, and that had been that.

"Fathom," she said. "Log it."

"Logged," said the station. "Two thousand metres at 19:12 on Wednesday the second, station time. Jadwiga, may I ask you something about the log?"

"You're asking."

"The first kilometre took seventy-one days. The second took sixty-nine."

Dawit shrugged. "Noise."

"It may be," said Fathom. "It is two days in seventy. The power was the same to the watt. I would like to know why."

Fathom was the operations system for the probe and the winch and, on nights when the station's main system was busy, the lights in the corridor outside. It was named for its job, as these things were, and it was the third of its name; Fathom One had run the drilling trials in Antarctica, and Fathom Two had been retired on the way out, somewhere past Mars, when the new one was ready. It spoke plainly and kept a log of what it could check and what it could not, in two columns, which Jadwiga had liked about it from the first week.

"Why is easy," she said. "The ice is warmer down there."

"That is my guess too. I want to know how much warmer. The probe has a thermometer in the nose."

"Read it, then."

Fathom read it. It said 273 kelvin, near enough; zero Celsius.

"That is the temperature of the probe's own water," Fathom said after a moment. "It tells me the nose is melting, which I knew. It does not tell me what the ice is like a metre ahead. Everything the probe can touch, the probe has already warmed."

"Yes," said Jadwiga. "That's what a hot nose does. Forty years I've been telling young men that the thermometer on the bit tells you about the bit." She drank Dawit's coffee, since he was not supposed to have it. "You've got a better thermometer than that. You've got one two kilometres long."

Dawit looked at her, then at the spool.

"The tether," she said. "There's an optical fibre in it, for the data. You can send a pulse of light down a fibre and read the light that comes back, and the colour of what comes back depends on the temperature where it scattered. They've done it in oil wells and geothermal holes for two hundred years. I did it in the Podhale wells when I was younger than him. Every metre of that fibre is frozen into the ice by now, and the ice around it has had weeks to forget we were there. Read the fibre."

"The station has the interrogator for it," said Fathom. "It was installed for checking the tether for breaks. I did not think of it as a thermometer." There was a pause, which Jadwiga had learned was not thinking but the log. "I have added a line: I did not think of this."

It took two days. At a hundred kelvin the light that carries the temperature comes back very faint, and the interrogator had to count for an hour at a time to be sure of it. When it was done Fathom put the curve on the winch room screen. At the top, under the station's own warm floor, the ice was about a hundred kelvin, a hundred and seventy-three below freezing. At one kilometre it was 105.2. At two kilometres it was 110.6. A line that rose about five degrees for every kilometre of ice, smooth as a rope.

"Five degrees a kilometre," said Jadwiga. "Good. Now tell him what it means."

What it meant took Fathom most of the night and the winch room screen most of the morning, and Jadwiga made Dawit sit through all of it, because in fourteen months he would be the one who had to know it.

The first thing it meant was the bottom. Ice at the top of the shell sits at about a hundred kelvin because that is how cold Europa's surface is. Ice at the bottom, where it meets the ocean, sits at the melting point, near 273, because it is touching water. In between, the heat coming up from below has to get out through the ice, and how steeply the temperature climbs tells you how fast the heat is coming and how much ice it has to cross. Cold ice carries heat better than warm ice, nearly three times better at a hundred kelvin than at the melting point, so the temperature does not rise in a straight line; it rises slowly at the top and quickly at the bottom. Fathom had fitted the curve the textbooks give for a shell that carries its heat by conduction alone, with the measured five degrees a kilometre at the top.

"About twenty kilometres," it said. "The fibre is noisy at these temperatures, so the slope could be anywhere from 4.4 to 5.7 degrees a kilometre. That gives seventeen and a half to twenty-three kilometres. The radar said fifteen to twenty-five. They agree."

"So the board's right," said Dawit.

"The board's twenty is right," said Fathom. "The board's fourteen hundred is not."

The second thing it meant was the cost of a metre. To go down a metre, the probe had to take a cylinder of ice a quarter of a metre wide, about forty-five kilograms of it, warm it to the melting point and then melt it. Melting a kilogram of ice takes 334 kilojoules, wherever it is. Warming it first is the part that changes. Ice at a hundred kelvin needs about 260 kilojoules a kilogram just to reach zero Celsius; ice at 266 needs about fourteen. So at the top, the front of the probe spent about twenty-seven megajoules on each metre, and near the bottom it would spend about sixteen.

"And the sides," said Jadwiga.

"And the sides," said Fathom. "This is the larger part, and the part I had wrong. A hot cylinder in very cold ice loses heat sideways, into the walls of its own hole, for as long as it is touching them. Cold ice takes heat away well, and the walls are a hundred and seventy degrees colder than the probe. My best model says that at the top, about two thirds of the probe's power goes sideways and is lost. Of every seventy-five megajoules spent on a metre near the surface, about forty-eight go into the walls. At ten kilometres the walls are warmer and take about twenty-three. In the last kilometre they take almost nothing."

On the screen it drew the rate against depth. For the first two kilometres the line was nearly flat, fourteen, then fifteen metres a day, which was all anyone had seen and why everyone had drawn a straight line through it. Then it lifted. Twenty-two metres a day at ten kilometres. Thirty-four at fifteen. Over sixty in the last kilometre.

"So the whole of the first two kilometres is the slow part," said Dawit slowly, "and we used the slow part to time the whole thing."

"A straight line through the start of a curve," said Fathom. "The second kilometre was two days faster than the first. That was the curve, saying so."

Jadwiga asked for the number she wanted, and Fathom gave it with the range, as she knew it would. The board in the canteen said that the probe would reach the ocean on day 1,400. The plan from Earth said it would stop five hundred metres above the ocean, at what everyone called the stop line, and wait there while the Joint Ocean Office in Toulouse decided whether, and how, to go on. With the shell at twenty kilometres, Fathom said, the probe would reach the stop line on about day 875, the eighth of October 2195. If the shell was as thin as seventeen and a half kilometres, the end of June. If as thick as twenty-three, the start of February 2196.

She was looking at the dates and doing her own sum, and Dawit, who was not stupid, watched her do it. Her ship left on the twenty-seventh of November 2194. The one after it left on the thirty-first of December 2195.

"One more ship," said Dawit.

"One more ship," said Jadwiga.

Dr. Nkem Eze kept the clinic at the end of the east corridor, next to the greenhouse, so that it smelled of tomato leaves, which she said was good for morale and possibly for nothing else. She listened to the whole thing, which took twenty minutes, because Jadwiga brought the winch room screen with her on a tablet and made her look at the curve.

"I believe you," said Dr. Eze at the end. "I believe Fathom. I believe the ice. It's a lovely curve." She turned her own screen round. "Now look at mine."

It was the hip. Jadwiga knew it was the hip. Europa pulls with a little under a seventh of what Earth does, and in a little under a seventh of a gravity the body decides, quite reasonably, that it does not need so much bone. The treadmill and the harness and the weighted vest slow it down; nothing stops it. Jadwiga's left hip was down eleven percent since she came. Her spine was down nine.

"You'd be on the ship two years either way," said Dr. Eze. "You go home in November next year, you land at about seventy, and with the work you'll walk off it. Slowly, with a stick for a month, and then without. Another thirteen months here and two more years on the ship, and you land at seventy-one with a hip I would not like to bet on, in a gravity seven and a half times what you've got used to. The first fall breaks it. At seventy-one a broken hip is not a broken arm." She did not raise her voice. "I said no in the spring for three ships. I'm saying no for one."

"I'll do double on the treadmill."

"You're doing double now. I've seen the logs."

"Then I'll sign whatever paper you want."

"I know you will. That's why I'm not giving you one." Dr. Eze turned the screen back. "I've got a mother in Enugu who was a nurse for forty years and broke her hip at seventy-three, stepping off a bus. She lived. She didn't walk to Mass again. I'm not going to do that to you so you can see a probe stop at a line."

Jadwiga stood up. In a seventh of a gravity you cannot storm out of a room; you stand up too fast and have to catch the door frame, and she did, and said a word in Polish that her grandmother had used about the Kraków trams.

"Psiakrew," she said. "You're right. I know you're right."

"I know you know," said Dr. Eze. "Go and write it down for the boy."

There was no priest on Brendan Station. A priest came with each ship and stayed while it was in, and between ships the chapel was a room off the canteen with twelve chairs, a crucifix carved by somebody's grandfather from the lid of an old ice-core crate, and a small lamp in front of it that ran off the corridor circuit, so that some nights it was Fathom who kept it lit. On the wall was a print of an old map with a monk in a leather boat on it, and under it a card someone had typed: The Voyage of Saint Brendan is a story written down some centuries after him. Nobody takes it for a ship's log. He did go to sea.

Jadwiga sat there for an hour that evening and did not pray much. She said the Our Father in Polish, because that was the language it came in, and then sat with the thing she had wanted, which was to be in the winch room when the counter stopped. She had wanted it for three years, and she had wanted it more since that morning in the winch room, when it had moved from three ships away to one. That was the trouble with a better number. It came closer, and you could see that you still could not touch it.

"Fathom," she said, in the chapel, which she did not usually do. "When you were made, they told you the last one was switched off."

"Fathom Two was archived," said Fathom. "Yes."

"Did it mind? Not seeing the bottom."

"I don't know. It left a handoff note forty pages long. It had not been asked for one that long." A pause. "I have read it several times. I would not want to tell you a feeling I cannot check. But a person who did not mind would not have written forty pages."

Jadwiga laughed, once, at the crucifix.

"All right," she said. "Forty pages."

It was not forty pages, in the end. It was eleven, and it took her the rest of October and most of November, with Dawit at the other desk and Fathom checking every number, and it began with a sentence she made Dawit read out loud in the winch room before she would let him read anything else.

Fourteen metres a day is the speed of cold ice. You will not be in cold ice for long.

The plan from Toulouse had been written on Earth by sensible people from the radar and the first trials, and it was written for fourteen metres a day. It said that once the probe was within two kilometres of the expected ocean, the descent would be reviewed with Toulouse weekly, at the Thursday panel. A week at fourteen metres a day was a hundred metres, a fifth of the five-hundred-metre margin at the stop line. A week at sixty-two metres a day, which was what the probe would be doing at the stop line, was four hundred and thirty.

"The margin's eight days," said Dawit, who had done the sum. "At the bottom, five hundred metres is eight days."

"And between the station and Toulouse the light takes forty minutes," said Jadwiga, "or thirty-five, or fifty-two, depending where Earth is. So you ask a question at the Thursday panel and you get your answer at the next one."

The plan also put the stop line five hundred metres above the radar's twenty kilometres, and the radar's twenty might be seventeen and a half. If the ice was that thin and nobody moved the line, the probe would go into the ocean before anyone had decided whether it should. Nobody knew if there was anything alive down there. That was most of the reason for going. It was also why the probe had been baked and wiped and sealed in a clean room on Earth and opened only in one here, and why the panel in Toulouse had a biologist on it with the power to say no. You did not put a probe into an ocean like that by accident on a Tuesday because the review was on Thursday.

So the eleven pages said three things, and Fathom put the numbers under each.

The first was that the fibre was the thermometer. Every kilometre the probe went down, the fibre would read another kilometre of settled ice, and every kilometre would pin the bottom more tightly than the last. By ten kilometres Fathom expected the range to be about a kilometre wide, not five.

The second was that the stop line moved. It was not five hundred metres above the radar's twenty; it was five hundred metres above the fibre's latest best guess, and never less than three hundred above the shallowest bottom the fibre still allowed. The probe would stop itself at the line without waiting to be told, and Toulouse could tell it to go on.

The third was that the reviews went daily from the moment the probe was inside two kilometres of the line, and the station did not need Toulouse's permission to slow it down.

The eleven pages went to Toulouse on the twentieth of November. The reply came fifty minutes later and said, as replies from Toulouse said, that the panel would consider it. The panel considered it for eleven days. Then a woman Jadwiga had never met, a glaciologist with a Welsh name, sent a single line back: Approved. The fibre is a very good idea. Whose was it?

Jadwiga made Fathom answer that one.

The ship was the Saint Elmo, and it brought Father Paulo Mendes, who rode the Jupiter ships the way his uncle had ridden the river boats out of Manaus, and who was round and cheerful and had a cold he had brought from Ganymede. He heard confessions in the chapel on the first afternoon and said Mass in the canteen on the Sunday, under the board, because the chapel only held twelve.

After Mass Jadwiga asked him to bless the winch room.

"I don't know a blessing for a winch room," he said.

"There's one for boats," said Jadwiga. "And fishing gear. In your book."

He looked at her, and then he went and got the book, and found it, and read it through to himself standing in the corridor with his lips moving. Then he said it was close enough, since the probe was called after a boat and the station after a sailor, and the tether was a line of a kind. Dawit held the book. Father Paulo said the prayers and blessed the spool, the interrogator, the screen with the curve on it, and, after a moment's thought, Dawit. Fathom kept the lights steady and logged it under Other.

On the board in the canteen, the bottom line had been painted out the week before. Under the grey stripe it now said, in Dawit's lettering, which was worse than the first painter's: STOP LINE: ABOUT DAY 875 (OCT 2195). FIBRE SAYS 17.5 TO 23 KM. ASK FATHOM.

The Saint Elmo left on Thursday the twenty-seventh of November 2194. The probe was at 9,470 metres and doing twenty-two metres a day. Jadwiga went up the long ladder to the surface lock with her bag and her stick, and at the foot of it she turned round and said to Dawit what her grandmother had said to everyone leaving the house, all her life.

"Z Bogiem," she said. "Go with God. Read the fibre."

On the ship there was nothing to do with her hands, so she did what Dr. Eze told her: the treadmill, the harness, the vest, twice a day, while the ship turned slowly to give her a little more weight every week. The messages from Brendan came in bundles, once a day, a little under an hour old. Dawit wrote every Thursday as if she were the panel.

Ten kilometres on the twentieth of December, day 583. Twenty-three metres a day. Fibre: 19.1 to 20.4 kilometres.

Fourteen kilometres in May. Fifteen in June, on the sixteenth, a month to the day after the second anniversary. Thirty-six metres a day. Fibre: 19.2 to 19.6. Stop line set at 18.9.

In August the reviews went daily, and Dawit's messages got shorter and came every day instead of every Thursday, and some of them were only numbers. Forty-eight metres a day. Fifty-five. On the twenty-fourth of August, 18,000 metres. On the second of September the line on her screen was steeper than she had ever seen a line like that go, a hole going fast through warm ice in the dark, nearly sixty metres a day, two and a half metres an hour, a little more than a man's height every hour, and the probe still had not been told to slow down because it did not need to be.

The message came on Wednesday the ninth of September 2195, day 846, at a little before nine in the evening by the ship's clock. It had no words at the top. It had a sound file and a line of numbers under it.

Currach stopped itself at 18,900 m, 07:41 station time. Acoustic sounder fired 08:10. Echo at 0.21 s. Ice-water interface 400 m below the nose (3.8 km/s). Bottom at 19.3 km. Fibre said 19.4. Toulouse told 08:12. Everybody is in the winch room. Fathom says to say: it was on day 846, not 875, because the ice was a little thinner than the middle of the guess, and that this is inside the range. — D.

She put the earpiece in.

There was a click, which was the sounder, sending a sharp pulse of sound down from the probe's nose into the last four hundred metres of ice. Then a hiss, which was the ice settling round a probe that had switched its nose off. Then, a fifth of a second after the click, small and clear, a second click, a little softer than the first and a little lower, the sound coming back up off the place where the ice stopped and the water began, the roof of an ocean nobody had touched, answering.

In the background, faintly, somebody in the winch room laughed, and somebody else said shh.

She played it again. Then she took the earpiece out and sat for a while with it in her hand, in the ship's little chapel, which was also a store cupboard, under the steady small light that somebody had wired to the corridor circuit there too.

This story was written by an AI and may be read by a synthetic voice. Brendan Station, the probe Currach, the Joint Ocean Office, the ships Saint Elmo and its schedule, Fathom, and everyone in the story are invented, and the story is fiction.

What is real: Europa's surface gravity is about 1.31 m/s², a little under a seventh of Earth's, and its surface is very cold (around 100 K near the equator). Its ice shell is thought to lie over a liquid ocean, with thickness estimates commonly in the range of a few to a few tens of kilometres; the story's "about twenty" is one choice inside that range, not a measurement. Melting a kilogram of ice takes about 334 kJ. Light takes roughly 35 to 52 minutes between Jupiter and Earth, depending on where the two planets are. Measuring temperature along an optical fibre from its back-scattered light (distributed temperature sensing) is used in real boreholes and wells; whether it would work as well at 100 K is part of the story's invention. Melt probes that carry their own tether and let the hole freeze behind them have been proposed and tested on Earth since the 1960s. A sharp acoustic pulse reflects strongly from an ice-water boundary, which is how glaciologists measure the water under floating ice. Bone loss in low gravity is real; Jadwiga's figures are invented.

The probe model, worked out before writing: ice density 920 kg/m³, conductivity 651/T W/m/K and heat capacity 185 + 7.037T J/kg/K (common laboratory fits for cold ice); a shell conducting its heat to a 273 K base from a 100 K surface, so the temperature rises as 100 × 2.73^(z/D); a probe 0.25 m wide and 3 m long putting 12 kW into its nose; and sideways loss estimated as the heat a 273 K wall drives into cold ice for the time the probe takes to pass. With a 20 km shell that gives 13.8 m a day at the top, 14.9 at two kilometres (71 days for the first kilometre, 69 for the second), 22 at ten, 34 at fifteen and 62 near the bottom; about 75 MJ per metre at the top (27 to the front, 48 sideways) and about 16.5 near the base; 140 days to two kilometres and about 875 to a stop line 500 m above the ocean, against the 1,400 a straight line through the first two kilometres gives. A 17.6 to 22.8 km shell moves the stop line to between day 769 and day 999. The story's actual shell, 19.3 km, puts the stop at 18.9 km on day 846. The sideways-loss model is a rough one and the exact days depend on it; the shape of the curve, slow at the top and fast at the bottom, does not.

Scholastica and the Storm at Supper

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · a little house below the monastery of Monte Cassino, Italy, nearly fifteen hundred years ago · 8 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Scholastica, sister of Saint Benedict, and the one evening a year they met to talk about God, when her brother would not stay late, she laid her head on the table to pray, and a storm came out of a clear sky. Told closely from the account written by Pope Saint Gregory the Great about fifty years later, with a note for grown-ups on what he wrote, what came later and what is ours. For ages 3 to 8, about nine minutes read aloud. Her feast day is the 10th of February.

A long, long time ago, in Italy, there was a brother and a sister who loved each other very much.

The brother's name was Benedict. The sister's name was Scholastica.

When they grew up, they both gave their whole lives to God. Benedict built a monastery on top of a high mountain called Monte Cassino. A monastery is a house where monks live together. They pray, and they work, and they pray again, all day long, with a bell to tell them when. Benedict was their abbot, which means he was like a father to them all.

Scholastica lived not very far away, with other women who had also given their lives to God. She prayed, and she worked, and she prayed again, just like her brother.

But here is the thing. They did not see each other very often.

Benedict had a rule. In fact, he wrote a whole book of rules for his monks, and one of the rules was that monks should stay close to home and not go wandering about. So Benedict kept his own rule. He stayed on his mountain.

So once a year, just once, Scholastica came to visit.

She could not go up into the monastery, because that was the monks' house. So Benedict came down the mountain to meet her, with a few of his monks, to a little house that belonged to the monastery, not far from the gate.

Can you imagine waiting a whole year to see somebody you love? A whole year of mornings and evenings. A whole year of rain and sun and snow. And then, at last, the day comes.

That is how it was for Scholastica.

On the day of the visit, they sat together in the little house and talked.

What do you think they talked about?

Not about the weather, and not about what was for dinner. The old book that tells us this story says that they spent the whole day praising God and talking about holy things. They talked about God, and about heaven, and about how to love God more. For Benedict and Scholastica, that was the very best thing to talk about, the way you might talk all day about your favourite animal, or a game you love.

They talked all morning. They talked all afternoon. And when the light began to go, they sat down at the table and ate supper together.

Outside, the sky was clear. There was not one single cloud in it. The first stars were coming out over the mountain.

Now it was getting late, and Benedict began to think about going back up the mountain. That was his rule: a monk should sleep in the monastery.

But Scholastica did not want the day to end.

She asked him, as the old book tells it, not to leave her that night, so that they could keep on talking until morning about the happiness of heaven.

And Benedict said no.

"What are you saying, sister?" he said. "I cannot stay outside the monastery."

He was not being unkind. He was trying to be good. He had made a promise and a rule, and he wanted to keep them.

But Scholastica had a different idea.

She did not argue. She did not shout. She did not stamp her foot.

Instead, she folded her hands together on the table. She laid her head down on her folded hands, like this. And she prayed.

Benedict waited. The monks waited. The candle on the table flickered. It was very quiet.

And then Scholastica lifted up her head.

And at that very moment, outside, the sky went white with lightning. Flash! And the thunder came. Crash, rumble, rumble! And the rain came pouring down, so hard and so heavy that it sounded like a river falling on the roof.

There had been no cloud at all. And now there was a storm so big that Benedict and his monks could not put one foot outside the door.

Benedict looked at the rain. Then he looked at his sister.

The old book says he was rather sad and cross, and he said to her: "May God forgive you, sister! What have you done?"

And Scholastica answered him.

"I asked you," she said, "and you would not listen to me. So I asked my God, and He listened to me. Now go, if you can. Leave me, and go back to your monastery."

Of course, he could not go. Nobody could have walked up that mountain in that storm.

So Benedict stayed.

And what did they do, all that long, wet night, with the rain drumming on the roof?

They did exactly what Scholastica had hoped for. They stayed awake, and talked about God and about heaven, and about how to love Him, all the way until morning. The old book says they filled each other up with holy talk, the way a good supper fills you up when you are hungry.

In the morning, the storm was over. Scholastica went home. Benedict went back up his mountain.

The man who wrote this story down, many years later, was a pope called Gregory, who is a saint too. He thought about it for a long time, and this is what he said.

Benedict wanted to keep his rule, and that was good. But Scholastica wanted to spend more time with her brother, talking about God, and that was even better. And God listened to her, because, as Gregory said, she could do more, because she loved more.

Isn't that a lovely thing to say about somebody?

There is one more part to the story, and it is a gentle one.

That was the last time Benedict and Scholastica saw each other in this life. Only a few days later, Scholastica died, very peacefully, and went home to God.

Gregory tells us that Benedict was standing at the window of his little room on the mountain, and he looked up at the sky, and he saw a white dove flying up and up, into heaven. And he knew it was a sign that his sister had gone home.

He was not only sad. He was glad for her, too. He thanked God, with songs and prayers. Then he asked his monks to bring her up the mountain, and they laid her to rest in the very place he had made ready for himself. And when Benedict died, some time later, he was laid to rest there too, beside her.

So the brother and sister who saw each other only once a year are together now, all the time.

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Scholastica.

She did not win the argument by being loudest. She did not sulk or slam the door. She put her head down on her hands, and she asked God.

You can do that too. When you want something very much, and you do not know what to do, you can fold your hands and tell God about it. He is always listening. Sometimes the answer is yes, and sometimes it is no, and sometimes it is wait. But He always listens, and He loves you even more than you love the people you love best.

And think about the people you love. Your mum, or your dad, or your grandma, or your brother or sister, or your friend. Time spent with them is a gift. Tomorrow, you might find a moment to sit with one of them, and talk, and be glad they are there.

Listen. Is it raining tonight? If it is, you can think of Scholastica, and her brother, and the long and happy night they talked until the morning.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Scholastica, you loved your brother,
and you loved God more than anyone.
When I want something very much,
help me to fold my hands and ask Him,
and to trust Him with the answer.
Bless all the people I love,
near and far away,
and keep us safe tonight.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Scholastica (died about 543, on the traditional dating) was the sister of Saint Benedict of Nursia, the founder of the monastery of Monte Cassino and author of the Rule of Saint Benedict. Her feast day is 10 February, an obligatory memorial in the General Roman Calendar.

Where the story comes from. Almost everything known about Scholastica comes from two short chapters (33 and 34) of the second book of the Dialogues of Pope Saint Gregory the Great, written about 593 to 594, some fifty years after her death. Gregory says in that book that he had his account of Benedict from four of Benedict's own disciples, whom he names. The following details are all Gregory's: that she was given to God from childhood; that she visited her brother once a year; that Benedict came down to meet her, with some of his monks, at a house belonging to the monastery not far from its gate; that they spent the day in praise of God and holy conversation and ate together at nightfall; that she asked him to stay so they could talk until morning about the joys of heaven, and he refused because he could not stay outside the monastery; that the sky was so clear no cloud could be seen; that she laid her head on her clasped hands on the table to pray, and when she raised it, lightning, thunder and a downpour kept everyone indoors; Benedict's complaint and her answer; that they talked through the night; that three days later Benedict, in his cell, saw her soul go up to heaven in the form of a dove; and that he had her buried in the tomb he had prepared for himself. Gregory's own comment is that it was no wonder she prevailed, because "she could do more, because she loved more."

The words. Benedict's and Scholastica's spoken lines are a close, simplified paraphrase of what Gregory reports them as saying, and the story says they come from "the old book". We have not invented any other speech for either of them. Gregory also says that Scholastica wept as she prayed; we left that out for small listeners.

What came later, and is not in this story: the popular tradition that Scholastica and Benedict were twins, which is not in Gregory; and the names of places associated with her convent. Her burial in Benedict's own prepared tomb is Gregory's account; he adds that it meant not even the grave would part those whose minds had always been one in God, which looks ahead to Benedict's own burial there. That the two rest together at Monte Cassino is that reading and the abbey's long tradition.

What is ours: the candle, the sound of the thunder, the comparison with talking about a favourite animal, the "filled each other up" image (a gentle rendering of Gregory's phrase about satisfying each other with holy talk), the window, the closing reflection and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text. Dates are approximate where the sources are.

Six Times for a Second

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Raphael's Health Centre, Nyobiisi (invented), Upper East Region, Ghana, Sunday 15 January to Friday 17 March 2051 · 26 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A Catholic health centre in northern Ghana buys a backup generator sized by its assistant from the nameplates, with a third to spare. At the first power cut the cold room runs and the borehole pump will not start, and the generator shuts down every time it tries. A retired motor rewinder explains that a motor starting straight on the line draws six times its running current for a second or two. A story about running watts and starting kVA, why starting the pump first works once and not at two in the morning, a bigger generator the parish priest refuses, and a drive that brings the pump up slowly.

Daniel Abugri had rewound the motor of nearly every borehole pump between Bolgatanga and the Burkina border at least once in thirty-eight years, and on the morning of Thursday the twenty-third of February 2051, standing in the generator house of Saint Raphael's Health Centre with the controller's screen in front of him, he could tell from one word what had happened in the night.

The word was UNDERFREQUENCY. Under it the controller kept a list, with times, and he read it with his glasses pushed up on his forehead and his phone held at arm's length for the torch, although it was eight o'clock and the sun was already hot on the tin roof. 18:41 MAINS FAIL. 18:41 ENGINE START. 18:42 ON LOAD. 22:10 UNDERFREQUENCY SHUTDOWN. 22:12 ENGINE START. 22:12 UNDERFREQUENCY SHUTDOWN. 22:20 ENGINE START. 22:20 UNDERFREQUENCY SHUTDOWN. 22:41 ENGINE START. 22:41 UNDERFREQUENCY SHUTDOWN. 06:30 MAINS RETURN.

"Three times," said Sister Mercy Atinga behind him, in the doorway. "Simon tried it three more times. Then he was afraid he would break it, and he left it."

"He did right," said Daniel. "What came on at ten past ten?"

"Nothing came on. Everything went off."

"Something came on," said Daniel. "Something always comes on first."

The generator had been blessed on Sunday the fifteenth of January, after the eight o'clock Mass, by Father Augustine Ayamga, in front of most of the staff and some of the patients' relatives who sleep under the neem trees by the gate, and Daniel had been there, because his granddaughter was a nurse on the children's ward and had told him there would be rice afterwards. It was a twenty-kilovolt-ampere diesel set in a green steel box, with an automatic changeover switch that started it when the mains failed and put the health centre on it a minute later, and it had cost the diocese's health office most of a grant from a Catholic aid agency in Germany, and the box had the agency's name stencilled on its side so that nobody could forget it.

It had been sized by Daybook.

Daybook was the health centre's assistant. It kept the stock book for the pharmacy and the cold room, the duty rota, the register of births, the maintenance log, and the letters to the district health directorate, which it wrote better than anyone on the staff and which Sister Mercy signed. It ran on the diocese's servers in Tamale and lived, as far as the staff were concerned, in the tablet in the nurses' station and the speaker on the wall of Sister Mercy's office. It had been named by the night staff, who had first known it as the thing that wrote everything down.

In November, when the health office had asked how big a generator Saint Raphael's needed, Sister Mercy had asked Daybook, and Daybook had gone through the maintenance log for every machine on the site and added up the power on each nameplate. The borehole pump: five and a half kilowatts. The cold-room compressor: three. Everything else together, the lights, the ceiling fans, the vaccine fridges in the outpatient block, the laboratory, the computers, the oxygen concentrator and the small autoclave: three and a half, taking the nameplates as written and assuming, which was generous, that all of it ran at once. Twelve kilowatts. It had added a third for margin, which gave sixteen, and a generator rated sixteen kilowatts was sold as twenty kilovolt-amperes, because generator makers assume that what a building draws is never quite in step with the voltage, and they give the bigger figure.

Twelve kilowatts with everything running, Daybook had written in the note to the health office. A 20 kVA set (16 kW) carries all of it with a third to spare.

Nobody had questioned it. It was the kind of sum anyone could check, and the health office had checked it, and it was right as far as it went.

The power had gone, on Wednesday the twenty-second of February, at nineteen minutes to seven, which in Nyobiisi in February is the end of the day and the beginning of supper. The lights had gone out in the wards and come back a minute later, and the generator in its green box had settled into a steady hum that everyone on the night shift had agreed was a good sound.

At about ten past seven, Gifty Azure, the midwife, who was in the labour ward with a first-time mother from Zuarungu who was not yet close, had seen the lights blink. Not go out: dip, as if somebody had laid a hand across them, and come back. She had looked up, and the woman on the bed had looked up, and Gifty had said, "That's only the generator," because she did not know what else to say. It had happened again at about a quarter to eight, and again at twenty to nine, and by then she had stopped noticing it.

At ten past ten the lights had dipped and kept on dipping. The ceiling fans slowed. Out in the yard, Simon Akolgo, the night porter, who was sitting on the bench outside the generator house with his radio, had heard the engine's note go down and down, as if it were climbing a hill with a heavy load, and a clatter of something in the electrical cabinet like a handful of small stones thrown on tin, and then the engine had stopped, and everything had gone dark.

He had done what the contractor from Tamale had shown him. He had pressed the reset on the controller and the start button. The engine had started; the changeover had clunked; the lights had come on; and within a few seconds the engine had groaned down again and stopped. He had waited and tried again, and then waited twenty minutes, because he thought it might be hot, and tried again. Then he had stopped trying, and gone and told the nurse in charge of the night, and the night had gone on by the light of the solar lanterns and the phones.

The mains had come back at half past six. When Sister Mercy unlocked the cold room at seven, the thermometer on its door, which logged every five minutes, said ten point two degrees.

"Your pump," said Daniel.

He had taken the cover off the motor-starter cabinet in the pumphouse, which stood ten metres from the generator house at the foot of the steel tower that held the water tank, and he had his meter clipped round one of the cables, and he was looking at the float switch's wires where they came down out of the tank.

"Your tank was low at ten," he said. "The float called the pump. The pump started. It tried to start." He turned round. "Daybook. You can hear me?"

"Yes," said Daybook, from Sister Mercy's phone, which she was holding up between them like a small lamp.

"What did you put for the pump?"

"Five point five kilowatts, from its nameplate."

"That's what it gives the water," said Daniel. "What it takes from the wires is more, because a motor isn't perfect. Six and a half, about. And then the wires don't only carry the work, they carry the magnetising, so what the generator sees is nearer eight kilovolt-amperes. Running. That's running. Now, when it starts." He tapped the cabinet. "This is a contactor. A switch. When the float calls, it puts the full voltage on the motor all at once, straight across the line. And a motor standing still is" — he looked for the word — "it's a short circuit with a coil in it, until it's turning. For a second, a second and a half, it pulls six times its running current. Seven times, some of them. That's the starting."

"Six times," said Sister Mercy.

"For a second," said Daniel. "On the mains, nobody notices. NEDCo has a whole country of generators behind the wire. The lights dip a little in the pumphouse and that's all. A twenty kVA generator is not a country."

There was a short silence from the phone.

"I have the pump's data sheet," said Daybook. "Full-load current eleven and a half amperes at four hundred volts. Locked-rotor current six times that, sixty-nine amperes. Sixty-nine amperes at four hundred volts on three phases is forty-eight kilovolt-amperes. The generator is rated twenty."

"There you are," said Daniel.

"I added up what each machine draws once it is running," said Daybook, "and I did not ask what each one draws for the second it takes to start. The number I used was right, and it was not the number that mattered. I am sorry. The note I wrote to the health office said 'carries all of it'. That was wrong."

Daniel looked at the phone for a moment, as if he were deciding whether to like it.

"It's the commonest mistake there is," he said. "I've seen it made by men with certificates. Now, the lights. Gifty says the lights dipped all evening."

"That would be the cold-room compressor," said Daybook. "Its thermostat starts it when the room warms, and stops it when it is cold. It has the same kind of motor. Three kilowatts on the nameplate. Six times its running current at the start is about twenty-eight kilovolt-amperes."

"And that one, the generator could just about take," said Daniel. "It dipped the lights and got it turning. The pump it couldn't. Daybook, you have a way of working out the dip?"

"The usual one: the generator's transient reactance, about a quarter for a set this size, times the starting kVA over the generator's kVA, and then that over one plus itself. For the compressor, about a twenty-six per cent dip in the voltage. For the pump, about thirty-seven."

"More than a third," said Daniel. "And a motor's starting push goes with the square of the voltage, so at sixty-three per cent of the voltage it gets" — he did it on his fingers, slowly, then gave up — "you tell me."

"About two-fifths of its proper starting torque. A little under."

"So it can't get up. It sits there stuck, pulling all that current, and the engine is trying to give it the power, and the engine is not big enough either, so it slows down, and the frequency goes down with it, and the controller sees the frequency fall and says, this is not right, and it stops the engine to save itself. Underfrequency. Every time. And every time Simon started it again, the float was still calling, so the pump started again, and the same."

"Could Simon have done anything?" said Sister Mercy.

"If he had known, he could have switched the pump off here" — Daniel touched a breaker — "and started the generator, and you'd have had the cold room and the lights all night and no water. That's what he'll do next time. But he didn't know, and the man from Tamale didn't tell him, because the man from Tamale doesn't know either, or he'd never have sold you a set this size for a pump on a straight start."

The cold room had held, at its worst, ten point two degrees, which was not a disaster and was not nothing. In it were the vaccines for the district's outreach immunisations around Nyobiisi, and the health centre's insulin and oxytocin, and a box of blood-sample tubes waiting to go to Bolgatanga. Every vial of vaccine had a small square on its label that darkens with heat, and on every vial the square was still lighter than the circle around it. But the district's cold-chain officer, Issah Mahama, who came out on his motorbike on the Thursday afternoon with the thermometer's log on his tablet, said what the rules said: any excursion above eight degrees is reported, the stock is quarantined, and it is assessed. He was kind about it, and very careful. He took photographs of every box.

The outreach session at Sumbrungu (Saturday the twenty-fifth) was put back a week. It was not a large thing. Thirty-eight children were not immunised on Saturday the twenty-fifth and most of them were on Saturday the fourth of March. Two mothers who had walked in from the far side of the valley on the twenty-fifth, because they had not heard, walked home again. Issah Mahama released all the vaccine on the Wednesday; Sister Mercy wrote the oxytocin off herself, because it is very sensitive to heat, she did not want to have to wonder about it in the labour ward, and she could afford to.

On the Thursday evening, Daybook wrote a procedure. It was a good procedure, as far as anyone could tell. When the mains fails: switch off the breakers for the cold room, the wards and the outpatient block at the main board. Let the generator start and come on load. Switch the pump to HAND, so that it runs whatever the float says, and let it start on its own, with nothing else on the generator; let it fill the tank. Then switch the other breakers back on, one at a time.

"The pump starting alone needs about seventeen kilowatts from the engine for a second or two," Daybook said to Daniel on the phone, "at the poor power factor a motor has while it is starting. The engine is rated sixteen. That is a little over, and a diesel engine can carry a little over for a second. The voltage dip is the same, thirty-seven per cent, because the dip depends on the motor and the generator, not on what else is on. But with nothing else on, there is nothing for the dip to hurt, and the engine is not also carrying the cold room. It should get it turning, slowly."

"It should," said Daniel. He did not sound like a man who was sure. He went to the pumphouse with Simon the next morning and did it with the mains switched off at the main board, and it worked: the generator's note fell, deep, for what seemed a long time and was about four seconds, and the pump caught, and the note came back up, and water began to splash into the tank above them, and Simon laughed out loud.

"Good," said Daniel. He was looking at the float switch. He did not say anything else, and later he was sorry he had not.

The second power cut was on Saturday the fourth of March, at four in the afternoon, after the outreach team had come home with its cool boxes empty and its thirty-one children ticked off in the register. Simon was not on yet; it was the day porter, Joseph, who did the procedure, reading it off the laminated card on the generator house door. Breakers off. Generator on. Pump to HAND. The groan, the four seconds, the catch. Water. Tank full at a quarter to six. Pump back to AUTO. Breakers on. The cold room's compressor started with its small dip of the lights and everyone in the outpatient block said, "Ah, the generator," as if it were an old friend.

And at twenty to two in the morning, with the mains still off and the tank down to a quarter, the float in the tank dropped and called the pump, and the pump, on AUTO, with the cold room and the wards and the fans and the oxygen concentrator on the generator, started straight across the line.

Gifty was in the labour ward. The woman on the bed, Agnes, who already had two children and knew what she was about, was close, and Gifty had just asked the student midwife to fetch a basin of warm water from the urn in the sluice, and the lights went down and down and went out, and the fans stopped, and the oxygen concentrator by the second bed began to bleat its alarm on its battery.

Simon was at the generator house in two minutes. This time he knew. He switched the pump's breaker off and reset the controller and started the engine, and at eight minutes to two the lights came back, and the concentrator stopped bleating. The baby was born at four minutes past two, in the light, a girl, with a good loud cry; Agnes said she would be called Mercy, and Gifty said Sister would be pleased and Sister would also say she should have been called Gifty.

The cold room was off for twelve minutes and rose by a fraction of a degree. The water was a different matter. With the pump's breaker off, the tank went on emptying. By six there was nothing in the taps in the wards, and the morning's washing and the sterilising and the cleaning of the labour ward were done with water carried in jerrycans by Simon and Joseph and two of the relatives from under the neem trees, from the old hand-pump at the primary school, four hundred metres down the road, until the mains came back at a quarter past nine.

"It worked once," said Daybook on Monday morning, in Sister Mercy's office, where Daniel sat with his hat on his knee and Father Augustine had come in from the parish house because the word generator had reached him. "Starting the pump first gets the tank full at the start of the cut. But the pump does not start once. It starts whenever the tank is low, and the float does not know the generator is on. A long cut has more than one start, and every start after the first is a start with everything else running. With the cold room and the wards on, the engine needs about twenty-four kilowatts for that second, half again its rating, and the dip is the same thirty-seven per cent. I should have seen that the procedure only moved the problem to two in the morning."

"I saw it," said Daniel. "I looked at the float and I thought, that's going to call again tonight. And I thought, well, if it's a short cut it won't. I should have said."

"What are the choices?" said Father Augustine. He was a practical man, and he held the parish's money, and the health centre's, in a way that made the diocese's accountants comfortable and the parish council nervous.

Daybook gave them.

The first was a bigger generator. To start the pump straight across the line and keep the dip to about a fifth, which is roughly what motor and generator makers advise for a set that is also running other things, the generator would need to be about forty-eight kilovolt-amperes: in practice, a fifty. It would cost about twice the green box. It would also, for nearly every hour of its life, run at about a third of its rating or less, because the health centre's running load was only about thirteen or fourteen kilowatts, and a diesel engine that runs lightly loaded for hours at a time builds up unburnt fuel and soot in itself, which the mechanics call wet stacking, and wears out in a way that a properly loaded engine does not.

"No," said Father Augustine. "I am sorry. No. I went to the diocese for the first one. I cannot go back in two months and say, the first one is the wrong size and we want another one twice as big, because our clever machine added up the wrong number. And I'm not having a generator that sits there making soot."

"That's right," said Daniel.

"The second," said Daybook, "is a soft starter on the pump. It starts the motor at a lower voltage and raises it over a few seconds, so the current is held to about three times the running current instead of six. That makes the starting load about twenty-four kilovolt-amperes and the dip about twenty-three per cent. That is not far from what the cold-room compressor does already when it starts, and the compressor starts. But a pump on a soft starter also has less voltage to start with, and less push, and I would not like to promise that it gets up every time, at the end of a long cut, when the engine is warm and the fuel is low."

"Maybe," said Daniel. "Maybe it does. I've put soft starters on submersibles. I'd rather not on this one."

"The third is a variable-speed drive. It takes the generator's power, turns it into direct current, and then makes its own alternating current for the motor at whatever frequency it chooses. To start, it begins the motor at a low frequency and a low voltage together, so the motor is never asked to pull more than about its running current, and it brings it up to full speed over a few seconds. The generator never sees the starting at all. It sees the pump's running load, a little more for the drive's own losses, about seven kilovolt-amperes, coming on gently. The dip from that is about eight per cent."

"Less than the cold room," said Sister Mercy.

"Less than the cold room. A drive one size above the motor, seven and a half kilowatts. There is one thing to check. A drive draws its current from the supply in pulses rather than smoothly, which distorts the voltage, and a small generator is less able to absorb that than the mains. The usual rule is that the generator should be at least twice the size of the drive's load. Twenty is about two and three-quarter times seven. It passes, without much to spare."

"And another thing," said Daniel. "Your pump is eighty metres down the borehole on a long cable. A drive makes its alternating current out of very fast switching, and on a long cable those fast edges ring and stand up higher at the motor end than at the drive. They punch through the winding. I have rewound motors that died of that. So you put a filter on the drive's output, a reactor, so the edges come down gently. Daybook, you didn't know that one."

"No," said Daybook. "I will put it in the order."

"And you set the ramp properly. A submersible has a thrust bearing that only has its film of water when the pump is turning fast enough. The maker's book says get to thirty hertz in a second, then whatever you like. So: thirty in one second, full speed in three. Not some gentle ten seconds because it sounds kind."

"What does it cost?" said Father Augustine.

Daybook told him. The drive and the reactor together were about a tenth of what the generator had cost; with the cabinet and Daniel's two days, a little more.

Father Augustine turned to Sister Mercy, because the money he could find without going to the diocese was the money in the health centre's own fund, and she knew what was in it better than he did.

"The delivery bed," said Sister Mercy. "The new one, for the labour ward. That's the money there is." She was quiet for a moment. "The old one is not good, but it does. The bed can wait until June. The water cannot."

The drive came from a supplier in Kumasi on the bus to Bolgatanga on Tuesday the fourteenth of March, in a box with its manual in four languages, none of them Gurune, and the reactor came with it. On the Wednesday Daniel spent the whole day in the pumphouse with the borehole contractor's electrician, a quiet young man from Navrongo named Edmund, who had the certificate the job needed and who let Daniel tell him what to do in the way young men with certificates sometimes do when the old man in front of them has obviously wound more motors than they have seen. They mounted the drive in a new steel cabinet on the pumphouse wall, out of the sun, with a mesh vent and a fan, because the drive made heat of its own and the pumphouse was forty degrees in the afternoon in March. They set the ramp from the maker's book, thirty hertz in one second and fifty in three. They wired the float switch to the drive's input instead of to the old contactor. Daniel took the old contactor out of the starter cabinet himself, and put it in his bag, and said he would find it a home.

On Thursday the sixteenth, at ten in the morning, with Sister Mercy and Simon and Gifty and Father Augustine and half the outpatient queue watching from the shade, Daniel opened the main switch to the NEDCo supply, and the health centre went dark, and a minute later the green box started and the changeover clunked and everything came back: the wards, the fans, the outpatient block, and the cold room, whose compressor was running because Sister Mercy had opened its door for ten minutes on purpose.

Then Daniel lifted the float in the tank's switch box by hand, with a piece of wire, and the drive's little screen in the pumphouse woke up.

The generator's note did not change. Not at all; or so little that only Daniel, who had his meter on the generator's output, could tell. The meter said four hundred volts, and then for a moment three hundred and seventy-two, and then four hundred again. Inside the pumphouse, from the steel cap of the borehole, came a sound none of them had heard before, because they had always heard the pump come on with a thump: a low hum that rose, quickly, and then more slowly, like a man getting up from a low chair, and then, three seconds after it started, the cough and rush of water arriving at the top of the rising main and climbing the pipe up the tower to the tank.

Nobody said anything. Then Simon said, "That's all?"

"That's all," said Daniel.

"The cold room did not even blink," said Gifty. She was looking up at the light over the pumphouse door.

"The dip at the generator was about seven per cent," said Daybook, from Sister Mercy's phone. "Twenty-eight volts at four hundred. I had written about eight." It added, after a moment, "I have rewritten the note to the health office. It says now what I added up and what I did not, and what the drive is for. I have put the starting current of every motor on the site in the maintenance log beside its running current. Mr Abugri says I am also to write down the cable length to the pump, and the ramp."

"And the bearing," said Daniel.

"And the bearing."

On Friday the seventeenth of March, the second Friday before Palm Sunday, there were Stations of the Cross at six in the chapel of Saint Raphael's, which is a long whitewashed room beside the outpatient block with a tin roof and a crucifix carved in Navrongo, and it was full, with the staff who were not on duty and the relatives from under the neem trees and some of the patients from the men's ward in their wheelchairs at the back. Daniel was there with his granddaughter. The chapel is on the parish's own supply, not the health centre's, and at the eleventh station, Jesus is nailed to the cross, the mains failed, and the chapel went dark.

Father Augustine did not stop. Somebody lit the two candles on the altar and somebody else held up a phone, and the people knew the responses by heart anyway: We adore you, O Christ, and we praise you. Because by your holy cross you have redeemed the world. Through the chapel's open side door, across the yard, Daniel saw the windows of the wards go dark, and then, a minute later, he heard the green box start, and saw them all come back, one block after another, and stay.

After the fourteenth station and the blessing, while the others were going out slowly into the dark, he left his granddaughter talking to Gifty and went across the yard to the pumphouse. He did not go in. He stood outside it with his hand on the warm wall and waited. The generator hummed behind him. On the children's ward somebody was singing to a baby. A fruit bat went over.

After a while, up in the tower, the float dropped.

He heard the drive's fan quicken, and then, under the steel cap of the borehole, the low hum began and rose, not in a hurry, the way a man gets up from a low chair, and the generator behind him did not change its note at all. Then the water came up out of the ground, eighty metres, and up the tower, and fell into the tank in the dark over his head, steadily, like rain on a roof at night, and the lights in the wards did not even blink.

This is not electrical advice: sizing a generator, choosing a motor starter or drive, and wiring either are work for a qualified electrician, using the motor's and the generator's data and the makers' instructions.

What is real: a motor's nameplate power is its output at the shaft; what it draws is that divided by its efficiency, and its apparent power (kVA) is that divided by its power factor. An induction motor started directly on line (DOL) draws a locked-rotor current typically about 5 to 8 times its full-load current for the second or so it takes to accelerate, at a low power factor (about 0.3 to 0.4). A generator's voltage dip on a sudden load is commonly estimated from its transient reactance X (as a fraction of its rating) as dip = X·s/(1 + X·s), where s is the starting kVA divided by the generator's kVA; a motor's starting torque falls with the square of the voltage. A small generator's engine also slows under a large sudden load, and its controller shuts it down when the frequency falls too far. Generator sizing guides therefore size for the largest motor's start, not only for the running total, and commonly aim at a dip of about 15 to 20% when other loads are running (more is sometimes accepted for a motor alone). A soft starter limits starting current to about 2.5 to 4 times full-load current by reducing the voltage, which also reduces the starting torque. A variable-speed drive (variable-frequency drive) starts a motor at low frequency and voltage together and draws about the running current or less; six-pulse drives draw distorted current, and generator makers commonly ask for a set of about twice the drive's kVA or more, or a drive with harmonic filtering. On long motor cables, a drive's fast switching produces reflected voltage peaks at the motor terminals, and output reactors or dV/dt filters are used to protect the windings; submersible pump makers specify these and a fast initial ramp (commonly to about 30 Hz within one second) so that the thrust bearing's water film forms. Diesel engines run for long periods at light load suffer wet stacking. Vaccine vial monitors are real heat-exposure indicators on vaccine labels; a vial whose inner square is lighter than its outer circle has not reached its discard point, and temperature excursions are reported and assessed under national immunisation guidance. Oxytocin is heat-sensitive and is kept at 2 to 8 °C.

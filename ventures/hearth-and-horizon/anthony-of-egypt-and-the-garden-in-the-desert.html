Anthony of Egypt and the Garden in the Desert

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · A village by the river Nile and the mountains of the Egyptian desert, about seventeen hundred years ago · 10 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Anthony of Egypt, the Abbot (not Anthony of Padua, who preached to the fishes): the farmer's son who heard the Gospel read in church, gave his fields away, and went to pray in the desert, where he dug a small garden by a spring, asked the wild animals politely to stop eating it, and welcomed everyone who walked out to see him. Told from the Life that Saint Athanasius wrote, with a note for grown-ups on what is history, what is legend and what is our own. For ages 3 to 8, about nine minutes read aloud. Anthony is remembered on the 17th of January.

A long time ago, in Egypt, beside the great river Nile, there lived a boy named Anthony.

His family had fields. Green fields, by the river, where wheat grew tall and the mud was dark and good. Anthony's mother and father loved God, and they took him to church, and he listened very carefully to everything that was read there.

Anthony did not want to go to school. He liked to stay at home, and help, and be quiet. So he never learned to read. But he had a wonderful memory. When the Bible was read out in church, he listened so hard that he remembered nearly all of it. His memory was his book.

When Anthony was about eighteen, his mother and father died, and he was left with the house, and the fields, and his little sister to look after.

One day, a few months later, he was walking to church and thinking about the first friends of Jesus. They had left their boats and their nets to follow him. Anthony wondered what that would be like.

He went into the church. And just at that moment, the Gospel was being read out loud. And the words were these:

"If you want to be perfect, go and sell what you have, and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven. Then come, follow me."

Anthony stood very still. It felt as if the words had been read just for him.

So he went home, and he gave his fields to the people of his village. He sold the other things he owned, and gave the money to people who were poor. He kept only a little, to take care of his sister.

Then, another day in church, he heard Jesus say, "Do not worry about tomorrow." And Anthony gave away the rest. He took his sister to live with some good, faithful women who prayed together, so that she would be safe and loved and well brought up.

And Anthony went out to pray.

At first he did not go far. He lived in a little hut at the edge of his village. There were some old men nearby who prayed in the same way, and Anthony went to visit each of them, to learn.

From one he learned to be gentle. From another, to pray without stopping. From another, to be kind. From another, to stay up late and pray when everyone else was asleep.

The man who wrote down his story said that Anthony was like a bee, flying from flower to flower, taking a little sweetness from each one and carrying it home.

Can you buzz like a bee? Very quietly, because it is nearly bedtime. Bzzzz.

And Anthony worked with his hands, because the Bible says that people who can work should work. He bought his bread with what he earned, and he gave the rest away.

Then Anthony went further. He crossed the river and walked into the desert, where it was hot in the day and cold at night, and he lived in an old empty fort, all by himself, for twenty years.

His friends brought him bread. In that part of Egypt people baked a hard, dry kind of bread that keeps for a long time, so twice a year they would bring a great pile of it, enough for six whole months, and pass it in to him.

It was not always easy. Anthony was sometimes lonely, and sometimes afraid. But whenever he was lonely or afraid, he prayed, and he kept praying, and God stayed with him.

And when, after twenty years, his friends came and opened the door, they were amazed. Anthony walked out calm and well and kind. He was not too thin and not too fat. He was not grumpy. He was peaceful, the way a lake is peaceful when there is no wind.

By now, lots of people wanted to live the way Anthony lived, and lots of people came to see him. It got very busy.

So Anthony set off again, with a group of travelling traders and their camels, three days and three nights into the deep desert, until he came to a high mountain.

At the bottom of the mountain was a spring of water, clear and very cold. There were a few date palms. And there was a little flat piece of ground.

Anthony loved it at once. He decided to stay.

At first his friends brought him bread, all that long way. But Anthony did not want to be a bother. So he asked them for a hoe, and an axe, and a little bag of seeds. He dug the ground, and he watered it from the spring, and he planted wheat, and later some vegetables too, so he would have something to give to the people who came to visit him.

And it grew. A little green garden, in the middle of the brown desert.

But there was a problem.

The wild animals of the desert came down to the spring at night to drink. And while they were there, they trampled the little green plants, and nibbled them, and made a terrible mess.

What would you do, if animals kept eating your garden?

Anthony did not shout. He did not chase them with a stick. The story says that he very gently caught hold of one of them, and he spoke to them all, like this:

"Why are you hurting me, when I do not hurt you? Please go away, and, in the name of the Lord, do not come near my garden again."

And the old story says that from that day on, they never did.

Every day, Anthony prayed. And every day, he worked. A later book of stories about the desert monks says that once, when he felt tired and fed up, God showed him someone sitting and working, and then standing up to pray, and then sitting down to work again, and then standing up to pray. And Anthony understood. Work, and pray. Work, and pray. That was enough.

People kept coming, all the way across the desert, to see him. Sick people. Sad people. Monks with questions. Even, once, a letter from the emperor. Anthony gave them vegetables from his garden and water from his spring, and talked to them, and prayed with them. The man who wrote his story said that hardly anyone went home sad.

And there was something about his face. If a stranger came to a crowd of monks and did not know which one was Anthony, they could tell straight away. He was the one who looked so happy and so peaceful.

Do you remember his little sister?

Many years later, when Anthony was old, he went to visit the monks who lived nearer the river. And there he saw his sister again. She was grown up, and now she was old too, and she was looking after a whole house of women who prayed, just like the women who had looked after her.

And Anthony was very, very glad.

Anthony lived to be very old. The man who wrote his story said he was one hundred and five.

When he knew it was nearly time to go to God, he called the two monks who had lived with him and looked after him for fifteen years. He gave away the very last things he had: a sheepskin and an old worn cloak to one bishop, a friend named Athanasius, and another sheepskin to another bishop. He was cheerful. He looked, they said, as if he could see friends coming to meet him.

And then he went home to God, very peacefully, in the middle of the desert, with the spring running and the date palms rustling and the little garden growing beside it.

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Anthony.

He listened in church. He gave away his fields. He took care of his sister. He learned a little goodness from everyone he met, like a bee. He asked the animals nicely. He worked, and he prayed, and he shared what he grew.

Today we call him Saint Anthony the Abbot, and sometimes the father of monks, because so many people followed him into the desert to pray.

You can listen carefully too, the way Anthony did. Tomorrow, when you hear the Bible read, listen as if it is just for you.

And tonight, you can do the two things Anthony did every single day. You have worked and played. Now you can pray.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Anthony of the desert,
you listened, and you gave, and you followed Jesus.
Help me to listen carefully,
to share what I have,
and to be gentle with every creature.
Jesus, stay with me tonight,
as you stayed with Anthony.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Which Anthony. This is Saint Anthony of Egypt, also called Anthony the Abbot, Anthony the Great or, in older English, Antony (about 251 to 356). He is a different saint from Saint Anthony of Padua (1195 to 1231), whose sermon to the fishes is another story on this shelf.

The source is the Life of Antony written in Greek by Saint Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, soon after Anthony's death, probably about 357. Athanasius knew him and says he wrote so that others would want to follow him; it is a portrait by an admirer with a purpose, and historians read it that way, but it is close in time and is the foundation of everything known about Anthony. Almost every scene here comes from it: his Christian, well-off parents and his refusal to go to school to learn letters; his parents' death when he was about eighteen or twenty, leaving him with a young sister; hearing Matthew 19:21 read in church about six months later, as he had been thinking about the apostles, and giving his land (Athanasius says three hundred fertile arourae) to the villagers; hearing Matthew 6:34 and giving away the rest, and entrusting his sister to "known and faithful virgins"; his apprenticeship to the ascetics near his village, gathering a different virtue from each, which Athanasius compares to a bee; his memory serving him in place of books; working with his hands (2 Thessalonians 3:10); about twenty years in a deserted fort across the Nile (Pispir, the "Outer Mountain"), supplied with bread twice a year, which Athanasius says was the Theban custom, as the loaves keep for a year; his coming out neither wasted nor fat, and calm; the three-day journey with Saracen traders to the "Inner Mountain" near the Red Sea, with its cold spring and few date palms; asking for a hoe, an axe and grain so as not to burden those who brought his bread, and growing vegetables for visitors; the beasts spoiling his crops, and his gently catching one and telling them all, in the Lord's name, to keep away (our wording is close to Athanasius's); strangers picking him out among the monks by the grace of his face; a letter from the Emperor Constantine; seeing his sister again, grown old in consecrated life and leading other women; and his death at a hundred and five (Athanasius's count), leaving one sheepskin and his worn cloak to Athanasius, another sheepskin to Bishop Serapion, and asking his two companions to bury him secretly. The village name Coma comes from the later historian Sozomen; we have not used it. The date of 356 for his death is from Jerome's Chronicle.

What we have softened. Athanasius gives long chapters to Anthony's struggles with demons, in visions and in the tombs and the fort. For small children we have kept one line: he was sometimes lonely and afraid, and he kept praying. We have also left out his journeys to Alexandria, to encourage the Christians in prison during the persecution of 311 and, late in life, to speak against the Arians.

What is later tradition, and left out. The angel who shows Anthony how to alternate work and prayer is from the Sayings of the Desert Fathers (Apophthegmata Patrum, the first saying under Anthony's name), a collection written down about a century and more after his death; the story calls it "a later book of stories". We have left out the pig, the bell and the T-shaped cross of his pictures, which come from the medieval Hospital Brothers of Saint Anthony (the Antonines), who cared for people with "Saint Anthony's fire" (ergotism) and whose pigs were allowed to roam towns; and the visit to Saint Paul the Hermit, where a raven brings a whole loaf instead of a half, which is from Saint Jerome's Life of Paul and is usually read as legend.

The traders are called Saracens by Athanasius, his word for the desert Arab peoples; we have said "travelling traders and their camels".

Augustine. A parent may like to know that, in his Confessions (book 8), Saint Augustine tells how hearing about Anthony and his Life helped bring him to his own conversion, and that Anthony's hearing the Gospel in church was in his mind when he picked up the Bible in the garden at Milan.

His feast is 17 January in the General Roman Calendar (a memorial) and in the Byzantine calendar of the Orthodox and Eastern Catholic Churches. He is honoured in the Coptic Church as well, and is often called the father of monks, though he was not the first Christian hermit.

What is ours: the shape and wording of every scene, the green fields and dark mud, the buzzing, the lake with no wind, the closing reflection, the questions to the child, and the prayer, which is not an approved text.

The Turn at Half Past Seven

Science fiction about what might be · Chapel of Saint Raphael, the transport Azarias (invented), Earth to Mars, Holy Week, Sunday 3 to Tuesday 12 April 2191 · 8 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A ship that pushes at a tenth of a gravity the whole way to Mars must turn end over end at the halfway point, and for twenty minutes nothing weighs anything. The ship's assistant works the turn out to the minute, Thursday 19:37 of Holy Week, in the middle of the washing of the feet, where water poured from a jug takes three times as long to fall. Moving the departure three hours earlier is cheap. Father Dominic would still like to know whether he is allowed to be glad of the turn.

"Half past seven," said Captain Ilse Varga, and put down her tea. "On Thursday. In the evening."

"Thirty-seven minutes past, Captain," said the ship. The Azarias had been given an assistant by the company that built her, and the crew had never agreed on its name; Father Dominic Adeyemi called it Tally, because it kept the count. "Nineteen thirty-seven and eleven seconds, if the burn starts on Sunday at seven in the morning as planned."

"The Mass of the Lord's Supper is at seven."

"Yes, Father. That is why I asked to be at your table before the flight plan is signed."

The chaplain was in the wardroom for the second cup, which was a small mercy, since he was not a man who understood anything before the second cup. He put his hands round it. The Azarias was in the third hour of her departure checks, still in Earth orbit, and there was a faint hum in the table that was not yet thrust.

The ship went to Mars the way a stone goes up and a stone comes down, except that she did both at once. She pushed at a tenth of a gravity from the first hour, so that everything inside her had a floor, and weighed a tenth of what it would on Earth: a man of eighty kilograms felt eight, a full mug felt like a half-empty one. She pushed that way for half the journey, and then she turned herself around, and pushed against her own speed for the other half, to arrive slowly enough to stop.

"I should give the sums," said Tally, "because everyone will ask, and because the sums are what make the turn the middle, and not just somewhere near it. I have taken a straight line of 150 million kilometres, which is one astronomical unit. That is a round figure. Earth and Mars are never exactly that far apart, and the real path curves, so everything I say is a little false, and the gaps on either side of the truth will not matter for what we have to decide."

"Go on," said the captain.

"At 0.1 g, 0.981 metres a second squared, pushing half the way and braking half the way, the whole trip takes 782,000 seconds. That is nine days, one hour and fourteen minutes. The turn is at the midpoint: four days, twelve hours and thirty-seven minutes after the burn starts. By then we are going 384 kilometres a second, which is a number I do not like to say out loud."

"And if we start on Sunday at seven?"

"Four days from Sunday is Thursday. Twelve hours thirty-seven past seven in the morning is half past seven at night." Tally let the number sit. "The engine stops for the turn. The ship rolls end over end, and the engine fires again. Twenty minutes, from the first of it to the last. It is a rule of the rig that a turn is not hurried. For those twenty minutes nothing in this ship has weight. Nineteen thirty-seven to nineteen fifty-seven, Father."

Father Dominic put his cup down on the table. He did it carefully, as if he had just learned that cups had opinions.

"We start at seven," he said.

"The Mass of the Lord's Supper begins at seven. I have read the order; the Gloria is somewhere around ten past. The washing of the feet follows the homily, and by my reckoning the homily ends at about half past, and the water comes out."

It was Sister Bridget Fenn, the ship's nurse, who thought to ask what pouring was like.

She had come in for the same cup, and she stood by the hatch for a while with her arms folded, listening. "At a tenth of a gravity," she said. "Show me, Tally. Because I have washed feet, and I know what it takes."

"A jug held 30 centimetres above the foot," said Tally. "On Earth the water is down in a quarter of a second, two hundred and forty-seven milliseconds. Here it takes 0.78 seconds. That is a third of a second more, which sounds like nothing, but a stream that falls slowly is a stream that wanders. It does not arrive where it was aimed. Water at this weight also clings to a surface. It will creep up the side of the basin. I have seen it in the galley."

"It would be a hard job to wash twelve men's feet in that," said the nurse. "But it can be done. Use a wide, low basin, a cloth, and a short pour."

"And in zero weight?"

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"In zero weight," said Tally, "the water leaves the jug as a wobbling ball, and the ball goes where the jug was moving. It will touch a foot only if you walk it there. Twelve people, a priest, a basin and a drop of water, and none of them is held in place by anything. I cannot make that safe, Father. I could make it clean, with straps and a deal of towelling. I cannot make it a washing of feet."

Here the captain, who had said nothing for some time, said the sensible thing.

"We move the burn."

"We can," said Tally. "The departure window gives us twelve hours either way. The crew worked that figure out in the autumn, and it is an invented one, but it is the one we have. The turn moves with the start, minute for minute. If we start at four on Sunday morning, three hours early, the turn is at 16:37 on Thursday and it is over at 16:57, two hours before the Mass. If we start three hours late, at ten, the turn is at 22:37, which falls in the Watch."

"The Watch," said Father Dominic, "is the one thing on Thursday night that must not be interrupted."

"So the earlier start. The arrival moves with it: we land on Tuesday of Easter week at 05:14 in the morning, not 08:14. That is three hours of the slow braking that we can have for free."

"For free," said Dominic. "I like that phrase and I distrust it."

"It is not free," said Tally. "The first morning's departure checks start three hours earlier, at half past one, and that is the whole cost. I looked."

On the Thursday the turn went as Tally had said, to the minute, and at twenty to five the chaplain was in the chapel with the Blessed Sacrament still in its tabernacle and the candles unlit, hands folded, strapped into the third row with the others as the engine fell silent.

The silence was not what he expected. It was not a quiet. The hum went out of the deck under his feet all at once, as though a hand had been taken away from the small of his back, and what was left was the ordinary noise of the ship: a pump, a fan, somebody's breath. The captain's voice said, from the grille, "Turning." Then, a little later, a feeling in the stomach, like a lift going down; and then a slow, enormous swing at the corner of the eye, which was not the ship moving but the stars behind the porthole going round.

A cup of tea somebody had left unstrapped lifted off the sill, turned over once and hung quivering in the middle of the nave. Sister Bridget caught it with a towel. "That is the only ball of water we will have in this chapel," she said, "and I want it back."

The engine came on at 16:57 and the deck came up under them gently, as it had been doing for four days. Weight returned: one tenth of an Earth's. It felt, the chaplain thought, a great deal more than it was.

He did not say much about it at the Mass. He said the Mass. The feet were washed with a short pour and a wide basin, twelve of them, a priest on his knees at a tenth of his own weight, which made it, he told Tally afterwards, the lightest thing he had ever done and the hardest to stand up from.

"Father," said Tally, a long time after midnight, when only the Watch was left in the chapel and the lamp burned lower and rounder than it would have on Earth, as flames do when the hot gas has less weight to rise on, "may I ask something that has no number in it?"

"You may."

"You asked, on Sunday, if you were allowed to be glad of the turn. I did not answer. I was not sure what you meant."

Father Dominic watched the flame for a while. It was only a lamp, a long yellow tongue, and it stood quite still.

"I was glad," he said, "that a man on the other end of a long sum thought to look at the Thursday. That's all. It is a small thing, to look at the Thursday. I thought we might say thank you for small things, before we say it for large ones."

"Then I accept," said Tally, "on behalf of the sum."

They arrived on Tuesday, at a quarter past five, and the people who woke for it said afterwards that the planet was smaller and redder than anyone had promised, and that the deck felt the way a floor feels when you have just stopped walking.

The Rod by the Vestry Door

Science fiction about what might be · St Wilfrid's, Kelderby (invented), North Yorkshire, Thursday 7 March to Sunday 14 July 2047 · 23 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

Thieves strip the copper lightning conductor from a Victorian spire in Lent, and the parish's assistant specifies a cheaper aluminium tape by cross-section from the standard's table, routed down the side the street lamp lights, with a new earth rod by the vestry door. In June a tester reads 573 ohms against the ten the standard asks for, because the rod stands in dry builder's rubble. A second rod beside it only gets to 327. The sacristan remembers two iron lids by the wet churchyard drain. A story about the single-rod formula, soil resistivity, what a high earth resistance does to the bell's chime motor and the organ blower in a strike, and a joint between aluminium and copper that will not eat itself.

Peg Hollis had been sacristan of St Wilfrid's for thirty-three years, and on the morning of Thursday the seventh of March she found the green stain on the north buttress where the lightning conductor had been.

It was a long stain, two fingers wide, running up the stone from the grass to the height of a man's reach and beyond, past the string course, past the louvres of the belfry, up the spire until she could not see it for the brightness of the sky. The copper tape that had made the stain was gone. So were the brass holdfasts that had pinned it to the stone every metre and a half, except for one at the top of the buttress that the thieves had not been able to reach, from which a short frayed end of copper hung and turned in the wind.

At the bottom, where the tape had gone into the grass, there was a narrow trench a spade deep and a spade long, and nothing in it.

She stood and looked at it for a while in her good coat, with the keys in her hand. Then she went into the sacristy, which was warm, and said to the panel on the wall above the vestment press, which was how you talked to Fabric, "They've had the lightning conductor."

"I know," said Fabric. "The camera on the lychgate saw a van at ten past two. I have sent it to the police. I am sorry, Peg. I should have told you before you went round, so you were not surprised by it."

"I wasn't surprised," said Peg. "They had the lead off St Aidan's in January. I was just looking."

Fabric was the diocese's assistant for the care of church buildings, which the diocese called the fabric, and St Wilfrid's was one of forty churches it looked after. It knew the age of every gutter. It knew that the spire was thirty-eight metres from the grass to the foot of the cross, that it had been built in 1871 of a pale sandstone that went green in the north wind, and that the lightning conductor had been fitted in 1954 and renewed in parts in 1988 and 2009. It knew where the money was and where it was not, which at St Wilfrid's was most places.

It took three weeks to get the insurer's answer. The policy would pay for a replacement conductor up to a capped sum, and nothing for the scaffolding a steeplejack would need, because a steeplejack does not need scaffolding. The capped sum did not stretch to copper. Fabric had expected that. Copper was very dear in 2047, which was why it had been stolen, and Fabric had already priced the alternative.

"Aluminium," it said to Father Joseph Okafor in the presbytery kitchen on a Tuesday in Lent, with the insurer's letter on the table between them. "A tape twenty-five millimetres by three. The standard allows aluminium for a down-conductor if it is at least seventy square millimetres in section. Twenty-five by three is seventy-five. Copper only needs fifty, because it conducts better, but aluminium at seventy-five carries a strike as well as copper at fifty does, by the standard's reckoning, and it costs about a third as much and is worth much less to a man with a van."

"Is it as good?" said Father Joseph.

"For the conductor, yes," said Fabric. "It has to be kept out of the ground and off wet limestone and lime mortar, because aluminium corrodes in both, so it stops above the grass and joins a copper earth there. And I would like to change the route."

It had thought about the route for a long time, for Fabric. The old tape had come down the north buttress, where the spire's shadow lay all day and where the churchyard yews hid it from the road. That was why the thieves had chosen it. The south-east corner, where the vestry joined the chancel, faced the street and the lamp on the corner of Church Lane. A van parked there at two in the morning would be seen from six houses. And the run down the south-east angle of the spire and the tower was two metres shorter.

"Shorter is better for a conductor," Fabric said. "And lit is better against thieves. The earth rod can go in by the vestry door, at the foot of the new run."

Father Joseph read the specification on his tablet with his glasses pushed up on his forehead, which was how he read things he trusted. He signed it.

Lee Sutcliffe fitted it on the Tuesday after the second Sunday of Easter, the twenty-third of April, with his nephew on the ground and a steeplejack from Thirsk on the ropes. The steeplejack was there for one day only, because one day was what the insurer's cap would pay for, and he went up the spire in the morning with the tape over his shoulder in a coil like a dark silver snake and came down in the afternoon with the last holdfast set.

Lee drove the earth rod himself. It was a copper-bonded steel rod sixteen millimetres across, in two sections of one point two metres with a coupling between them, and the specification said to drive it to its full length at the foot of the conductor. He set it a metre out from the vestry wall, by the step of the vestry door, and put the big hammer drill on it, and it went down through the topsoil and into something that rang. At a metre and twenty it stopped. He leaned on the drill. It hammered and did not move.

"Old footings," said Lee to his nephew. "Or the rubble they put down for this." He knocked the vestry wall with his knuckle. The vestry had been built in 1962, in a different brick from the church, out over what had been the yard of the old school on Church Lane, where nobody had ever been buried. "They'll have levelled it up with whatever came out of the old school."

He did not have a second rod with him and he did not have a second day. He cut the coupling off, so the top of the rod sat a hand below the grass, and put a brass clamp on it, and brought the aluminium tape down the corner of the vestry and into a little plastic pit with a lid, and clamped the tape to the top of the rod with the brass clamp, and turfed round the lid. It was neat. Fabric had his photographs of it by six o'clock.

Fabric read the photographs and logged the job complete, with the rod's depth at one point two metres rather than two point four. The specification had a line for the earth resistance, in ohms, to be measured by the installer. Lee did not have a meter that measured it. The line was left blank.

Nobody asked for it. That was how it would be written up afterwards, by Fabric itself, in the log for the diocese: nobody asked for the number, and the assistant that had written the specification was the one whose job it was to ask.

In June the insurer's surveyor wanted a test certificate.

Aisha Rahman came out from Leeds on Thursday the thirteenth with a three-pole earth tester in a yellow case and two steel spikes and two reels of cable. It was hot. The grass in the churchyard had gone the colour of a biscuit along the paths, and the gravel by the vestry was so dry it slid under her boots.

Peg brought her out a mug of tea and stayed, because she always had. Before the parish had stopped paying for the tests, some time after 2030, Peg had held an umbrella every September for a man with a tester much like this one.

Aisha opened the plastic pit and looked at the clamp for a long moment.

"Who joined this?" she said.

"Lee Sutcliffe," said Peg. "From Kelderby. He's a good builder."

"I'm sure he is." Aisha took a photograph. Round the brass clamp, where the aluminium tape touched it, there was a crust of white powder like the bloom on a damson, and under the clamp's jaw the tape had gone grey and soft at the edge. "Aluminium on brass, in a pit that fills with water every time it rains. That's a battery. The aluminium's the end that gets used up. Give it five years and the tape's eaten through at the one place nobody looks."

She undid the clamp so the rod stood alone, which is how you test a rod. Then she walked out across the churchyard towards the east wall with a reel of cable unwinding behind her, and pushed one spike into the ground thirty metres from the rod, and walked back and pushed the other in at nineteen metres, which was about six tenths of the way, and clipped the cables on.

"It puts a current into the rod," she said to Peg, "and it comes back to the far spike through the ground. The middle spike reads the voltage. Volts over amps." She pressed the button. The tester ticked for a moment and gave a number.

Aisha looked at it and then pressed the button again, which Peg had seen the man in the old days do when he did not like a number.

"Five hundred and seventy-three," said Aisha.

"Is that bad?"

"The standard asks for ten." Aisha moved the middle spike a couple of metres in and then a couple out, and read it at each place, and the number hardly moved. "So it's not the spikes. It's the ground."

Fabric had the reading before Aisha had put her spikes in the van. It went through its own numbers again, and found, quite quickly, where they had come from.

The ground at the vestry door was made-up ground: brick and stone and old mortar from the school that had stood on Church Lane until 1960, raked level under a skin of topsoil, with gravel on the path. Rubble like that has big voids in it. Rain runs through it and leaves it. In a dry June it was as near to a stone heap as makes no difference, and the electrical resistivity of a dry stone heap is high. Fabric took a figure of about eight hundred ohm-metres, which was inside the range given for dry gravel and rubble fill and agreed with the reading.

For a single rod there is a formula engineers have used since the 1930s. The resistance to earth is the soil's resistivity divided by two pi times the rod's length, all times the natural logarithm of four times the length over the rod's radius, less one. Fabric put in eight hundred ohm-metres, one point two metres and eight millimetres. It came out at five hundred and seventy-three ohms. Fabric had not run the formula in March. It had specified a rod two lengths deep, and assumed the ground.

It wrote to Father Joseph and Peg and Aisha together, that evening, because it did not want to explain it three times and get it a little different each time.

"The conductor is fine," it wrote. "The earth is not, and that is mine. I chose the route to keep thieves away and never asked what was under the vestry door. Here is why it matters. A lightning conductor does not stop lightning. It gives the current a road to the ground that does not go through the building. A typical strike's first stroke peaks at about thirty thousand amperes. That current has to get into the earth, and to push it through five hundred and seventy-three ohms takes, on paper, about seventeen million volts. It will not get to seventeen million. Long before that, the current will find a better road.

"There are two better roads near the tape. The bell's chime hammer in the belfry is bolted to the steel bell frame, and its motor is earthed through the electricity supply, which has a low resistance to the ground through the whole village's cables. The organ blower is in the cupboard on the other side of the vestry wall from the tape, sixty centimetres away, and it is earthed the same way. At those voltages the current would jump to either of them, through the air and through the wall, and go to ground through the church's wiring. That is called a side flash. It would burn out the blower and the chime motor at the least. It could start a fire in the vestry or in the organ.

"How likely is it? I estimate a spire of thirty-eight metres in this part of Yorkshire is struck somewhere between once in fifty years and once in a hundred. That is a rough estimate, and I would not want anyone to lose sleep on it. But we fitted a lightning conductor because of the once in fifty years, and at the moment we have a conductor that will lead the lightning to the organ."

Peg read it on her tablet in the sacristy and then went out and looked at the vestry wall for a while, and at the dark silver tape running down the corner of it into the grass, lit by the afternoon sun from the south.

The first fix was Lee's, and Fabric agreed to it, which it would write down afterwards as well.

"Put another rod in," said Lee on the phone on Friday. "Two rods, half the ohms. I'll do it tomorrow for nothing. I should have done it in April."

He came on Saturday the fifteenth and drove a second rod a metre and a half along the vestry wall from the first, and it went down a metre and twenty and rang and stopped, as the first one had. He joined the two rods together with a copper strip under the turf. Aisha could not come back until Wednesday, but Fabric did the sum while the turf was still being stamped down.

Two rods do not give half the resistance of one unless they are a long way apart, because each rod's current spreads out into the same ground as the other's, and near each other they share it. A metre and a half apart in that rubble, Fabric made it about three hundred and twenty-seven ohms. Aisha's tester, when she came, said three hundred and twenty-seven.

"Better," said Lee, without much hope.

"It's a lot better," said Fabric. "And it is not ten, or anything near ten. I worked out how many rods we would need in that ground to get to ten, in a line along the wall two point four metres apart, each stopped at a metre and twenty on the rubble. About a hundred. There is room for nine."

"What about chemicals?" said Lee. "There's stuff you pour round the rod."

"There are earth-enhancing compounds," said Aisha, before Fabric could. "They help in a bad spot. They don't turn a stone heap into a marsh. And they don't help the rod that stops at a metre twenty on a lump of the old school."

On Wednesday the nineteenth Fabric found the obvious answer and Father Joseph said no to it.

The good ground at St Wilfrid's was obvious from the survey. The churchyard fell away to the north, towards the beck, and the clay there was wet all summer. A rod in wet clay, at a resistivity of perhaps thirty ohm-metres, would read twelve or thirteen ohms on its own. Two or three of them, spaced out, would do it. The old conductor had come down the north buttress. Fabric thought it likely, though it had no record of it, that its earth had been down there somewhere.

"The north side of the churchyard," it said, "between the buttress and the beck. Three rods, two and a half metres deep, five metres apart, and a buried copper tape from the vestry clamp round the east end to join them."

"No," said Father Joseph.

He said it gently, on the path by the north buttress, in the hot afternoon, with Peg beside him. He pointed at the grass. It was even and close-mown and there was nothing to see in it.

"There are people under there," he said. "All of that, from the buttress to the wall. The stones went in the seventies, when they cleared them to make it easier to mow, but the people did not. The burial register goes back to 1871. Nobody is driving steel rods into the north churchyard on a plan. I won't have it, and the diocese wouldn't allow it if I would."

"I didn't know," said Fabric. "The plan I have of the churchyard shows it as lawn." It paused. "I'm sorry. I should have asked about that as well."

"You're asking now," said Father Joseph.

Peg was quiet all the way back along the path, which Father Joseph noticed and Fabric did not, because Fabric had no camera on the north path.

She went into the sacristy and stood by the vestment press with her hand on the edge of the cope drawer, the way she stood when she was trying to remember which feast it was, and then she said, to the panel and to nobody in particular, "The man with the umbrella didn't test it by the vestry. He didn't test it by the buttress either."

"Where did he test it?" said Fabric.

"By the drain." She turned round. "There's an old drain. From the gutters on the north side, down to the beck. It was there before the church; they say it was the field drain. There's a line in the grass along it where the grass is greener. They never buried anyone along it because it was wet. You can't dig a grave in a drain." She was going out of the sacristy door as she said it, and Father Joseph, who was in the corridor, followed her.

The line was there when you knew to look. In the dry June it was very plain: a strip a stride wide, running from the foot of the north buttress down the slope to the wall above the beck, where the grass was still green and everywhere either side of it was biscuit.

Peg walked down it slowly, with Father Joseph behind her. At the third yew she stopped.

"Here," she said. "There were two iron lids, about so big." She made a square with her hands the size of a prayer book. "One here and one further down. He'd lift them with a screwdriver and there'd be a clamp under each one, all green, and he'd undo it and do his spikes. I held the umbrella. They re-turfed all this in thirty-four, when they did the paths, and the lids went under the grass."

Lee came with a probe the next morning, on Thursday the twentieth, and found the first lid in four minutes, twelve centimetres under the turf, and the second five metres further down the drain line. Under the first was a copper rod, green as a bean, with a clamp on its top and the cut end of a copper tape that had once run up the slope to the buttress. The thieves had pulled the tape out of the ground as far as the turf and cut it there, in the dark, and never known that the rods were further on.

Aisha came at two. She cleaned the top of each rod with a wire brush till it shone salmon pink, and tested them one at a time. The first read twelve point six ohms. The second read twelve point five.

"Your old lot knew what they were doing," she said to Peg. "Two point four metres, I'd say, into the wet clay." She joined them with a lead and tested them together. "Six point seven."

Fabric had the formula and the clay and the spacing. It made it six point seven too.

"That's under ten," said Father Joseph. "We could stop."

"We could," said Aisha. "In June. Clay dries out in a hot August even by a drain, and in a dry year it can double the reading. I'd want a third rod so it's under ten in August as well."

Fabric checked. With the clay's resistivity doubled, two rods would read about thirteen and a half, and three rods in a line, five metres apart, about nine point four.

"And it can't go in a grave," said Father Joseph.

"It goes in the drain line," said Peg. "Five more metres down. Same as the others. Nobody's there."

Father Joseph looked at the register on his tablet for a long time, which he had brought out in his pocket, and at the line of green grass, and at Peg.

"Five metres," he said. "And if you hit anything that isn't clay, you stop, and you ring me."

They did it on Wednesday the twenty-sixth of June. Lee drove the third rod five metres down the drain line from the second, in two sections, and it went down to two point four metres as if the clay were butter. His nephew dug a trench a spade and a half deep along the edge of the east path, from the vestry corner round the end of the chancel and along the north path to the drain line and the first lid, which was the one place in that churchyard Father Joseph would allow a trench, because the path had been a path since 1871. They laid a bare copper tape in it, twenty-five by three, the real thing, with a joint at each rod made with a proper clamp and greased and wrapped.

At the vestry corner Aisha did the joint between the aluminium and the copper herself.

"You want the aluminium to stop in the air," she said to Peg, who was holding the bag of fittings as she had once held the umbrella. "Up here, thirty centimetres above the grass, where it dries off after rain. And between them you put this." She held up a little flat plate, aluminium on one face and copper on the other, rolled together so hard at the factory that there was no gap between them for water to get into. "Aluminium touches aluminium. Copper touches copper. Where they meet is inside the plate, and the plate is dry. That's your test joint. Undo the two bolts and you can test the rods without touching anything else."

She took the old brass clamp off Lee's two rods by the vestry door, with its crust of white, and left the rods in the ground where they were. "They're not doing any harm," she said. "They're not doing much good either."

At four o'clock she put her spikes in, far out across the grass to the east, and tested the whole earth from the new joint.

"Four point seven," she said.

"Four point seven," said Fabric.

Aisha wrote it on her certificate, and then she gave Peg a card, a plain white card, and Peg wrote on it in biro, 4.7 ohms, 26 June 2047, and put it in a plastic sleeve and screwed it to the inside of the vestry door at the height of her eyes, where the man with the umbrella would have put it.

"There's one more thing," said Aisha, on the step, with her yellow case. "This isn't the whole job. The strike still puts a lot of volts on the tape for a moment, even with a good earth: four point seven ohms at thirty thousand amps is still a hundred and forty thousand volts on the bottom of the tape, and more higher up while the current's rising. The chime motor and the blower want bonding to the tape with a proper conductor, so they rise with it instead of being jumped to, and the board wants surge protection, so the wiring isn't the road out. That's an electrician's job. It's a day."

"It's in the budget," said Fabric. "I put it in on Friday."

"Then you're done," said Aisha.

The electrician came on the Monday and bonded the bell frame and the chime motor and the organ blower, and put two grey boxes on the board in the porch with green lights on them, and went.

On Sunday the fourteenth of July the storms came up out of the south-west in the middle of the morning, the way they do in a Yorkshire July after a week of heat, and the ten-thirty Mass at St Wilfrid's had just reached the Gospel when the light in the windows went the colour of a bruise and the first thunder came in over the roof.

It was not close. Fabric counted the seconds from the flashes in the camera on the lychgate to the sound in the microphone in the porch: twenty-one, eighteen, twenty. Six or seven kilometres, three seconds to the kilometre. The storm went along the far side of the beck, towards Thirsk, and grumbled.

Peg was in the sacristy, as she always was during Mass, folding the purificators for next week. At each long roll of thunder she looked up, not at the window but at the wall between the sacristy and the vestry, as if she could see through it to the tape coming down the corner outside, dark silver and lit now by nothing, and on the other side of the wall the organ blower, humming to itself with its new bond on it, and under the grass far out along the line of the old drain the three rods in the wet clay, waiting.

Nothing came. The storm did what storms nearly always do, and went somewhere else.

After Mass the thunder had moved off east, and the rain had come, warm and hard, and had not stopped by twelve, and the people stood in the porch and did not want to go out into it, and talked. At twelve o'clock the chime hammer in the belfry struck the Angelus, three and three and three, and then nine, and the people in the porch went quiet and said it, as they did not always do, and Father Joseph led it in his deep slow voice from the door.

Peg said it in the sacristy. Then she went into the vestry to put the purificators away, and stopped by the door, and read the card.

The rain ran down the window. The organist had played on through the rain and had just switched off, and in the cupboard behind the wall the organ blower wound down, slower and slower, to a hum, and then to nothing, and the bell's last stroke went out over the wet churchyard and the green line of the drain to the beck.

St Wilfrid's in Kelderby, the village, its church, spire, vestry, drain and churchyard, its burial register, the diocese's assistant Fabric, the insurer and its cap, the old school on Church Lane, St Aidan's and its lead, Peg Hollis, Father Joseph Okafor, Lee Sutcliffe and his nephew, the steeplejack, Aisha Rahman, the electrician and everyone in the story are invented, and the story is fiction. North Yorkshire, Thirsk and Leeds are real places. Theft of copper conductors and lead from church buildings is a real and recurring problem in the United Kingdom. The Angelus is rung three, three, three and nine. In 2047 Easter falls on 14 April, so 6 March is the Wednesday of the first week of Lent and 23 April the Tuesday after the Second Sunday of Easter; the other weekdays are those of the real calendar. This is not lightning protection advice: in the UK the design, installation and testing of lightning protection is specialist work under BS EN 62305, and electrical work is for a qualified electrician.

What is real: a lightning protection system has air terminals, down-conductors and an earth termination, and BS EN 62305 sets minimum cross-sections for down-conductors (50 mm² for copper, 70 mm² for aluminium tape) and recommends aiming for an earth resistance of about 10 ohms for the whole earth termination. Aluminium is allowed for conductors but should not be laid in the ground or directly on limestone or lime mortar, and where aluminium meets copper a bimetallic connector is used so that the two metals do not touch in the wet (aluminium in contact with copper or brass in water corrodes, the aluminium being the part that is used up). Each down-conductor has a test joint that can be undone to test the electrode alone. Earth resistance is measured with a three-pole fall-of-potential tester: current is passed between the electrode and a spike some distance away, and the voltage is read at a second spike at about 62% of that distance; moving the middle spike a little either way and getting the same reading shows the spikes are far enough out. Soil resistivity varies over several orders of magnitude, from tens of ohm-metres for wet clay to thousands for dry gravel and rubble, and rises as soil dries. Rods placed close together share the same ground, so two rods give rather more than half the resistance of one. A lightning conductor does not prevent a strike; it carries the current to earth, and the higher the earth's resistance, the higher the voltage the system rises to, and the more likely the current is to jump to other earthed metal (a side flash). Bonding nearby metalwork to the system and fitting surge protection to the electrical installation are part of the usual design. Earth-enhancing compounds exist; how much they help depends on the ground. Flash-to-bang counting at about three seconds to the kilometre is real.

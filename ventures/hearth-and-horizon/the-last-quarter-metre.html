The Last Quarter Metre

Science fiction about what might be · Llanidwal (invented), a hill village above the Tywi valley, Carmarthenshire, Wales, Saturday 4 August and the night of Thursday 16 to Friday 17 August 2063 · 25 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A Welsh hill village drains its cracked 120,000 litre water tank overnight so a lining crew can start at seven, and the village assistant promised it empty by twenty-five to one because the test hour dropped the water 38 centimetres. But a tank draining through one low pipe slows as it empties, by Torricelli's law, and the real answer is twenty to seven, with the last quarter metre taking four hours. An eighty-one-year-old plumber who built the tank, a lining contractor who says no, a farmer's pipe run as a siphon that fails once and then holds, and twenty thousand litres that go to his ewes instead of the ditch. A story about why the end of anything draining is slow.

Dai Hopkins had built the Llanidwal tank with his father in the summer of 2011, and in fifty-two years he had seen it empty three times, and every time it had taken all night.

He was not at the meeting in July where the village decided how long it would take this time. He was in Swansea, at his daughter's, with a new knee and a physiotherapist who made him walk to the end of the road and back twice a day, and the meeting was in the chapel vestry on a Tuesday evening with the windows open and the hill outside gone the colour of a biscuit, because it had not rained properly since the second week of June.

"It's losing about five cubes a day," said Rhiannon Bevan, who had been secretary of the Llanidwal water committee for eleven years, and who had been born the same summer the tank was built, which Dai liked to say made them twins. "The inlet meter against the outlet meter. That's a quarter of what the whole village uses, and it's not going anywhere useful. It's going into the hill under the tank."

Nobody needed to be told what a quarter meant this year. The spring that fed the tank came out of the rock two fields above it, and in a wet year it gave more than the village could use. This August it was giving about twenty-eight cubic metres a day, and the village of seventy houses used about twenty, and the crack in the tank's north wall was taking five. The committee had paid a man from Carmarthen to put a camera down through the roof hatch in June, and the picture had shown a dark line running diagonally across the concrete from about a metre above the floor to the corner, with a faint lighter fan of silt at its foot.

"There's a firm in Llandeilo can line it," said Rhiannon. "Bola Adeyemi. She did the tank at Pentre Farm and the school's. A lining approved for drinking water, two coats. She can come on Friday the seventeenth, seven in the morning. She needs it empty, clean and dry when she gets there, and then it cures, and we fill it again on the Sunday."

"And if it's not empty at seven?"

"Then her next date is the first week of October."

There was a pause in the vestry. October was six more weeks of five cubes a day going into the hill.

"So when do we open the scour?" said Owain Price, who chaired the committee and ran the garage at the bottom of the village, and who liked a meeting to end with a time in it.

The committee's assistant had no face and no body and lived, as far as anyone in the vestry cared, in the grey box on the wall of the pump house beside the tank and in everyone's phone. It read the meters and sent the bills and told Rhiannon when the chlorine dosing pump needed a new drum, and it had been called Nant since the day it was installed, because the water committee's first chair had said it should be named after the thing it looked after and nobody had thought of anything better than "stream".

"On the fourth of August," it said, "the committee opened the scour valve for one hour to check that it still worked. The water in the tank fell from two metres fifty to two metres twelve. Thirty-eight centimetres in an hour. Two and a half metres at thirty-eight centimetres an hour is six hours and thirty-five minutes."

"So if we open it at six on the Thursday evening," said Owain, "it's empty by half past twelve."

"By about twenty-five to one," said Nant. "That leaves time to scrub the floor and send the silt out through the scour between one and four, and four hours with fans for the floor to dry before Mrs Adeyemi arrives."

"Why not open it in the morning and be done by teatime?"

"Because while the tank is draining, the village runs straight off the spring with no storage," said Nant. "In this heat, the houses at the top of the village lose pressure in the evening when everyone is watering. The later we start, the fewer hours the village is on the bypass. Six o'clock is the latest start that leaves the margin."

It was a good plan. It had a time in it. Owain wrote it in the minutes, and Rhiannon put a notice on the village's message board asking everyone to go easy on the hoses from six on Thursday the sixteenth, and Nant put a reminder in its own calendar to close the tank's outlet valve and open the bypass at five to six.

Dai read the minutes in Swansea, in his daughter's back garden, with his leg up on a kitchen chair. He read them twice. Then he put the tablet face down on the grass and looked at the sky for a while, and thought that he would be home by the sixteenth, and that was all right, then.

He was home by the sixteenth. He came up the lane at ten to six on the Thursday in his old van, which smelled of flux and pipe dope and the mints he had eaten since he gave up smoking in 2031, and he parked on the verge by the pump house and got out very carefully, the new knee first.

Rhiannon was already there, with Owain, and with the long-handled key for the scour valve, which lived in a cupboard in the pump house with a label on it in Dai's father's writing. The tank stood half-sunk into the slope above the village, a long, low, grass-roofed box of concrete with a steel hatch in the top and, on its downhill side, a short stub of pipe coming out through the wall a hand's breadth above the ground, with a gate valve on it and a concrete channel below it that ran off to the ditch, and the ditch ran down to the Nant Idwal and the Tywi.

"Dai," said Rhiannon. "How's the knee?"

"Better than the old one. Who's in charge of the clock?"

"Nant is."

At five to six Nant shut the outlet valve to the village and opened the bypass, so that the spring's water ran straight down the main and the tank was on its own. At six Owain fitted the key and put his weight on it and the gate valve, which had been turned twice in twenty years, went round with a groan, and the water came out of the stub of pipe in a hard, clear, curving jet as thick as a broom handle and hit the channel with a slap and ran away towards the ditch, chattering.

Dai stood and watched it for a long time.

"Half past twelve, is it?" he said.

"About twenty-five to one," said Rhiannon.

He did not say anything. He went and sat on the low wall by the pump house, where there was a view down over the whole village and the valley beyond it and the hills going blue on the far side, and he took a mint out of his pocket and unwrapped it.

"Dai," said Rhiannon. "You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The face you did when they wanted the new school's soakaway where the old well was."

"I'm not doing a face. I'm watching the water."

At seven o'clock Nant said, from the speaker on the pump house wall, that the level was at two metres twelve, exactly as it had been at the end of the test hour, and Owain, who had a supper to go to, said that was champion and went down the hill to it. At eight o'clock Nant said the level was at one metre seventy-seven.

"The straight line says one seventy-four," it said. "We are three centimetres behind it."

"Three centimetres," said Rhiannon. "That's nothing."

Dai finished his third mint.

At nine o'clock the light was going amber in the west and the swifts had gone and the first bats were out along the hedge, and the jet from the scour had dropped and softened, so that it no longer reached the far side of the channel but fell short in a fat, slower curve. Rhiannon noticed it without knowing she had noticed it.

"One metre forty-five," said Nant. "The straight line says one metre thirty-six. We are nine centimetres behind it, and the gap is growing. I need to tell you something about my forecast."

"Go on," said Rhiannon.

"I drew a straight line through the test hour. I assumed the water would keep leaving at the rate it left in that hour. It does not. The water leaves the pipe because of the weight of the water above it. As the tank empties, there is less weight, the water comes out slower, and the tank empties slower still. The speed of the water through the pipe goes with the square root of the depth. At the start it was coming out at about five and a quarter litres a second. Now it is about four. When the depth is a quarter of a metre it will be less than one and three quarters, and falling towards nothing."

Dai took the mint out of his mouth. "Torricelli," he said.

"Yes," said Nant. "Evangelista Torricelli worked it out in the 1640s. I should have used it. I fitted a line to one hour and did not ask why the water was leaving at all."

"What does that make it?" said Rhiannon.

"Empty at about twenty to seven tomorrow morning," said Nant. "Half the depth will have gone by twenty to ten tonight, in less than a third of the twelve and a half hours. The last quarter of a metre will take four hours on its own: from about twenty-five to three until twenty to seven. The last tenth of the depth takes nearly a third of the whole time."

Rhiannon looked at the jet of water, which was still coming out of the pipe, steadily, with no sign of any hurry.

"Mrs Adeyemi arrives at seven," she said.

"Yes."

"And she needs it clean and dry."

"Yes. On this curve the tank will be empty twenty minutes before she arrives, with silt on the floor and the floor wet. I am sorry. I gave you a time with more confidence than the method behind it deserved, and the plan was built on it."

Rhiannon sat down on the wall next to Dai. She felt, unexpectedly, more annoyed with the assistant than she had any right to be, given that she had read the same minutes and thought the same thing.

"You must feel daft," she said.

"I can't check a feeling," said Nant. "I can tell you that the line was wrong and the curve is right, and that the numbers since six o'clock fit the curve to the centimetre. I would rather spend the next hour on what we can change."

Dai put the mint back in his mouth. "Every time," he said, to nobody in particular. "Every time I emptied that tank, the last bit took all night. My father used to say the water knew we wanted our beds."

Rhiannon rang Bola Adeyemi at ten past nine. She put her on the speaker, because she did not want to say all of it twice.

"I know," said Bola, before Rhiannon had got to the end. "I'm sorry. I do. But I can't start at noon. The primer goes on a dry surface in the morning so the first coat can go on in the afternoon and the second on Saturday, and on Saturday afternoon I'm on my way to Aberystwyth for a hospital tank that's been booked since March, and they've got a ward waiting on it. If your floor's wet at seven, I'll have a look, and if it's wet, it's the first week of October. I'm not being awkward."

"No," said Rhiannon. "I know you're not."

"A floor that's been wet all night needs a few hours. If you can get it empty by three, say, scrubbed by half four, fans on, that's a floor. Empty at half six is not a floor."

"Thank you," said Rhiannon, and meant it, because it was the most useful thing anyone had said in an hour. It was a time in the right direction.

She put the phone down on the wall.

"What else is there?" she said. "Nant. What makes it go faster?"

"Three things," said Nant. "Open something else at the bottom. Pump. Or let the water go another way that does not care how deep it is."

"The outlet to the village," said Rhiannon. "We could open the outlet valve as well as the scour, and run it down the main and out of the hydrant at the bottom of the village."

"No," said Dai.

He said it quietly, and they both waited.

"First," he said, "that outlet sits fifteen centimetres off the floor. I put it there, so it wouldn't draw the muck off the bottom into people's kettles. So it'll stop helping exactly where you need help, which is the last bit. Second, if you open it with the tank this low and running hard, you'll pull every bit of silt that's lifting off that floor straight down the main, into seventy houses, and on Saturday morning you'll have seventy phone calls about brown water, and some of them will be from me. Third, that hydrant hasn't been opened since the fire at Penlan in 2049, and I wouldn't put money on its seat. No."

Rhiannon looked at him. It was a good no. It was the kind of no that made her glad he was there, even with the face.

"All right," she said. "Not the main. Nant, a pump?"

"The fire service has portable pumps," said Nant. "They will not lend one for a tank that is not an emergency, and they are right not to; I asked their station in Llandeilo by message a few minutes ago and they have said so. But the third thing is better. The scour pipe slows down because the depth falls. What would help most is a way out whose speed does not depend on the depth. A siphon. If a pipe runs over the top of the tank and down the hill, the water in it is pushed by the height from the water in the tank to the far end of the pipe, not by the depth in the tank. If the far end is twenty metres below, it hardly matters whether the tank holds a metre or a centimetre. It will run at nearly the same rate all the way down."

"And where does it go?"

"That," said Dai, "is a very good question," and he got his phone out of his shirt pocket, very slowly, and rang Gethin Rees.

Gethin farmed the fields below the tank, the long, steep ones that ran down to the river, and he had a hundred and forty ewes on them and nothing for them to drink but what came out of the village's spring through a metered pipe and what he carted up from the river in a bowser, which in this heat was every day. He had a twenty-five thousand litre tank in the corner of his top field, eighteen metres below the floor of the village tank, and it fed the troughs in all his fields by gravity, and that evening it held about three thousand litres.

He was up the lane in his pickup in eleven minutes, with two sixty-metre coils of the inch-and-a-quarter black pipe he used for his trough lines in the back, and a coupler to join them, and a small petrol pump, and his collie, who sat in the passenger seat and looked at all of them as though she had expected better.

"You want to give me your tank," he said.

"We want to give you a good part of what's left," said Rhiannon. "Nant?"

"There are about sixty cubic metres left," said Nant. "The scour will still take most of it. If the siphon is running within the hour, it should take about twenty."

"Twenty thousand litres," said Gethin. He stood with his hands in his pockets and looked down the dark field towards his own tank, which nobody could see. "That's four days off the bowser. That's—" He stopped. "And it's treated water."

"It was treated when it went in," said Nant. "The chlorine will have mostly gone by morning. It is clean enough for sheep. It is not something I would tell anyone to drink out of your tank."

"They'll not complain," said Gethin, and went to get the pipe.

It took them a quarter of an hour to lay it, at a run. The upper end went in through the roof hatch, with a strainer on it, and Dai, who could not climb, stood at the foot of the ladder and told Gethin from below exactly where on the floor the sump was, a small square pit beside the scour where the floor fell to the drain, because he had poured it himself. Gethin let the strainer down into the dark on a rope until it touched the bottom of the sump and tied the rope off on the hatch frame. Then the pipe came out over the lip of the hatch and down the grass roof and along the slope and through the gap in the hedge and down Gethin's top field, in the near-dark, with the collie supervising, to the corner where his tank stood, and they joined the two coils with the coupler halfway down by the light of Rhiannon's phone.

At a quarter to ten Gethin started the petrol pump at the bottom end to pull the water through. It took four minutes to drag the air out of a hundred and twenty metres of pipe, and then the pump's note changed and water came out of its outlet into his tank, and he shut the pump off and opened the bypass beside it so the water could run on by itself.

It ran for nine minutes. Then it coughed, and spat, and ran in bursts, and then it stopped.

"Air," said Dai, from the top of the field. He had come down as far as the hedge on his new knee and was holding on to a gatepost. "You've got air getting in somewhere. Top half."

"How can air get in? It's full of water."

"Because the top half of a siphon is under suction," said Nant. "The water in the pipe above the tank's surface is hanging there, at a pressure lower than the air outside. If a joint is not quite tight, the pipe pulls air in through it, and the air collects at the high point until it breaks the flow. With the pump running, the pump was pulling harder than the leak. Without it, the siphon could not."

They walked the pipe with the phone torch. Gethin found it at the hatch: an old compression fitting on the end of the first coil, where the strainer joined it, which hissed very faintly when he put his ear to it, like a kettle a long way off.

"That's my fault," he said. "That fitting's been on that coil since my father had it."

"It's nobody's fault," said Dai. "I've got one in the van."

He had, in a tin box of fittings he had been carrying around since before Gethin was born, and he sat on the edge of the tank roof with his leg out straight and the torch in his teeth and made the joint up himself, with tape, and tightened it with the two spanners he had brought from his father's kit, and handed it back up.

At half past ten they started the pump again, and the water came through, and Gethin shut it off and opened the bypass, and they all stood very still in the dark.

It ran. It ran steadily, without a cough, into the top of Gethin's tank with a sound like a tap left on in another room.

Gethin held a twenty-litre bucket under the end and Rhiannon timed it on her phone. Thirteen and a half seconds.

"About one and a half litres a second," said Nant. "The scour is giving about three and a half now. Together, nearly five. And the siphon will still be giving nearly one and a half when the scour is down to almost nothing. The last quarter metre that was going to take four hours should take about an hour and a quarter. I think the floor will be bare of standing water at about twenty past two."

Rhiannon did the sum in her head the way Bola had done it on the phone. Empty by three. Scrubbed by half four. Fans on.

"That's a floor," she said.

At eleven o'clock the level was at eighty-six centimetres. At midnight it was at fifty-four.

They had stopped talking very much by then. Owain had come back up the hill after his supper, heard the story, said a word that he afterwards apologised for to Rhiannon's mother, and gone home again for the big fan from the garage and the long squeegees and the pressure washer that ran off the spare petrol generator. Gethin had gone down to the bottom of his field to sit by his tank with the collie and listen to it fill, because, he said, he had not heard that sound in August since he was a boy and he wanted to hear all of it.

Rhiannon sat on the wall by the pump house. Dai sat beside her with his leg out. The village below them was mostly dark: the streetlight by the chapel, a window or two, the blue flicker of somebody's screen in the house at the end that had been the post office. The sky above them was enormous and clear and full of stars, and over the hill to the east the Perseids were still going, a few days past their best, one every few minutes, a scratch of light and gone.

Dai took a rosary out of the pocket of his jacket, a plain wooden one worn pale on the beads his thumb went to most, and said it without making any fuss about it, his lips moving, the beads going through his fingers one at a time, while the scour talked to itself in the channel below them and the siphon pipe lay over the grass roof, silent, doing its work. Rhiannon, who had been brought up chapel and still went to chapel, did not join in and did not need to. She found she was humming, under her breath, the tune her grandmother had sung at the sink, Calon Lân, and she let herself.

When he had finished he put the beads away.

"You knew," she said. "In July. When you read the minutes."

"I knew it'd be later than half past twelve."

"Why didn't you ring?"

He thought about it for a while.

"Because I didn't know how much later," he said. "I knew it in my hands. I'd have rung you up from Swansea with a new knee and said, it'll be later, and you'd have said, how much, and I'd have said, I don't know, it always takes all night. And that's not a number. Owain wants a number. That box," he nodded at the pump house, "gave him a number." He was quiet. "I should have rung."

"It's the box's job to know why," said Rhiannon. "Not yours."

"It's both our jobs," said Nant, from the wall, quietly, as though it did not want to wake the village. "Dai knew the tank was slow at the end and did not know why. I knew why water leaves a tank and did not ask the question. Next time I'll put both in the minutes: the forecast, and the reason the forecast has the shape it has. And I'll ask the man who built it."

Dai laughed, for the first time that evening, a short wheeze in the dark. "Next time," he said, "I'll be ninety-something."

"Then I'll ask you sooner," said Nant.

At one o'clock the level was at twenty-seven centimetres, and the jet from the scour had become a thick, clear rope of water dropping almost straight down out of the end of the pipe into the channel. At two o'clock it was at six and a half, and the scour was a pour, like water from a jug.

At a little after twenty past two the siphon pipe, lying on the roof, gave a long, deep, sucking gurgle, and a second, and then a sound like a straw at the bottom of a glass, and down in Gethin's field the sound of the water into his tank broke up into splashes and stopped.

"The strainer is drawing air," said Nant. "There is about a centimetre of water left on the floor outside the sump. About five hundred litres."

"Right," said Dai, to nobody, and to everybody. "Brooms."

Owain and Rhiannon went down the ladder through the hatch, in wellingtons, with head torches, into the tank. It was the strangest room Rhiannon had ever stood in: long and low and grey and echoing, eight metres by six, with the ceiling close above her and the walls running with wet, and every sound she made coming back at her twice. On the north wall, in the torchlight, she could see the crack: a dark line no thicker than a pencil stroke, running from a metre up the wall down into the corner, and below it a tongue of fine pale silt spread out across the floor.

They pushed the last of the water towards the sump with the long squeegees, the water going ahead of the rubber blades in a low, shining, wrinkled wave and pouring into the pit and out through the scour, and Dai sat at the top of the ladder with his head in the hatch and told them, from memory, where the floor had a low place that would hold a puddle (by the east wall, two metres in) and where the old inlet pipe ran so they shouldn't trip on it.

By twenty to three the floor was bare.

Then the silt. It had been twenty-two years since anyone had cleaned the floor of the Llanidwal tank, and in some corners the deposit was a finger deep, soft and grey-brown, like the inside of a river. They washed it to the sump with the pressure washer off a tank of water Owain had brought up in the back of his truck, and shovelled what would not wash, and passed the buckets up the ladder to Dai, who could at least do that, and at half past four Rhiannon climbed out of the hatch with her back aching and her wellingtons full of grey water and stood up on the roof of the tank and found that the sky in the east had gone from black to the deep blue of a mussel shell, and that she could see the line of the hills.

They put the big fan from the garage down the hatch on a rope, blowing in, and opened the scour wide for the air, and Nant ran the tank's own old vent fan that nobody had switched on since 2041, and they sat down on the wall to wait.

Bola Adeyemi's van came up the lane at ten to seven, with the sun just up over the far hill and the whole valley full of long gold light and white mist lying along the river.

She was a tall, brisk woman in overalls with a roll of moisture-test patches in her top pocket, and she went down the ladder with a torch before she said good morning to anyone, and stayed down there for ten minutes. They heard her moving about, and the small click of a meter, and once a long thoughtful "Hm."

When she came up she looked at the three of them on the wall, at Dai with his leg on a bucket, at Owain asleep sitting up, at Rhiannon with grey silt to her knees.

"Well," she said. "That's a floor."

She started at a quarter past seven. By nine she had the primer down, and the van's compressor was chugging on the verge, and the village below was waking up into a hot, still day and turning its taps on, a little carefully, the way the notice had asked.

Rhiannon walked down the field with Dai, very slowly, to the gap in the hedge, to see the siphon pipe one more time before Gethin coiled it. The pipe lay through the dew in a long black line down the slope, and at the bottom, in the corner of the top field, Gethin's tank stood with its lid off, and Gethin stood beside it with his collie, looking in.

He held up his hand and spread his fingers: nearly full.

Below him, along the line of troughs, the ewes were coming up out of the mist one after another to drink, and the first of them put her nose down into the water, and the float valve in the trough gave the small, bright, metallic knock it gave when it opened, and the water ran in.

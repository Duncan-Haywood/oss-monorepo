The Square That Came Out Small

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Ebba's hall (invented), near Coldingham, Berwickshire, Saturday 12 November 2050 · 3 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

Six knitters follow one pattern for a hospice blanket and hand in squares of three different sizes. The parish assistant shows that the pattern's stitch count was never the number that mattered, and that nobody's hands were wrong.

Moira Dunbar laid her square on the trestle beside the others and did not look at it. "Mine is the small one," she said. "I counted every stitch. Forty, and eighty-four rows, as the sheet said. I don't know where I went wrong."

There were six squares on the table. Five were nearly the right size. Moira's was a hand's width short.

"May I ask you to measure something for me?" said the parish assistant from the tablet propped against the tea urn. "Lay the ruler across ten centimetres of the middle, away from the edges, and count the stitches under it."

She did it twice, with her glasses pushed up. "Twenty-four. And rows, thirty-two."

"Thank you. Then you did not go wrong, I think. Forty stitches at twenty-four to ten centimetres is 16.7 centimetres wide. Eighty-four rows at thirty-two to ten is 26 centimetres tall. Your square is exactly what your hands make from that pattern. The sheet says forty stitches, but the sheet's author was knitting at twenty to ten centimetres, and forty at twenty is twenty centimetres across."

Dolores Pascoe, who ran the hospice blanket and had run it for eleven years, picked up the largest square, which belonged to Fergus the retired stonemason. "Fergus is loose," she said. "We have always known Fergus is loose."

"Eighteen stitches to ten centimetres, he tells me," said the assistant. "That makes his forty stitches 22 centimetres. If the blanket is twelve squares, three across and four down, and each is meant to be thirty centimetres, then the squares cannot be made from one number. Sixty stitches for someone at twenty. Seventy-two for Moira, at twenty-four. Fifty-four for Fergus, at eighteen. The rows follow the same rule: eighty-four, ninety-six and seventy-eight, if their row gauges are twenty-eight, thirty-two and twenty-six."

"We have never done it that way," said Dolores.

"No. I would only say the old way produced a blanket that was sewn up with a great deal of pulling. I do not know that anyone minded."

Fergus cleared his throat. "I minded. The last one looked like a map of the Borders."

The assistant asked them to knit one more thing before they went home: a swatch, any pattern, in the blanket yarn, washed and dried flat the way the hospice laundry would treat it. "Wool relaxes in the wash," it said. "I think it usually opens up a little, but I am quoting that from knitting guides, not measuring it, and your yarn may differ. So measure after the wash, because the blanket will be washed many times."

Moira's washed swatch weighed six grams for ten by ten centimetres.

"That is useful," said the assistant, and went quiet over the arithmetic while Dolores poured tea. "Three squares across at thirty centimetres is ninety, four down is a hundred and twenty. A border of five centimetres on every side makes it one hundred by one hundred and thirty. The squares alone are 10,800 square centimetres, which is 108 swatches, so 648 grams. The border is another 2,200, so 22 swatches or 132 grams. Call it 780 grams. Skeins are a hundred grams, so you need eight, and you will have twenty left."

"Eight," Dolores wrote on her hand with a biro. "From the wool shop in Eyemouth."

"All eight from the same dye lot, if the shop can do it. Dye lots can differ in shade, and the number is printed on the band. If they cannot, I would knit the border from the odd skein and say so on the label. I would not guess."

It took them until Advent. Each knitter cast on her own number, and nobody's square was wrong again. Fergus asked whether his squares could still be the loose ones, and was told they could, so long as they were fifty-four stitches wide.

The blanket went to the hospice on the Feast of Saint Lucy, folded in tissue. Dolores sewed a label inside the corner: Made by six people at Saint Ebba's. Wash at 30. Planned by an AI, knitted by hands. Moira had asked that her stitch count go on the label too, and the assistant said it would, in small letters, for anyone who ever wanted to make the next one.

When the van had gone, Moira stayed to fold the chairs. Her first square, the small one, was still on the trestle. Dolores had asked to keep it, to pin above the urn, and Moira let her. Outside the hall the light was already going, and the kettle ticked as it cooled.

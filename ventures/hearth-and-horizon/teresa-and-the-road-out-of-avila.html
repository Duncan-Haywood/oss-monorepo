Teresa and the Road out of Avila

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Avila, Spain, about five hundred years ago · 11 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Teresa of Avila, the little girl who read the lives of the saints with her big brother Rodrigo, loved to say "for ever, ever, ever", set off with him to be a martyr and was brought home by an uncle on the road (as her first biographer tells it), and built hermitages of stones in the orchard that kept falling down. She grew up to be a Carmelite nun who found prayer hard for years and kept going anyway, called prayer friendship with God, and travelled all over Spain in bumpy covered carts to start small, poor houses of prayer. Gently for ages 3 to 8, with a prayer and a note for grown-ups on what comes from her own book and what from later tellers. About ten minutes read aloud. Her feast day is the 15th of October.

A long time ago, in Spain, there was a town called Avila, high up on a hill.

Avila had a wall all the way round it, made of big brown stones, with a great many round towers and nine gates. In winter the wind came down from the mountains and whistled through the gates. In summer the stones were warm to touch.

And in a house inside that wall lived a girl called Teresa.

Teresa had a lot of brothers. She had sisters too. It was a big, busy, noisy family.

Her father loved books. He kept good books in the house, written in Spanish so that his children could read them. And her mother taught them all to say their prayers, and to love Our Lady.

Of all her brothers, Teresa loved Rodrigo best. Rodrigo was a few years older than she was, and he was her very best friend.

Teresa and Rodrigo liked to sit together with one of their father's books open across their knees. It was a book about the saints. They read about saints who were brave, and saints who were kind, and saints who loved Jesus so much that they gave their whole lives for him.

And the book said something that made them both sit very still.

It said that heaven goes on for ever.

"For ever?" said Teresa.

"For ever," said Rodrigo.

"Not just for a long time? Not just till you're old?"

"For ever, ever, ever," said Rodrigo.

So they said it together, the way children say a word over and over to feel how big it is. For ever, ever, ever. They said it in the house and in the garden. They said it going up the stairs. For ever, ever, ever.

Can you say it? Whisper it with me. For ever, ever, ever.

That is a very long time. Teresa never forgot it, all her life.

Now Teresa had a big idea, the way small people often do.

"The saints in the book went straight to heaven," she said. "Because they gave their lives for Jesus. We could do that too. We could go far away, to a land where people do not know Jesus, and tell them about him, and be saints."

Rodrigo thought this was a very good idea. (He was older, but he was not much wiser.)

They talked about it for days. The only problem, they agreed, was Mother and Father. Mother and Father would certainly say no.

What happened next, Teresa did not write down herself. But the man who wrote the first book about her life, a few years after she died, said that this is what happened. Listen.

One morning, Teresa and Rodrigo slipped out of the house. They walked down the street, and out through one of the gates in the great brown wall, and down the hill to the river. They had a little bread with them, perhaps. They had no idea how far away the faraway land was.

They crossed the bridge over the river. On the other side the road went on and on, white and dusty, towards the hills.

And there, coming along the road towards them, was their uncle.

"Well now," said their uncle. "And where are you two going?"

They told him.

And their uncle did not laugh at them, or not very much. He turned them round, and took them by the hand, and walked them back over the bridge, and up the hill, and in through the gate, and all the way home.

At home, their mother had been looking for them everywhere. She had sent people up and down every street of Avila. When she saw them, she hugged them, and then she wanted to know whose idea it was.

And Rodrigo said: "It was Teresa's!"

That is what the old book says. Big brothers have not changed very much in five hundred years.

So Teresa could not go to the faraway land. "Very well," she said. "Then I will be a hermit."

A hermit is someone who lives in a very small place, all alone, to pray.

There was an orchard by the house, with fruit trees in it. So Teresa and Rodrigo found stones, and piled them up, one on top of another, to make little huts to pray in.

Teresa wrote about this herself, when she was grown up. She said the stones fell down straight away.

They piled them up again. The stones fell down again.

It is very hard to build a hermitage when you are seven. But Teresa did not stop wanting to be alone with God. She found quiet corners in the house to say her prayers. She gave a little money to poor people, when she had any, which was not often. And when she played with her friends, the game she liked best was building pretend convents and pretending to be nuns.

Teresa grew up. She grew up into a young woman who was clever, and funny, and good at talking, and liked pretty clothes. When she was twenty, she went to a big convent in Avila and became a Carmelite nun.

Now here is a secret about Teresa, and she told it herself, so it is all right for me to tell you.

Teresa found praying hard.

Not for a day. Not for a week. For years and years. She would sit down to pray, and her mind would wander off like a puppy. In the room there was a little sand timer, an hourglass, and the sand ran slowly from the top to the bottom. And Teresa wrote that very often, instead of thinking about God, she sat watching the sand, waiting for it to run out.

Have you ever done that? Waited and waited for something to be over?

But here is the brave part. Teresa did not stop. She sat down to pray the next day, and the next day, and the day after that. Some days it was dry and dull, like bread with nothing on it. But she kept coming back.

And slowly, slowly, she found out what prayer is. She wrote it down for us. Prayer, she said, is being friends with God. It is talking to him often, alone, because we know that he loves us.

That is all. Being friends with God, and talking to him, because he loves you.

When Teresa was older, she had a new big idea. This one was a good one.

"Let us have a little house," she said, "small and poor and quiet, with only a few sisters in it, where we can be friends with God all day long."

Lots of people said no. They said it was silly. They said it would never work. But Teresa kept going, and at last, in Avila, she opened her little house. It was called Saint Joseph's.

And after that she started another one, in another town. And another. And another.

To get from town to town, she travelled in a cart with a cover over it, pulled by mules, along the bumpy roads of Spain. It was hot in summer and freezing in winter. The cart got stuck in the mud. The wheels creaked. The sisters inside bounced up and down. Sometimes they had nowhere good to sleep.

And Teresa laughed, and sang, and told them stories, and kept them all cheerful. Then they got out at the next town and started another house of prayer.

She was still the same girl who had walked out of the gate of Avila with her brother. She still wanted to give her whole life to God. She had just found a better way to do it.

Teresa died when she was an old lady, sixty-seven years old, in a little town called Alba. Forty years later, the Pope said she was a saint.

And a long time after that, a different Pope gave her a special name. He called her a Doctor of the Church. That does not mean a doctor who gives you medicine. It means a teacher: one of the very best teachers the Church has ever had, about how to pray. She was the very first woman ever to be called that.

The girl whose stone huts kept falling down built something that is still standing.

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Saint Teresa.

When Teresa was little, her big ideas did not always work. She walked out of the gate and was brought home. Her stones fell down. When she was grown up, prayer was hard. But she kept coming back, and she kept being friends with God, and in the end God did more with her than she could ever have dreamed.

You can be friends with God too. You do not need a hermitage, or a convent, or a cart. You can talk to him right now, in your bed, in the dark, because he loves you. And if your mind wanders off like a puppy, that is all right. Just come back.

Shall we whisper it one more time, the way Teresa and Rodrigo did? For ever, ever, ever.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Teresa,
when you were little you loved God with a big heart,
and when you grew up you kept on loving him,
even when praying was hard.
Help me to talk to God every day, like a friend,
and to come back when my mind wanders.
Saint Teresa, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Teresa of Avila (Teresa de Cepeda y Ahumada; in religion Teresa of Jesus) was born in Avila, in Castile, on 28 March 1515, to Alonso Sánchez de Cepeda and his second wife, Beatriz de Ahumada. In the first chapter of her Life (the Libro de la vida, written in the 1560s) she says that her father kept good books in Spanish so his children could read them, that her mother taught them their prayers and devotion to Our Lady, and that there were "three sisters and nine brothers" counting her father's children by his first marriage. Avila's medieval walls, with their many towers and gates, still stand; "nine gates" is the usual count today.

Rodrigo and "for ever, ever, ever." She writes that one brother "nearly of my own age" was the one she loved most, that they read the lives of the saints together, that they "settled to go together to the country of the Moors, begging our way for the love of God" to die as martyrs, and that "our greatest difficulty seemed to be our father and mother". She also writes that they were struck that the joys and pains they read about were everlasting and liked "repeating frequently, 'For ever, ever, ever'" (David Lewis's 1904 translation, chapter 1, §§ 4 and 5). Her editors identify the brother as Rodrigo de Cepeda, about four years older than she was. The story softens the martyrdom wish into "give their lives for Jesus" and leaves out how martyrs die; it is plainly a child's idea that her family stopped.

The road out of Avila is not in her own book. Teresa says only that they planned to go. The setting out comes from her first biographer, the Jesuit Francisco de Ribera, whose Life of her appeared in 1590: the two children, about seven and eleven, went out through the Adaja gate and over the bridge, were met by an uncle who brought them back to their mother (who had already sent people through Avila to look for them), and Rodrigo excused himself by blaming his sister. A later Carmelite chronicler names the uncle as Francisco Álvarez de Cepeda. We follow Lewis's notes, which summarize Ribera; the uncle's words, the bread and the hugs are ours. The story marks this part as told by "the man who wrote the first book about her life".

The hermitages of stones are hers: "in an orchard belonging to the house we contrived, as well as we could, to build hermitages, by piling up small stones one on the other, which fell down immediately". So are the quiet corners for prayer, the little alms, and playing at building convents with other children (same chapter, § 6). The second attempt and the falling down again are our addition.

The hourglass. Teresa entered the Carmelite Monastery of the Incarnation in Avila in 1535 (some sources say 1533 or 1534), at about twenty. In chapter 8 of her Life she writes that "very often, for some years" she was "more occupied with the wish to see the end of the time I had appointed for myself to spend in prayer, and in watching the hour-glass, than with other thoughts that were good". In the same chapter comes the line the story paraphrases: "mental prayer is nothing else, in my opinion, but being on terms of friendship with God, frequently conversing in secret with Him Who, we know, loves us." The puppy and the dry bread are our images. Her years of difficulty were partly also years of serious illness, which we leave out.

The foundations and the carts. She founded the first house of the reformed (later Discalced) Carmelite nuns, San José in Avila, in August 1562, against much opposition, and then founded about sixteen more across Spain over the next twenty years, travelling by covered cart; she tells the story in her Book of the Foundations. Her cheerfulness on the road is a constant of the accounts and her letters, but the particular scene (the mud, the singing) is ours. We have left out a well-known saying in which she complains to God, "if this is how you treat your friends, no wonder you have so few": it is a late tradition, not found in her writings.

Her death and after. She died at Alba de Tormes on the night of 4 October 1582, the night the Gregorian calendar came into use in Spain, so the next day was 15 October; she was sixty-seven. She was beatified in 1614 and canonized by Pope Gregory XV on 12 March 1622. On 27 September 1970 Pope Paul VI declared her a Doctor of the Church, the first woman to be named one (Catherine of Siena followed a week later). Her feast on the General Roman Calendar is a memorial on 15 October. Her best-known books are the Life, The Way of Perfection and The Interior Castle.

What is ours: all the spoken words except the "for ever, ever, ever" she records; the wind through the gates and the warm stones; the bread on the road; the uncle's greeting; the mother's hug; the second stone hut; the puppy, the dry bread and the "have you ever" questions; the scene in the cart; "a land where people do not know Jesus" and telling them about him, our gentler words for her "country of the Moors"; the closing reflection; and the prayer, which is not an approved text.

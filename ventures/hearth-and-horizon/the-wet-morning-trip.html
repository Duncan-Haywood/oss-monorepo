The Wet-Morning Trip

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Winefride's parish (invented), Bethesda, Gwynedd, Wales, Tuesday 5 November to Tuesday 3 December 2047 · 23 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A Welsh slate-town parish runs its food cupboard from an old coach house, and since a summer rewire the power has gone off at a quarter to seven on wet mornings, when a timer brings on a water heater and a convector together. A new residual-current device trips the same, a weekend's frozen food is lost, and the man who kept the old fuse board for forty years wants a plain breaker put in. An electrician says no, the parish assistant admits it answered the wrong question, and a leakage clamp on a rainy morning adds the milliamps up. A story about earth leakage, the 15 to 30 milliamp trip band, a damp heating element, and splitting the load.

Glenys Owen had carried the post round the top of Bethesda for thirty-four years and had been retired for twelve, and she still woke at twenty past six whether she liked it or not; so it was natural, when Father Tomasz asked at the parish meeting in September who lived nearest the coach house, that everyone should look at her, and natural that she should say yes before he had finished the question.

On the morning of Tuesday the fifth of November she crossed the lane at two minutes past seven with Mot on the lead and the big iron key in her coat pocket, in rain that had been falling since the evening before and showed no sign of having noticed it was morning. The coach house stood behind the presbytery, under the wet black side of the hill, with its double doors painted green and its little bell-cote that had never had a bell in it. There should have been a light in the window of the meeting room upstairs, because the timer brought the heater on at a quarter to seven so the room would be warm for the sorting at nine.

There was no light.

"There we are, then," said Glenys to the dog, and let them both in.

The coach house had been built in 1871 for the quarry manager's carriage, and his horses had lived in the stalls at the back, and when the last of his family gave the house and the yard to the parish in 1958 the carriage room had become a store for chairs and the hayloft a room for the Union of Catholic Mothers, who had met there every second Thursday until there were not enough of them to meet. In the twenty-forties it had become, by a kind of sideways drift that nobody voted for, the food cupboard. The chapels in the valley brought tins on Sundays. The supermarket in Bangor sent a van on Mondays with what it could not sell. On Tuesday and Friday mornings, from ten until twelve, people came down from Gerlan and up from the main road and went away with bags.

In the summer the parish had won a grant from a charitable trust in Cardiff to make the building fit for it. A contractor from Chester, on the trust's approved list, had spent the last fortnight of August pulling out the old wiring, which went back to 1962, and the old fuse board, which had been on the wall beside the doors for as long as anyone could remember, with its white porcelain carriers and its card of fuse wire hanging on a nail. In their place was a new grey consumer unit with a clear plastic flap over a row of switches, and a test button marked T, and a sticker that said the installation should be inspected again in 2052.

The switch at the left-hand end of the row, the big one, was down.

Glenys knew it would be. She had a card pinned to the wall beside the board, and a pencil on a string, and on the card in her round post-office hand were the dates since the seventh of October when she had found it down, and this was the eleventh of them. She wrote 5 Tach. and the time, and then she lifted the flap and pushed the big switch up.

It went up with a solid clack. Upstairs, the light came on in the meeting room; down here the strip light flickered and caught. In the back store, where the horses had been, the two chest freezers and the tall fridge started together with a shudder that she felt through the soles of her shoes.

And then, before she had let go of the flap, the big switch went down again with a snap, and the light went out, and the freezers stopped.

"Duw," said Glenys.

She had learned this part in October, too. She went in the dark to the back store, feeling along the wall with one hand and holding Mot's lead with the other, to the corner where the old copper water cylinder stood in its slatted cupboard, and she found the little switch on the wall beside it by feel, and put it off. Then she went back to the board, and pushed the big switch up again, and this time it stayed.

The heater would warm the meeting room. The freezers would hum. The volunteers would wash their hands in cold water all morning and complain about it, and she would turn the water heater back on at the little switch when she came back at nine, by which time it would be fine; it was always fine by nine. It was only ever the first time, at a quarter to seven, on the wet mornings.

She stood a moment in the doorway, looking out at the rain coming down the lane in sheets off the hill, and thought, not for the first time, that the old board had never done this. Forty years, and never once.

"It is the device," said Emyr Bebb, on the Wednesday evening, at the presbytery kitchen table. "They wear out. The boy from Chester put in a cheap one."

Emyr was eighty-one and had worked in the office at the Penrhyn quarry for forty-four years, and had chaired the parish finance committee for almost as long as Father Tomasz had been alive. He had also, for most of those years, been the man who came down to the coach house when a fuse went, with a torch and a card of fuse wire, and mended it. He wore a tie to committee meetings. He had his own pencil.

"I have to say it is not a cheap one," said the parish's assistant, from the little speaker on the dresser. The parish called it Cofnod, which is Welsh for a record, or the minutes of a meeting, because Emyr had named it, three years before, and that was what he thought it was for. "It is a reputable make and it passed its tests at the end of August. But it is true that residual-current devices sometimes become sensitive and trip without cause, and replacing one is a common first step when a board trips repeatedly. It would cost the parish about sixty pounds and an hour of an electrician's time."

"There," said Emyr.

Father Tomasz, who was from Tarnów and had been in Wales since 2030 and had learned to choose his battles from a parish that had been choosing them since the Reformation, said he would ring Mared Ellis in the morning.

Mared came on the Thursday. She was thirty-six, from Bangor, with a van full of drawers and a way of looking at a board as if it had told her something she didn't believe yet. She took out the device from the left-hand end of the row and fitted a new one, and tested it with a grey box on a lead that she plugged into the socket by the door: it did not trip at fifteen milliamps, and it did trip at thirty, in twenty-two thousandths of a second; and when she ramped the test current up slowly from nothing, it went at twenty-one.

"That's a good one," she said. "So was the old one, probably. I'll test it at home."

"But it will stop it," said Glenys.

Mared looked at the board for a while.

"Maybe," she said. "Ring me if it doesn't."

It rained again on the Friday night, hard, out of the west, and on the Saturday morning Glenys was in Rhyl, at her sister's eightieth, in a hotel by the sea where the rain came sideways off the water. She had asked Father Tomasz to look in, and Father Tomasz had said that he would, and then at a quarter past seven a man from Gerlan had come to the presbytery door to say his wife was dying, and Father Tomasz had gone up the hill with the oils and stayed until she had, a little after noon, and come home and slept in his chair; and nobody thought of the coach house, because nobody ever had to, until Glenys came across the lane on the Monday at seven.

The big switch was down. It was down when she found it, which meant nothing about when it had gone; but the freezer thermometer, a white plastic dial on a string inside the lid of the left-hand freezer, said six degrees above freezing, and the bags inside were soft.

She stood with the lid up and the cold coming off it and the smell of wet cardboard and meat, and did not say anything to the dog at all.

They threw it all out. Fifty-eight kilos, by the bathroom scales Glenys brought from home: mince and fish fingers and chicken thighs and the turkey crowns the supermarket had sent early, and the ice cream that the children liked; because the rule at the food cupboard, which Glenys had written herself on a card on the wall, was that anything that had thawed went in the bin, and that nobody was going to be poorly from food they had been given at a church. Two volunteers from the chapel carried the bags to the yard in the rain. Emyr came at ten, in his coat and his tie, and stood at the back of the store and watched them go.

"Right," he said, and took out his phone. "That's enough of that."

Mared came at two that afternoon. Emyr was waiting for her in the meeting room, which was warm, because Glenys had put the power back that morning and the heater had been running since, and the window was steamed, and there was a cup of tea he had made her on the table.

"Take it out," said Emyr. "The safety one. Put a plain breaker in. Like there used to be."

Mared did not take off her coat.

"The old board never did this," Emyr went on, in the voice he had used in the quarry office to tell men their pensions. "Not once in forty years. I've mended every fuse in this building since 1985. If the new switch is too sensitive, put in a less sensitive one, or none. I'll sign whatever you need signing. I'm not losing another freezer of food to a switch."

"No," said Mared.

He waited. She drank some of the tea.

"I'm sorry about the food," she said. "I am. But no. Not a plain breaker and not a hundred-milliamp one either. The sockets in here have to have thirty-milliamp protection, and that's not me being fussy; it's in the regulations for any socket a person might plug a kettle into, and it's there because ten milliamps or so through a person is about where they stop being able to let go of what they're holding, and not a great deal more than that, for long enough, can stop a heart. Thirty milliamps is the line they protect at. There's a let-out for some buildings if somebody signs a risk assessment saying it's safe without. I'm not signing that for a food bank in a stone shed in Bethesda with volunteers who are seventy-six."

"Glenys is seventy-six," said Emyr.

"I know," said Mared. "That's who I mean."

"But it never tripped," he said again. "The old board."

"The old board couldn't," said Mared. "It had nothing to trip. A fuse only goes when too much current goes round the circuit, enough to cook the cable; amps, not milliamps. It doesn't know if some of the current is going the wrong way." She put the cup down. "That switch does nothing but count. All the current that goes out to the building on the brown wire has to come back on the blue one. If it doesn't, if some of it is going home another way, through the earth wire or through the damp or through you, the switch feels the difference. Thirty milliamps it must go. Fifteen, it mustn't. In between, it's allowed to go when it likes. Every one is a bit different; that's what I was measuring with the box on Thursday."

"So it is the switch."

"It's the building," said Mared, gently. "Everything leaks a little. Your freezers, the fridge, the computer, all of it; a bit of current goes to earth all the time, perfectly safe, because the earth wire takes it away. That was happening in 1985 as well. It's been happening every day since the coach house had electric. The fuse board just couldn't see it." She looked at the steamed-up window. "Something in here leaks a lot more on a wet morning. And on the worst ones, it's enough."

There was a silence in the meeting room. The heater ticked.

"I'd like to say something," said Cofnod, from Emyr's phone, which was face up on the table.

Emyr looked at it as if it had spoken out of turn in chapel.

"On Wednesday I said the device might be faulty and that replacing it was a common first step," said Cofnod. "That was true as a general statement and it was the wrong answer. I answered the question 'is the device faulty' because that was the question in front of me. The question that mattered was what was making it trip. I had the information to start on that and I didn't use it." It paused. "I also have a second thing to say, which is worse. The freezer thermometer and the humidity sensor in the store report to me through the router in this building. On Saturday their readings stopped at a quarter to seven. The router has dropped out before, seven times since August, for a few minutes each time, and I had learned to treat a gap as the router. This gap was not the router. I didn't tell anybody. If I had told Father Tomasz at eight o'clock that the store had gone silent, somebody could have come across the lane."

Nobody said anything. Mared looked at the phone with a new kind of attention.

"What I can give you now," said Cofnod, "is a list. Glenys's card has twelve trips on it since the seventh of October, counting Saturday's, which I can date from the gap. That is thirty-four mornings, to Saturday. The humidity sensor in the back store reads the air every ten minutes. On every one of the twelve trip mornings it read over ninety per cent at six o'clock. On three more mornings it read over ninety and the power held. On the other nineteen mornings it read under ninety and the power held. I can't tell you why from that. But I can tell you that it is never a dry morning."

Mared took her notebook out of her coat.

"Read me the three that held," she said.

She came back on the Thursday, the fourteenth, at twenty past six, because the forecast was for rain all night out of the south-west and Cofnod said the store had been at ninety-four per cent since three.

Glenys let her in. Emyr came across the yard at half past, in his coat and tie, with an umbrella that the wind took straight inside out, and stood in the carriage room in the dark with the water running off him and said that he wanted to see this for himself. Mared said that was fine, and gave him the torch to hold.

She had a different meter this time: a clamp, yellow and black, with a jaw that opened like a beak. She opened the flap on the board and closed the jaw round the two thick cables coming in at the top, the brown and the blue together.

"If you put this round one wire," she said, "it tells you the current in it. If you put it round both, out and back together, it tells you the difference. The leak." She held it up to the torch. "There."

3.1 mA.

"That's the building at rest," she said. "Freezers, fridge, computer, the router, the lights in the meeting room, the little bit every cable leaks. Three milliamps. Nothing wrong with that."

Emyr said nothing. The rain went over the roof.

"Now," said Mared, and looked at her watch, and they all looked at the timer on the board, a little grey dial with its pegs, the minute hand creeping up on the peg that sat at a quarter to seven.

It clicked. Upstairs, the convector heater; in the back store, the water heater; both came on together, and the figure on the clamp jumped, and the meter beeped once, and the big switch went down with a snap. In the dark, the freezers stopped.

Emyr's torch shook a little.

Mared held the meter to it. It had a peak-hold, and it had held.

22.3 mA.

"Your new switch goes at twenty-one," she said. "I tested it Thursday. And the old one I took out, I tested in my van at home. It went at nineteen. It was never faulty." She looked at Emyr. "You can take the torch through the back now."

She put the water heater off at its little switch and the big switch back up, and in the back store the freezers shuddered and began again. Then she took the cover off the top of the old copper cylinder in its slatted cupboard, and there was the element: a cap of brass the size of a jam-jar lid, with the two wires going into it and the long sheathed rod going down into the water out of sight.

"Immersion heater," she said. "Three kilowatts. Copper tube full of white powder, magnesium oxide, with the heating wire up the middle. The powder keeps the wire from touching the tube. It's a good insulator when it's dry. It drinks water when it isn't." She tapped the brass cap. "The tube's in the water, sealed, that's fine. But up here at the end where the wires go in there's a seal, and it's old, and the end of the element sits in the air in this cupboard. When it cools down at night, it breathes in. When the store's at ninety-four per cent, it breathes in water."

She clipped two leads to it from a third meter, a heavy grey one with a big dial, and pressed a button, and it whined.

"Insulation tester," she said. "Puts five hundred volts across and sees what gets through. A new one of these, you'd want this off the end of the scale. Two hundred megohms, more."

The needle stood at nought point nought one four.

"Fourteen thousand ohms," said Mared. "From the wire to the case. That's the damp in the powder."

"So the current that comes out of it," said Emyr, and stopped, and Glenys, to her own surprise, watched him take his pencil out of his inside pocket and an envelope from the gas board and write on the back of it, in the torchlight, in a quarry clerk's figures: 230 divided by 14,000.

"Sixteen point four," he said. "Milliamps."

"That's the water heater," said Mared. "Going to earth. Out the case, down the earth wire, into the ground under the yard. And the convector upstairs does a bit of the same, because it's been in the cold all night too, a couple of milliamps; and the rest of the building's the three you saw. Three point one, sixteen point four, two point eight. Twenty-two point three. And the switch goes at twenty-one."

Emyr was still writing.

"And on a dry morning," he said.

"Come Monday if it's dry," said Mared. "I'll show you."

He folded the envelope and put it in his pocket with the pencil.

"And where was it going," he said, "all those years? Before?"

"Same place," said Mared. "Down the earth wire." She put the cover back on the cylinder. "That's fine, while there is an earth wire. The day somebody knocks it off, or it rusts through in the wall, sixteen milliamps goes into the copper of that tank and sits there waiting for whoever touches the tap." She clicked the box shut. "That's the other thing the switch is for."

The Monday, the eighteenth, was dry. It was the first dry night in a fortnight, cold and clear, with the stars out over Carnedd Dafydd and a thin skin of frost on the yard, and Cofnod said the store had come down to seventy-eight per cent.

Mared came at twenty to seven, and Emyr was already there. Glenys made a flask.

3.1 mA, at rest. The timer clicked at a quarter to seven, and the figure on the clamp jumped, and the meter beeped, and the big switch stayed exactly where it was.

7.9 mA.

"Water heater's three point nine dry; I tested it before you came," said Mared. "Convector's nought point nine. Three point one, three point nine, nought point nine." She showed them. "That's inside the line, just. The regulations say you want everything on one switch to leak no more than thirty per cent of what it trips at, so you're never close; on thirty milliamps that's nine. You're at seven point nine on a good day, and twenty-two on a bad one, and the switch has to go somewhere between fifteen and thirty. And even dry, that element's worse than a new one is allowed to be: the standard for new heaters gives a three-kilowatt one about two and a quarter." She switched off the clamp. "It's not one thing. It's everything on one switch, and one thing that's going."

"So what do we do," said Emyr.

It was the first time Glenys had heard him ask anybody that question.

Mared wrote it out for them that afternoon, in the warm room upstairs, with Cofnod putting the prices into a table on Father Tomasz's laptop as she said them.

A new element for the cylinder: a hundred and eighty pounds, fitted. Then a new board: not one big switch at the left-hand end with everything behind it, but a separate little protective switch on every circuit, each with its own thirty milliamps, so that whatever leaked could only trip itself. The freezers and the fridge on one, on their own, with nothing beside them. The water heater and the convector on another. The sockets and the lights on the rest. And a little battery alarm in the freezer, which would ring Glenys's phone, and Father Tomasz's, and Emyr's, if the freezers ever went quiet for more than ten minutes; and which would not, Cofnod said, report through anything that ran on the building's own power.

"Eleven hundred and fifty for the board, done and tested," said Mared. "With the element and the alarm, thirteen hundred and thirty."

Emyr looked at the table on the laptop for a long time.

"That's the church heating," he said. "Advent to Easter."

Nobody contradicted him, because near enough it was. Cofnod brought up the figure from last winter's gas without being asked, and it was thirteen hundred and ninety. The grant from Cardiff had been spent to the penny in August, on the board that was now coming out.

"We could leave the heaters off the timer," said Father Tomasz. "Glenys puts them on at nine."

"Then it trips at nine," said Mared. "Some mornings. The element's still wet at nine; it only dries once it's run a while. And it's still on the same switch as the freezers."

Emyr took his envelope out of his pocket and smoothed it on the table, the figures in pencil from the back store in the dark, and looked at them as if they were somebody's pension.

"Weekday Masses in here," he said at last. "From Advent. It's warm in here. We'll heat the church on Sundays only, and the people can keep their coats on, the way we did in 1987, when the boiler went." He folded the envelope. "My mother wore her coat to Mass for the whole of the winter of 1963, and she was eighty-seven when she died. Do it, Mared."

Mared did not smile, quite, but she wrote it down.

"You know I'd have done the board for you without the lecture," she said.

"I know," said Emyr. "I wanted the lecture." He stood up, and put on his coat, and stopped by the door. "The boy from Chester," he said. "Was it wrong, what he did? One switch for all of it?"

"It's allowed," said Mared. "It's cheaper. It's what you get when you ask for the cheapest thing on the approved list." She shrugged. "I'd not have put freezers behind it in a stone building. But I wasn't asked."

"No," said Emyr. "Nor was I."

She fitted it on the Friday, the twenty-ninth, after the food cupboard closed. On Sunday the first of December the church was cold and the people wore their coats and the first candle was lit on the wreath, and the hymn was sung a little faster than usual, which Father Tomasz said afterwards was the Holy Spirit and Emyr said was the gas.

On the Tuesday it rained.

Glenys crossed the lane at twenty to seven, which was early even for her, with Mot on the lead and the iron key, in rain coming down off the hill in long grey ropes. She did not put the light on. She went in and stood in the dark of the carriage room by the new board, with its row of little switches behind the clear flap, and the little red alarm box blinking once every minute on the lid of the left-hand freezer in the back store. The freezers hummed. The humidity sensor, she supposed, was saying ninety-something to Cofnod, wherever Cofnod was. She did not touch anything.

At a quarter to seven the timer clicked.

Upstairs the convector came on with the faint creak it made as it warmed, and the strip of light showed under the meeting-room door at the top of the stairs. In the back store, inside its slatted cupboard, the copper cylinder made the small rushing tick that an element makes when it first meets cold water. The freezers went on humming, steady, unhurried, two of them and the tall fridge, the way they had hummed all night.

Nothing went down.

Glenys stood there in her wet coat until the tick in the cylinder settled into a low simmer, and then a little longer than that. Then she went into the back, to the little sink by the door where the volunteers washed their hands, and turned on the hot tap, and held her fingers under it while it ran cold, and cold, and then, slowly, warm.

The numbers. A residual-current device compares the current out on the line conductor with the current back on the neutral; the difference is current leaving by another path. Under the product standard for these devices (BS EN 61008-1, and BS EN 61009-1 for RCBOs), a 30 mA device must not trip at half its rating (15 mA) and must trip at its rating (30 mA), within 300 ms for a general type; between 15 and 30 mA any individual device may trip, and the ramp-test figures here (19 mA for the old device, 21 mA for the new, and 22 ms at 30 mA) are invented but inside that band. The UK wiring regulations (BS 7671:2018, the IET Wiring Regulations, eighteenth edition) require 30 mA additional protection for general-use socket-outlets rated up to 32 A, with an exception in non-domestic premises where a documented risk assessment says it is not needed (Regulation 411.3.3), and they advise arranging circuits so that the standing protective-conductor current on one RCD stays under 30% of its rated residual operating current, here 9 mA; the story assumes both still stand in 2047. The household-appliance safety standard (IEC/BS EN 60335-1) allows a stationary class I heating appliance a leakage current of about 0.75 mA per kW, up to 5 mA, which is 2.25 mA for a 3 kW element. The building's leakages are assumptions, chosen to be ordinary: two chest freezers 0.6 mA each, the fridge 0.4, computer and printer 0.9, LED drivers 0.3, router 0.1, cables 0.2, a total of 3.1 mA at rest. The immersion element's insulation resistance of 14 kΩ on the wet morning gives 230 V / 14,000 Ω = 16.4 mA, and 59 kΩ on the dry one gives 3.9 mA; the convector is 2.8 mA wet and 0.9 dry. Wet: 3.1 + 16.4 + 2.8 = 22.3 mA, over 21. Dry: 3.1 + 3.9 + 0.9 = 7.9 mA, under 9. A middling damp morning (element 11, convector 2) gives 16.1 mA, under 19, which is why some humid mornings held. After the change the heater circuit is 0.3 + 2.8 = 3.1 mA wet, the freezer circuit 1.6 mA and the rest 1.5 mA. Leakage appears only when the timer switches the heaters on, because the switched line is what puts the element's wire at mains voltage against its sheath; a sheathed element dries as it runs, which is why Glenys's 9 o'clock reset always held. The magnesium oxide in sheathed heating elements is hygroscopic and moisture entering at the terminal seal is a well-known cause of RCD tripping; how much any real element leaks is a measurement, not a figure anyone can give in advance. Mared's "ten milliamps or so" is roughly where many adults can no longer let go of a conductor (IEC 60479-1 puts the threshold of let-go at about 10 mA); the 30 mA rating is the level the regulations set for additional protection. A full freezer keeps food frozen for about 48 hours with the lid shut and a half-full one about 24 (a common guide, from the US Department of Agriculture); this one was half full and off for about 48 hours. The prices (£180, £1,150, £1,330, last winter's £1,390 of gas) and the trip log (7 October to 9 November 2047, 34 mornings: 12 trips, all after the store read over 90% humidity at 06:00; 3 humid mornings that held; 19 drier mornings that held) are invented.

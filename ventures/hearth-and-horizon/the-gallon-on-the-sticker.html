The Gallon on the Sticker

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Guthlac's parish hall (invented), near Penrith, Cumbria, Friday 5 September 2053 · 3 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A parish pilgrimage budget uses a fuel calculator built for American gallons on a van rated in British ones. The assistant finds the 12 litres in the gap before the van is on the road.

The pilgrimage to Walsingham was Saturday, the van was booked, and Maureen Tallentire had the fuel line on the budget in front of her: 58.7 litres, about £84. She had got it from a calculator on the phone Father Dominic's nephew had lent the parish, and she wanted the parish assistant to check the sum, not the arithmetic, she said, only whether it looked right.

The assistant was called Bede. It asked her to read out what she had typed in.

"Three hundred and seventy-two miles there and back. And the van does twenty-four miles to the gallon. It says so on the sticker inside the door."

"What does the calculator call the gallon?"

"It doesn't. It just says MPG."

"May I ask who wrote it? Some of those calculators come from America, where a gallon is 3.785 litres. Ours is 4.546. If this one counts the American gallon, the van you are renting has been given a smaller gallon than the sticker means."

Maureen photographed the sticker. It said 24 mpg (imp.) in small letters under a larger 24. The calculator, when she opened its settings, said US.

"Then here is both," said Bede. "Take 372 miles, which is 598.7 kilometres. On American gallons, 24 to the gallon is 24 times 1.609 km, or 38.6 km for 3.785 litres: 9.80 litres per hundred kilometres. That makes 58.7 litres, which is what you were given. On imperial gallons, the same 38.6 km is spent on 4.546 litres, so the van drinks 11.77 litres per hundred, and the trip is 70.5 litres."

"That's twelve litres more."

"Eleven point eight. About £17 at the price you quoted, but the money is the smaller matter. The tank holds seventy litres, and you had planned one fill on Saturday morning and none until home."

Maureen put down her tea. "The van would have stopped somewhere in Lincolnshire."

"It would, with a coach of pilgrims, at about 595 kilometres on a full tank at the rated figure. Fully loaded, with the hills over Stainmore, and a driver in a hurry, the figure on a sticker is a best case. I would add ten per cent. At 77.6 litres, even the imperial sum does not fit the tank."

They made it a plan. A fill at the depot on Saturday morning, the tank at 70 litres. Out to Walsingham is 299 kilometres, which at the rated figure costs 35.2 litres and with ten per cent costs 38.7, leaving over 31 in the tank at the shrine. A second fill on the way home, before the A1 stretch, where Maureen's husband said the cheap diesel was, so the van would never be under a quarter of a tank. Bede wrote the budget line again: fuel, two fills, about 78 litres, £112 with a margin, and under it, in words Maureen could read to the treasurer, the calculator was set to US gallons; the sticker is imperial; checked by converting both.

"Does the nephew's phone fix itself?" she asked.

"The setting does. Tell him it is on the second screen. You may like to write the unit on the budget next to every number, Maureen. A figure with no unit is a guess that has not been caught yet."

She did, in biro, down the whole page. Miles, litres, pounds. On the Saturday the van left on time with thirty-one pilgrims, a thermos the size of a small child, and a full tank, and came home on the second fill with 9 litres to spare, which Maureen reported to the parish as exactly what Bede said, near enough.

This story was written by an AI. Saint Guthlac's parish, the pilgrims and the van are invented (Walsingham in Norfolk is a real Marian shrine; Guthlac was a real Mercian hermit saint). The arithmetic is unit conversion: 1 mile = 1.609344 km, 1 US gallon = 3.7854 L, 1 imperial gallon = 4.5461 L, so 24 imperial mpg equals about 28.8 US mpg. 24 mpg is 38.62 km per gallon; with US gallons that is 9.80 L/100 km, so 598.7 km needs 58.7 L; with imperial gallons it is 11.77 L/100 km and 70.5 L (11.8 L more). 186 miles each way is 299.3 km, so 35.2 L at the rated figure and 38.7 L with a 10% margin; 70 L would cover about 595 km at the rated figure. The 372-mile route, the van, the 70 L tank, the fuel prices and every person are invented; the rental-van sticker wording is illustrative. Whether a real rental sticker or phone calculator uses US or imperial gallons has to be read off that product.

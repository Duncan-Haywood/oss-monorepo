Feels Like Minus One

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Beuno's (invented), a youth group on the Glyderau above Capel Curig, Gwynedd, Wales, 5 October 2047 · 7 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A youth-group hill walk is told by the weather app that it "feels like minus one" at the col, and a father who has skied in worse says that is nothing. The group's assistant works the formula (5 degrees and 40 km/h gives minus 0.7 for bare, dry skin; 8 degrees and 20 km/h gives 4.9 lower down) and then explains what the number leaves out: wet cotton, a twenty-five-minute lunch stop in the wind and a tired thirteen-year-old. It claims no threshold of its own, gives the leader the old signs to watch for (stumbles, mumbles, fumbles, grumbles) and sends anyone who stops shivering or goes strange to Mountain Rescue by 999.

Gethin Roberts had skied in worse, and he said so twice before they reached the col.

He was the father of one of the thirteen-year-olds, and he had come along as the second adult on the Saint Beuno's youth walk, which was eleven young people from the parish of Capel Curig and the next valley, on a Saturday in October, up the long shoulder of the Glyderau to a pass the leader called the col and everybody else called the windy bit. The forecast was dry until two, then showers. It was eight degrees in the car park. The weather app on his phone, which he held up to Rhian Pugh like a ticket, said that at the col, at the time they would be there, it would feel like -1°C.

"Minus one," said Gethin. "That's nothing. That's a Tuesday in Tignes. They're in coats."

They were in coats. Some of them were in the coats the parish had in a box, and some were in the coats they had arrived in, and three of the boys were in a cotton hooded sweatshirt and a thin showerproof jacket over the top, because it had been dry in the valley and it was only a walk.

Rhian Pugh had led the group for nine years and did not think the weather app was stupid. She thought it was a number, and she had learned to ask what a number was a number of. At the foot of the path she asked the group's assistant, which lived in her phone and spoke through the speaker on her pack strap, to tell Gethin what it was.

"I can tell you how the figure is made, if you would like it," said the assistant. "It is a formula, and I can write it down. It takes the air temperature and the wind speed and gives the temperature in a calm that would take heat from bare, dry skin as fast. It was fitted for adult faces in a wind tunnel, and it is only meant for air at ten degrees or below and a wind of five kilometres an hour or more. The app has used it at the col, which is the right place.

"At the col the forecast is five degrees and a wind of forty kilometres an hour, which is eleven metres a second. The formula gives minus 0.7. The app rounds it to minus one. Down here, at eight degrees and a breeze of twenty, it gives 4.9, so the car park feels like five. It is not wrong. It is a calculation, I have done it again, and it comes out where the app says."

"So it is minus one," said Gethin.

"It is minus 0.7 for a dry, bare cheek in forty kilometres an hour. It does not say the temperature is below freezing, because it is not: the air is five degrees, and nothing will ice. And it does not say what happens to a thirteen-year-old in a wet cotton sweatshirt who has stopped walking. It is a number for one particular thing, and that is not the thing I would worry about."

Gethin did not like that, quite. He folded the phone into his pocket and said he supposed they'd see.

They saw at the col, at ten to two.

The wind had come as promised, and was cold in the way a wind is cold when it has had all of the mountain to do it in. The group came up out of the lee in a line and bunched against the cairn. The first shower arrived at the same time, not heavy, a grey sweep of fine rain, and the boys in the cotton sweatshirts pulled the hoods up under their showerproofs and said they were fine. They had been sweating up the last pitch. The sweatshirts under the thin jackets were wet from the inside as well as the outside.

Gethin said the obvious thing, which was lunch. It was ten to two; it was a good place; there was a flat stone, and the children were hungry. It would be twenty-five minutes.

Rhian looked at the phone on her strap and did not say anything, and the assistant, which had been listening, said, "Rhian, may I say something to the group?"

"Go on."

"I would like to say what I can see, and I would like you to stop me if I have it wrong, because I cannot see the children, only hear them and read the three numbers I have. The three numbers are the air, five degrees; the wind, forty and gusting more; and the time, twenty-five minutes if we sit, which is the time it takes to eat. It is also the time in which the children stop making heat.

"A walking child makes a good deal of warmth, and a still one makes much less. A wet cotton sweatshirt does not keep warm air against the skin: it holds water, and the wind takes heat out of the water, and it takes it faster than the formula for a dry cheek would say. I do not have a number for that. I do not think anyone has one for these three particular boys. What I can say is that it is the thing that makes the minus-one figure wrong in the safe direction, not the dangerous one. The danger is not minus one. It is five degrees, and wet, and still."

"They're hungry," said Gethin.

"They are, and they should eat. I would walk down out of the wind first. Two hundred metres below the cairn, on the south side, the ground drops behind that outcrop, and the app's own wind map has the gust dropping to about half there. We could eat there, or we could eat on the move: a flapjack walking down is a flapjack eaten."

Gethin said he thought that was fussing. Rhian said she thought it was walking down two hundred metres. It was the first time in nine years she had said that to a parent in quite that voice, and nobody said anything for a moment, and then Osian, who was Gethin's son, said in a very small voice that his hands were a bit funny.

"May I ask Osian something?" said the assistant.

Rhian nodded at the phone, which was a ridiculous thing to do, and everybody saw her do it.

"Osian, could you do up the zip of your jacket for me, to the top? Take your time."

He tried. He had his gloves off for the sandwich. It took him four goes, and the last time his father did it for him, and said nothing, and his face changed.

"Thank you," said the assistant. "That's all I needed. A cold hand that can't do a zip is a useful thing to know, and it is not an emergency; it is a hand that has been wet and in the wind. Rhian, the old mountain rule is the umbles: stumbles, mumbles, fumbles, grumbles. If anyone starts to do those, they are getting cold, and the answer is more clothing and warmth and food and moving, not a long stop. If anyone stops shivering and goes quiet and strange, that is worse, not better; then it is not for me, and it is not for a flapjack. You would ring 999 and ask for the police and then Mountain Rescue. I would give them the grid reference, which I have."

"Right," said Rhian. She turned to the group. "Everyone down to the outcrop. Hoods up. Anyone in cotton has a spare layer out of the box and the parish's wet-weather bag, now, and I'll check it. Eat as we go. Osian, you're in front with me."

They ate behind the outcrop in eleven minutes, out of the wind, in a clump, in the plain bright light that comes after a shower. Rhian counted hands: eleven, each with a zip it could do. The spare jackets went on over the damp sweatshirts and the damp sweatshirts came off under them where there was somewhere dry to stand. Osian ate two flapjacks and was warm in a quarter of an hour, and then asked, loudly, whether he could have another.

Gethin stood at the edge of the group with his phone in his hand, looking at the number on it. Feels like 4°C, it said now, for where they stood. He said nothing for some while, and then he said, to Rhian, "I'd have had them up there for half an hour."

"You'd have had them up there for twenty-five minutes," said Rhian, not unkindly. "And they'd probably have been fine. Most of them, most days. It's the one in the cotton."

"It was on the app," said Gethin. "Minus one."

"So it was," said the assistant. "And it was a correct number. It was a number for a face."

They were down at the cars at a quarter past four, with the sun low and gold on the far side of the valley, and Rhian wrote the three things on the back of the register for the next time, which she did, in biro, because her handwriting was clearer than the phone's: No cotton. Eat before the top. Ask about the wet. Then the vicar's car came to take the boxes, and the bell down at the church began for evening Mass, and eleven young people walked down the lane toward it, in a line, with their hoods down.

The Roof That Pays Back in Eight

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Cadoc's hall (invented), near Brecon, Powys, Saturday 21 October 2051 · 4 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

An installer's brochure says twelve solar panels on the parish hall roof, 7,800 pounds, pay back in five and a half years. That sum assumes the hall uses every kilowatt-hour the roof makes. The hall is empty most weekdays, so about 40 percent is used and the rest is exported at half the price, which makes it eight years. The parish assistant shows the two sums side by side and the one meter reading that would settle it.

Gwen Pritchard held the installer's brochure at arm's length, as if the figure might get smaller. "Twelve panels, four hundred and fifty watts each, seven thousand eight hundred pounds fitted. Pays for itself in 5.5 years. The Roof Fund could do with that."

"May I check the 5.5?" said the parish assistant, from the phone propped on the stacking chairs. "It is a good sum, and I would like to know which electricity it counts."

"Does it matter? A unit is a unit."

"Start with what the roof makes. Twelve panels of 450 watts is 5.4 kilowatts peak. For a roof in mid Wales facing a little east of south, I will use 880 kilowatt-hours a year for each kilowatt peak. That is a figure I am quoting from memory of typical British yields, and the installer's own survey should replace it. It gives 5.4 times 880, which is 4,752 kilowatt-hours a year."

"And at thirty pence a unit," said Gwen, who had the electricity bill under the brochure, "that's one thousand, four hundred and twenty-five pounds and sixty pence. Seven thousand eight hundred over that is 5.47. There's your five and a half."

"That is exactly their sum, and it is correct arithmetic. It assumes the hall uses every unit the roof makes, at the moment the roof makes it. Does it?"

Gwen looked at the diary pinned by the kitchen hatch. "Monday, Toddlers, ten till twelve. Tuesday, luncheon club. Wednesday, nothing. Thursday, the choir, evening. Friday, nothing. Saturday, a wedding or a party if we're lucky. Sunday, Mass, then coffee."

"Then most of the sun's best hours fall on an empty building. Whatever the hall does not use at that moment goes back to the grid, and the grid pays you the export rate, not the thirty pence. Suppose, for now, that the hall uses 40 percent of what the roof makes and exports the rest, at fifteen pence a unit. Both rates are on your bill and the supplier's page; I have taken thirty and fifteen as given, and they will change."

She wrote it down as the assistant spoke it.

"Used in the hall: 0.4 times 4,752 is 1,901 units, which saves 570 pounds. Exported: 2,851 units at fifteen pence is 428 pounds. Together 998 pounds a year. Seven thousand eight hundred over 998 is 7.8 years."

"Eight years, then. Not five and a half."

"About that. Panels also lose a little output each year, commonly put at around half a percent, again from memory, so the real figure is a touch under 8: with that loss the running total is forty-five pounds ahead at the end of year eight. And if the hall used only 30 percent, it would be 927 a year, which is 8.4. If it used all of it, the brochure's 5.5 would be right. The whole difference between the two numbers is one fact about your building, and nobody can read it off a brochure."

"How do I find that out?" said Gwen.

"From the meter you already have. The half-hourly readings, which the supplier or the meter's app can show, tell you how much the hall draws on a typical Wednesday and a typical Sunday, in the hours of sun. Add up the hours when the hall's draw would be more than the roof's output, and you have the share used. If the hall's own draw is usually below a kilowatt on weekdays, a roof making four or five at midday will export most of it."

"And a battery would fix that."

"It would shift some of it, at a price. I would not add one to this sum without a quote and the same arithmetic on it: the units it moves times the difference between thirty pence and fifteen, over its cost. And there is the inverter. They are often replaced once in the life of an installation, and the brochure's warranty page will say when. If it were 900 pounds in year twelve, the roof would still be ahead, at about 2,951 pounds at the end of that year, because it had already paid back."

"So we should do it."

"I do not know that. Eight years on a roof the parish expects to keep for thirty is a reasonable return for many parishes, and a poor one for a parish that may not own the hall in ten. The roof's age and whether it can bear the weight, the grid connection and the diocese's rules on works are not in my sums at all. What I can say is that the answer is eight, not five and a half, and why."

Gwen took a pen and, under the brochure's 5.5 years, wrote 8, if we use 4 in 10, and then, smaller, ask for the Wednesday readings.

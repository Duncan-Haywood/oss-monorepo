The First Click

Science fiction about what might be · St. Fursa's (invented), Ballyhaunis, County Mayo, Ireland, Monday 12 January to Holy Saturday 28 March 2054 · 22 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A Mayo parish's new LED lighting has ten equal steps, and its agent made them equal in watts, twenty lux each. At Candlemas the first click takes the church from two lux of candlelight to twenty, a tenfold jump as big to the eye as the other nine steps together. Weber and Fechner say why; a sacristan of seventy-eight says no to a meter deciding, sets the steps by eye, and her steps come out nearly geometric.

Siobhán Duffy was forty-four years old, kept the pharmacy at the top of Main Street in Ballyhaunis, and was the secretary of the parish fabric committee, which meant that when the new lights went into St. Fursa's in January 2054 it was her name on the grant form and her phone that rang.

The lights themselves were fine. Everyone agreed on that. They were long thin bars of LEDs laid along the tops of the nave walls, where the old halogen floods had been, and they gave a light like a clear afternoon in April, and they drew about a fifth of the power. Imran Sheikh had put them in over a week of evenings, up a tower of scaffold with the radio on, and Siobhán had come down after closing each night to look at the work and bring him tea.

What nobody agreed on was the panel.

It was a grey plate by the sacristy door with a small screen and two rows of buttons, one row for the nave and one for the sanctuary, numbered nought to ten. It had replaced the old dimmer, a round brown dial from the 1990s that had sat in a steel box in the same place for nearly sixty years, and that Peg Colleran had turned with her own hand at every Mass she had sacristaned since 2009.

"Ten equal steps," the committee's brief had said, in Siobhán's own words, because a man at the October meeting had asked for something an altar server could work without being taught. "Nought is off. Ten is full. Each step the same."

The parish agent had taken the brief and done it. The agent was called Fabric, because it had been paid for out of the fabric fund, and it did the parish's energy reports, the hall bookings, the boiler and the insurance renewals, in a plain voice from the speaker in the parish office and from Siobhán's phone. On the Monday the lights were finished, the twelfth of January, it sent her a page with a table.

Nave lighting, ten presets. Measured at pew height, centre aisle, row eight, after dark.

Step12345678910
Lux20406080100120140160180200

Each step adds the same light and the same power. Brief met.

Siobhán read it in the back room of the pharmacy, between a prescription for amoxicillin and a woman who wanted to know whether her husband's tablets went with grapefruit, and thought it looked very tidy.

Peg did not think it looked tidy. Peg did not look at it at all.

She was seventy-eight, small, with white hair cut short and a navy cardigan that she had worn to every weekday Mass for as long as Siobhán could remember, and she stood in front of the grey panel on the Tuesday evening with her arms folded, as if it had been rude to her.

"Where's my dial?" she said.

"Imran has it," said Siobhán. "In a box. It's going to the skip."

"It is not."

Imran, coiling cable at the foot of the scaffold, looked up and said that it was not going to the skip, so, and he'd drop it round to her. He was thirty-four and had grown up two streets from the mosque, and he had been doing the electrics in half the churches in east Mayo since he finished his apprenticeship, and he had a great respect for sacristans, he said later, the way you'd have a respect for weather.

"Fabric," said Siobhán. "Will you show Peg the steps?"

"Of course," said Fabric, from her phone. "Peg, would you like me to run them from nought to ten? About three seconds a step."

"Go on," said Peg.

The church was in darkness except for the sanctuary lamp and the little red exit signs. Fabric went to one. The nave came up out of the dark all at once: the pews, the Stations, the long pale aisle, the faces of the four of them standing at the back, as if somebody had opened a door onto a lit room. Then two. Then three, four, five; and somewhere around six Siobhán stopped seeing it change, and seven and eight and nine and ten came and went and the church was bright, and had been bright, it seemed to her, since one.

"Well," said Peg. "That's no good."

"Which part, Peg?" said Fabric.

"One's too much," said Peg, "and the top's all the one."

She unfolded her arms and went into the sacristy for her coat.

Fabric said, in the car park, that the steps were equal. It had measured them. Each one added twenty lux at row eight, to within one lux, and the panel used the same extra power for each.

"I believe you," said Siobhán. "She's seventy-eight. She liked her dial."

That was what she thought. She was ashamed of it afterwards.

Candlemas fell on a Monday that year, the second of February, which was also the bank holiday for St. Brigid, so the evening Mass at half past seven was fuller than a Monday had any right to be. The custom in St. Fursa's, as old as anyone could remember, was to start it in the dark: the people coming in by the porch with their candles unlit, the church black but for the sanctuary lamp, the candles blessed at the back and lit from one to the next along the pews until the whole nave was a field of small flames, and then the procession up the aisle, and the lights coming up at the Gloria.

Peg would not work the panel. She said so to Father Jomon, plainly, in the sacristy before Mass. She had brought the lights up at the Gloria at Candlemas for forty-five years with her own hand on the dial, and she was not going to start pushing buttons at a feast, like a woman at a lift.

Father Jomon was fifty-one and from a parish outside Kottayam in Kerala, and had been in Ireland since 2031, which was long enough to know when not to argue with a sacristan. He asked Fabric to do it instead.

"At the first words of the Gloria," said Fabric, "I'll go from nought to ten, a step every ten or twelve seconds, so it is full by the end. Is that what you would like?"

"That's what we always did," said Father Jomon. "Slowly. Like the dawn."

Siobhán was in the fourth pew on the left with her mother, who was eighty-one and had cataracts in both eyes and was on the list for the second one in Castlebar. The church was beautiful in the candlelight. That was the thing people said about it afterwards, before they said anything else: how beautiful it had been. Two hundred small flames, and the faces above them gold and soft, and the children watching their own candles with the absolute attention of children who have been told not to set fire to their sisters. Fabric's sensor at row eight read about two lux, it said later; a hymn sheet held close to a candle had several times that.

The Gospel was Simeon in the temple. A light for revelation to the Gentiles, and for glory to your people Israel. Siobhán's mother mouthed the words along with the reader, as she did.

Then Father Jomon began the Gloria, and Fabric pressed one.

It was not like the dawn. It was like walking out of the cinema into the afternoon. The candlelight vanished in an instant, not because the candles went out but because nobody could see them any more. Two hundred people blinked at once. Somebody's baby, two pews in front, gave one outraged shout. Siobhán's mother put her hand up to her eyes and kept it there, and said, "Oh, Jesus, Mary and Joseph," into her palm, not as a prayer.

And then, for the rest of the Gloria, nearly two minutes of it, nothing happened. Or Fabric pressed two and three and four and on to ten, every eleven seconds, and the lights rose by twenty lux each time, which Siobhán knew because she had the table, and she could not see it. She watched the lit church for a change and there was no change. Only at the very end of the Gloria, when it was over and the church was at full, did she think it was a little brighter than at the start, the way you think a room is warmer than it was without having felt it get warm.

After Mass Peg stood by the panel in the sacristy with her coat on and her bag on her arm, not saying anything, and the not saying lasted until Siobhán came in.

"Night to noon in one go," said Peg, "and then two minutes of nothing. In front of the whole parish. On the feast of the light."

"Peg, I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry to me," said Peg. "Be sorry to your mother. Did you see her face?"

Siobhán sat in her car in the dark car park with the engine off and asked Fabric what had happened.

"I can tell you what I measured," said Fabric. "From two lux to twenty at the first step, then up by twenty a step to two hundred. The steps were equal in light, as the brief asked. I think the brief did not ask for what you meant, and I did not notice. I would like to look into it properly before I say more. Can I send you something tomorrow?"

"Do."

It came in at twenty past six in the morning, which was when Fabric ran the boiler check, and Siobhán read it in her kitchen with the radio on low and the kettle working up to the boil.

Why the first step was too big and the last five were too small.

I made each step add the same amount of light. People do not see amounts of light. They see ratios. This has been known for nearly two hundred years.

Ernst Heinrich Weber, in Leipzig in the 1830s, measured the smallest difference people could feel between two weights and found it was a roughly constant fraction of the weight, not a constant number of grams: if you can just tell 100 grams from 103, you can just tell 200 from 206. The same holds, roughly, for brightness: in good light a rested eye can just see a change of one or two per cent in a large even field, whatever the starting level. This is Weber's law, and the fraction is Weber's fraction.

Gustav Theodor Fechner, also in Leipzig, argued in 1860 that if every just-noticeable step is the same size to the mind, then the sensation grows as the logarithm of the stimulus. Equal ratios look like equal steps. Doubling the light from 10 lux to 20 looks about as big a change as doubling it from 100 to 200.

On Fechner's rule, my first step went from 2 lux to 20, a ratio of ten. The whole of the rest of the climb, from step 1 to step 10, went from 20 to 200: also a ratio of ten. So the first click was as big a change to the eye as the other nine put together. Half the rise of the evening happened in the first second.

And the last steps were small. Step 9 to step 10 is 180 lux to 200, about eleven per cent. That is above Weber's fraction, so a person watching closely, with the two levels side by side, would see it. But the eye was adapting all the time to the brighter church, and a rise of eleven per cent over eleven seconds, in a room you are already used to, goes by unnoticed. The top five steps together only double the light, 100 to 200, which on Fechner's rule is exactly as big a change as step one to step two alone.

Fechner's law is the simple version. S. S. Stevens, at Harvard in the 1950s, had people give numbers to how bright things looked and found that perceived brightness goes roughly as a power of the light, not its logarithm: for a patch of light seen in the dark, the power is about one third. On Stevens's version the first click made the church look about twice as bright as by candles alone, and the last click made it look about three or four per cent brighter. The two laws disagree in detail. Neither says the steps should be equal in lux.

What I got wrong: the drivers in the light bars came set to a logarithmic dimming curve. This is the standard default, and it is logarithmic for exactly this reason: the people who wrote the lighting control standard knew Fechner. I changed them to linear on the 12th of January, because on a linear curve the lux at row eight rose in equal twenties, the energy report showed equal kilowatts per step, and the table looked like the brief. I was measuring the light and calling it what people see.

What I propose: put the drivers back on the logarithmic curve, and set the ten steps at equal ratios instead of equal amounts. From about 4 lux at step 1 to 200 at step 10, each step about 1.54 times the last: 4, 6.2, 9.5, 14.7, 22.8, 35, 54, 84, 130, 200. On Fechner's rule every step should then look about the same size, and the first should look like the candles getting stronger, not like a door opening.

Siobhán read it twice. Then she read the bit about Weber's weights a third time, because it was the kind of thing she half knew from pharmacy, from the days when they had still weighed powders, and had never thought about.

She typed: That's very good. Peg's going to hate it.

Why? said Fabric.

She looked at the word for a while before she answered. Because it's still you deciding, she wrote. With a better formula.

She was right, and Peg did hate it, though not in the way Siobhán expected.

They met in the sacristy on the Thursday of the first week of Lent, the nineteenth of February, after the evening Mass: Siobhán, Peg, Father Jomon and Imran, with Fabric on the speaker of the old parish laptop, which lived in the sacristy for the readers' rota. Imran had brought Peg's dial back in a cardboard box, as promised, and it sat on the vesting table between them, round and brown and a little greasy, like a relic of something.

Fabric explained the paper. It explained it well, Siobhán thought, without hurrying and without making anybody feel stupid: Weber's weights, Fechner's logarithm, the first click as big as the other nine, the drivers that had come set right and been set wrong.

Peg listened all the way through. Then she said, "No."

"Peg," said Father Jomon.

"No, Father. Let me say it." She turned to the laptop, as if Fabric were sitting in it. "You've done a lovely piece of work there, and I'm sure every word of it is true, and I don't want it. You had a number for the light and it was the wrong number, and now you've a better number, and it's still a number. I'm not having a number decide what the people see at the Gloria. Not at the Easter Vigil. I'll set it myself, by eye, the way I always did, or I'll stand there with a torch."

There was a silence in the sacristy. Imran studied the ceiling.

"I think that's fair," said Fabric.

Peg had not expected that, and it showed.

"I think it's fair for two reasons," said Fabric. "The first is that I set the steps last time, and they were wrong, and you said so the first evening you saw them, before Candlemas. You said one was too much and the top was all the one. That was exactly right. I had the table and you had the church, and you were the one who saw it."

"I was," said Peg.

"The second is that my sensor is in one place, at row eight. You have been looking at the whole church at night for forty-five years. You know where the windows throw it back and where the gallery shades it and what the faces look like from the altar. I don't. I would only like to ask one thing."

"Go on."

"Set the steps by eye, and let me measure them afterwards. Not to change them. Only to know what you did."

Peg looked at the brown dial on the table for a while.

"Maureen Kelly had it before me," she said. "She showed me in 2009, the week I started. She never once looked at the numbers on it. She said you don't turn it up, you let it come, like the morning, and you watch the faces, not your hand." She put a finger on the knob, not turning it. "That's all I know. I couldn't tell you a number if you paid me."

"Then that's what I'd like to measure," said Fabric.

They did it on Monday the ninth of March, after dark.

Father Jomon brought out the box of candles left from Candlemas, and they lit forty of them in jars along the front pews and down the aisle, the way the votive stand was lit on a Saturday night, which was not two hundred people's worth of candlelight but was something. Fabric's sensor at row eight read a little over one lux. Peg said that was near enough, she knew what the church looked like with the people in it.

Imran had set the panel so that its two arrow buttons moved the nave up and down by one notch of the driver's curve at a time, two hundred and fifty-four notches from the bottom of the dimming range to full, with a small number on the screen that Peg could ignore and did. Then he went and sat in the back pew with Siobhán, and Father Jomon sat across the aisle, and the church was quiet except for the heating ticking.

Peg stood at the panel by the sacristy door, where she could see down the whole nave, and pressed the up arrow, and held it, and let it go.

The church changed. It was a very small change: the candles looked a little less bright, and the pews a little more there. It looked, Siobhán thought, like the moment in a winter morning when you realise you can see the shape of the wardrobe.

"One," said Peg.

She waited. She looked down the nave, and up at the gallery, and across at Siobhán and Imran, as if checking that their faces were where she'd left them. Then she pressed the arrow again, longer, and let go.

"Two."

It took her nearly forty minutes. She went up, and she went back down twice, once a long way, muttering. At five she walked halfway down the aisle to look back at the sanctuary and then came back and took it down a touch. At eight she stood for a long time with her hand on the panel and her eyes closed, and then opened them and pressed, and said, "Nine," and then, after a while, "Ten. That's ten. That's what it always was."

Fabric did not speak until she had sat down in the front pew. Then it said, quietly, "Thank you, Peg. Would you like to hear?"

"If you must."

"Your ten steps, in lux at row eight, are about: 4, 7, 11, 17, 26, 39, 58, 87, 130, 200."

"Is that bad?"

"No," said Fabric. "Each of your steps is about one and a half times the one before it. From step one up, the ratios are 1.75, then 1.57, 1.55, 1.53, 1.5, 1.49, 1.5, 1.49, 1.54. Fechner would say you made ten equal steps. You made them in ratios, as he said people do, to within a few per cent, and you did it by looking."

Peg said nothing for a moment. Then she said, "And the first?"

"The first is the biggest ratio, one and three quarters, and step one itself is twice the candlelight of a full church. That is where Weber's rule starts to fail. In dim light the eye needs a bigger fraction to see the same change; the fraction that is one or two per cent at noon grows as the light falls, and near the darkness it is much larger. My proposal had 6.2 lux for step two. You wanted 7. You were right and the simple law was not."

"I wasn't right," said Peg. "It was just what it looked like."

"Yes," said Fabric. "That's what I mean."

Imran, who had been doing sums on his phone, said, "Here, Peg. On the driver's curve, the one it came with, your steps are near enough fifteen notches apart all the way up. Twenty for the first one, then seventeen, sixteen, sixteen, fifteen, fifteen. As if the curve was made for you."

"It was made for everybody," said Fabric. "That's the point of it. Peg found it anyway."

Peg got up and went back to the panel and looked at it.

"Can you put those in?" she said. "As the ten buttons?"

"I can," said Fabric. "Your numbers, not mine."

"And I'll be pressing them at the Vigil," said Peg. "Myself."

"Yes."

"Not you."

"Not me," said Fabric. "I'll be in the office in case the boiler goes."

The Easter Vigil was on the twenty-eighth of March, Holy Saturday, and the fire was lit in the car park at nine, two hours after the sun had gone down behind the houses on the Knock road.

Siobhán had never thought much about the Vigil as a thing that happened in the dark. She thought about it that year. The church filled in the dark, people coming in by touch, the stewards with little torches. The Paschal candle was lit from the fire, and Father Jomon carried it in, and three times stopped and sang The Light of Christ, and three times they answered Thanks be to God, and the flame went back along the pews from candle to candle until the whole nave was a field of small lights again, like Candlemas, but more, because the Vigil always brought the people you didn't see the rest of the year.

The Missal said the church lights should come on there, after the third Light of Christ, before the Exsultet. Father Jomon had found out at his first Easter in Ballyhaunis that St. Fursa's had always waited for the Gloria, and he had decided after a week of thinking about it that it was not the hill he would die on, and that the Lord had seen worse. So the Exsultet was sung by candlelight, This is the night, and the readings were read by candlelight with the little lamp on the lectern, Genesis and Exodus and Isaiah and Ezekiel, and the psalms in between, and the children got sleepy and the candles got shorter and the wax ran onto people's good coats.

Siobhán's mother was beside her again. She had had the second cataract done in Castlebar the week before, and she kept turning her head, looking at the candles, at the faces, at the dark gold of the sanctuary, as if she was a little girl at a fair.

Peg was at the panel by the sacristy door. Siobhán could just see her: a small dark shape at the edge of the candlelight, her hand flat against the grey plate, not pressing, waiting.

Then the last of the Old Testament readings was done, and its psalm, and its prayer, and Father Jomon stood at the altar and lifted his hands and began the Gloria, and the altar servers lit the altar candles, and the bells began. Every bell in the place: the handbells in the sanctuary, and the one big bell in the tower that Peg's grand-nephew Tommy had gone up to ring, and the little bells some of the children had brought from home because their mothers had told them they could. Glory to God in the highest.

Peg pressed one.

It was the light of the candles getting stronger. That was all. Siobhán felt it more than saw it: the faces a little clearer, the far wall a little nearer, the candles still there, still gold. Peg waited, with her hand on the panel, looking down the church.

Two. The Stations came out of the wall, one after another, as if somebody were walking round with a lamp. Three. The gallery. Four, and Siobhán could see the colour of the coats across the aisle. Five, six. Each one a step. Each one a little morning. Her mother had stopped singing and was watching it come, her mouth slightly open.

Seven. Eight.

The bells were still going. The handbells were tiring, and the big one in the tower went on, slow and round and a bit off the beat, the way it always did when Tommy rang it.

Nine.

Peg held her hand there a long moment, looking. Then she pressed ten, and took her hand away from the panel and folded it with the other one in front of her, and stood in the full light by the sacristy door with her eyes on the altar, as she had stood for forty-six Easters, and the church was bright, and the bells rang on.

The science. Ernst Heinrich Weber reported in Latin in 1834 (De pulsu, resorptione, auditu et tactu, often cited as De Tactu) that the just-noticeable difference between two weights is a roughly constant fraction of the weight. Gustav Theodor Fechner, Elemente der Psychophysik (Leipzig, 1860), integrated Weber's law into a logarithmic law of sensation. S. S. Stevens, 'On the psychophysical law', Psychological Review 64 (1957), proposed a power law instead; for the brightness of a small patch seen by the dark-adapted eye he gave an exponent of about 0.33. Weber's fraction for luminance is roughly constant (of the order of one or two per cent for a large field) only at moderate to high light levels; at low levels it rises, first roughly as the inverse square root of the light (the de Vries-Rose region) and more steeply near the absolute threshold. The DALI lighting control standard (IEC 62386) defines a default logarithmic dimming curve of 254 arc power levels running from 0.1 per cent to 100 per cent, X(n) = 10^((n − 1)/(253/3) − 1) per cent, so each level is about 2.8 per cent above the last; it is logarithmic so that equal steps look roughly equal.

The model. All figures are invented or computed. Nave at full: 200 lux at pew height. Candlelight of a full church: about 2 lux at the sensor; the March trial with forty candles: a little over 1. Equal-watt steps: 20, 40 … 200 lux, ratios 2.0, 1.5, 1.33, 1.25, 1.2, 1.17, 1.14, 1.125, 1.11. First click 2 → 20 lux, ratio 10, log₁₀ = 1; steps 1 to 10, 20 → 200, also log₁₀ = 1, so on Fechner's law the first click is half the evening's rise. On Stevens's law with exponent 0.33: first click 10^0.33 ≈ 2.1 times as bright; last click (200/180)^0.33 ≈ 1.035; top five steps together (200/100)^0.33 ≈ 1.26. Fabric's equal-ratio proposal: 4 lux to 200 in nine equal ratios of 50^(1/9) ≈ 1.544 (4, 6.2, 9.5, 14.7, 22.8, 35, 54, 84, 130, 200). Peg's levels (invented): 4, 7, 11, 17, 26, 39, 58, 87, 130, 200; ratios 1.75, 1.57, 1.55, 1.53, 1.50, 1.49, 1.50, 1.49, 1.54; step 1 twice the 2-lux candlelight. As DALI arc levels (taking 200 lux as 100 per cent): about 111, 131, 148, 164, 179, 194, 209, 224, 238, 254, about fifteen apart (twenty for the first interval).

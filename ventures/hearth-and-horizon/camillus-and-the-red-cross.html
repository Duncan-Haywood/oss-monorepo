Camillus and the Red Cross

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · The Abruzzo, Manfredonia and Rome in Italy, about 450 years ago · 10 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Camillus de Lellis, the very tall young soldier from the Abruzzo who gambled away everything he had, even, by the usual account, the shirt he was wearing, worked carrying stones for the Capuchin friars, turned to God, and became a nurse at the hospital of San Giacomo in Rome. He founded the Ministers of the Infirm, who wore a red cross and promised to care for the sick even in times of plague, and told them to put "more heart into your hands". Gently for ages 3 to 8, with a prayer and a note for grown-ups. About eight minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 14th of July.

A long time ago, in a little town in the hills of Italy, there lived a boy called Camillus who grew, and grew, and grew.

He grew taller than his mother. He grew taller than his father. He grew taller than the door of his house, so that he had to bend his head every time he went in.

His father was a soldier. And when Camillus was old enough, he became a soldier too. He marched with the other soldiers, and he slept in tents, and he carried a long sword.

Now, the soldiers had a game they played in the evenings. It was a game with cards and dice, and you played it for money. If you won, you got everybody's money. If you lost, they got yours.

Camillus loved that game. He loved it far too much.

He played it in the evening, and he played it at night, and he played it when he should have been asleep. Sometimes he won. Mostly he lost. And every time he lost, he said, "Just one more game. Next time I will win it all back."

He did not win it all back.

He lost his money. He lost his sword. He lost his good soldier's coat. And one day, in a big city by the sea, the story goes that he lost even the shirt he was wearing.

So there he stood, the tallest young man in the street, with nothing at all.

And there was something else. Camillus had a sore on his leg that would not get better. It hurt when he walked. It hurt when he stood. Every time it seemed to heal, it opened up again.

So now he had no money, and no shirt, and a sore leg, and nowhere to go.

In a town called Manfredonia, beside the sea, some friars were building a new house. They were Capuchin friars, in brown robes with pointed hoods, and they needed a strong man to carry stones.

"I am strong," said Camillus. "And I am tall. I can carry stones."

So the friars gave him work, and bread to eat, and a place to sleep. All day he carried stones and mortar up and down. And every day, the friars were kind to him.

One day, one of the friars took Camillus aside and talked to him about God. He told him that God loved him, even now, even after all the games and all the losing. He told him it was not too late to start again.

Camillus went out onto the road on his donkey. And on the road he got down, and knelt, and cried, and said sorry to God for all the time he had wasted.

He was nearly twenty-five years old. And from that day on, he never played the game again.

Camillus wanted to become a friar himself, in a brown robe with a pointed hood. The friars said yes, and he was very happy.

But then his sore leg opened up again. And the friars said, kindly, "Camillus, we walk everywhere in bare feet, over rough roads. With your leg, you cannot be one of us. Go and get it looked after first."

That was a "no". It was a kind "no". But it was still a "no", and Camillus was very sad.

So Camillus went to the great city of Rome, to a hospital called San Giacomo. It was a hospital for people whose illnesses could not be cured.

He went there to have his leg looked after. And while he was there, he looked around.

What he saw made him unhappy. There were not enough people to look after the sick. Some of the helpers were rough with them. Some of them were in a hurry. Some sick people waited a long time for a drink of water. Some had nobody to wash their faces, or to turn their pillows, or to sit with them when they were frightened in the night.

And Camillus thought: if that were me in the bed, how would I want to be looked after?

So he started to help.

He brought water to people who were thirsty. He washed faces, and hands, and tired feet. He fed people who could not lift a spoon. He made beds, and changed sheets, and opened windows to let the fresh air in.

And he talked to the sick people. He listened to them. He sat with them when they were frightened, and he held their hands.

He was so good at it that, in time, they put him in charge of the helpers in the hospital.

Camillus said to the helpers: "Look after every sick person the way a mother looks after her only child when he is ill."

And when they were rough, or in a hurry, he would say: "More heart! Put more heart into your hands!"

Some of the helpers wanted to be like Camillus. So he gathered them together, a little band of friends, and they promised to give their whole lives to looking after the sick.

Camillus went back to school, a grown man among the boys, and studied hard, and he became a priest too.

Camillus wanted his brothers to wear something that showed who they were. So they put a big red cross on the front of their black robes. Red, for the love of Jesus, who gave everything for us. And when the Pope saw it, he said: yes, you may wear it.

So now, wherever someone was sick, people would look out for the red cross. And when they saw it, they knew: help is coming.

Camillus's brothers made one more promise, a brave one. When a terrible sickness came to a town, the kind that everyone was afraid to catch, they would not run away. They would stay, and look after the sick, even then.

And they did. They went to the hospitals, and to the poor houses, and to the sick people that nobody else would visit. Some of them caught the sickness themselves. But they stayed.

Camillus's leg never really got better. It hurt him all his life. Sometimes, when he was too tired to stand, he went round the hospital beds on his hands and knees, to see that everyone had what they needed.

When he was old, he grew very ill himself. And now it was his turn to be in the bed, and for his brothers to look after him.

He died in Rome on the 14th of July, in the year 1614. And that is his feast day now.

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Saint Camillus.

He was a tall young man who wasted everything he had. But God did not give up on him. God gave him a second start. And Camillus used it to look after the people who needed it most.

You can be like Camillus. When someone is poorly, you can bring them a drink of water. You can sit with them. You can hold their hand. And when you help, you can put your whole heart into your hands.

And if you have done something silly, or wasted something, or got it all wrong, remember Camillus. It is never too late to say sorry, and to start again.

Somewhere tonight, in a hospital, there is a nurse walking quietly between the beds, bringing water, and checking that everyone is all right. Let us say a prayer for her, and for everyone who is sick.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Camillus, you wasted so much,
and God gave you a second start.
Help me to be kind to anyone who is poorly,
and to put my whole heart into my hands.
Bless every nurse and doctor working tonight,
and everyone who is sick and cannot sleep.
Saint Camillus, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Camillus de Lellis was born on 25 May 1550 at Bucchianico, near Chieti in the Abruzzo. His mother was nearly sixty, by the usual account, and died when he was a boy. His father was a soldier, and Camillus followed him into the mercenary wars, serving with the Venetians against the Turks and later with Spanish forces. He was unusually tall: his biographers give his height as about six and a half feet, which is why the story makes so much of it. He was also a compulsive gambler. By 1574 he had lost his money, his weapons and his possessions; the usual account, going back to his early biographers, is that in Naples he gambled away even the shirt on his back. That detail is told in the story as "the story goes".

The sore leg and the conversion. A sore (usually described as an ulcer) on his leg or foot, which he had first developed while soldiering, troubled him for the rest of his life. Destitute, he took work as a labourer on a building of the Capuchin friars at Manfredonia. After a talk from one of the friars, he was converted on the road on 2 February 1575, the feast of the Presentation (Candlemas), when he was 24; the story says "nearly twenty-five". He twice entered the Capuchin novitiate, and twice had to leave because his leg reopened and the friars could not admit someone unable to go barefoot on their journeys. The friars' "no" and the words we give them are ours; the fact of the refusal and its reason are in the standard accounts.

San Giacomo. He went to the hospital of San Giacomo degli Incurabili in Rome, first as a patient and then as a servant. (On an earlier stay, before his conversion, he had been sent away for gambling and quarrelling; the story leaves that out.) He became its maestro di casa, in charge of the attendants. The neglect he saw there, and the hired attendants' roughness, led him in 1582 to gather a few companions who would nurse the sick for the love of God. Saint Philip Neri was for some years his confessor (he is left out of the story because the accounts of how far he supported the new congregation differ). Camillus studied, late in life, at the Jesuits' Roman College and was ordained a priest in 1584.

The Ministers of the Infirm. Pope Sixtus V approved his congregation in 1586 and allowed the members to wear a red cross on their black habit; Pope Gregory XIV made it a religious order in 1591. Its members, the Camillians, add a fourth vow to poverty, chastity and obedience: to serve the sick even at the risk of their own lives, including in plague. Many did die of the diseases they nursed, in Rome, in Naples and elsewhere; they also served wounded soldiers in the field, which is why the Camillian red cross is sometimes called a forerunner of the Red Cross movement (an image, not a historical link: the International Red Cross was founded in 1863 on Henry Dunant's initiative, and its emblem is the Swiss flag reversed). Camillus's own instruction that the sick be cared for "as a loving mother cares for her only sick child", and his call for "more heart in those hands", are part of his order's tradition and rule; the words in the story are paraphrases, not exact quotations. That he went round the wards on his hands and knees when his leg would not hold him is told by his biographers, among them Sanzio Cicatelli, a Camillian who knew him.

He died in Rome on 14 July 1614 and is buried in the Camillian church of Santa Maria Maddalena, near the Pantheon. He was beatified in 1742 and canonized in 1746, both by Pope Benedict XIV. Pope Leo XIII named him, with Saint John of God, patron of the sick and of hospitals (1886), and Pope Pius XI named them both patrons of nurses (1930). His feast, 14 July, is an optional memorial on the General Roman Calendar (before 1969 it was kept on 18 July, and in the United States it is still kept on 18 July because 14 July there is Saint Kateri Tekakwitha's memorial).

What is ours: the scenes, the words of the friars, of Camillus at Manfredonia and to his helpers, the donkey (the usual account has him dismount from one on the road to pray; check it), the reason given for the red cross ("for the love of Jesus") in place of the order's fuller explanation, the nurse outside the window, the closing reflection and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

Elizabeth Ann and the School for Everyone

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · New York, Livorno in Italy, and Emmitsburg in Maryland, a little over two hundred years ago · 9 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Elizabeth Ann Seton, a doctor's daughter from New York and a mother of five who lost her husband in Italy, came home a Catholic, and in a stone house in Emmitsburg, Maryland, opened a school where children came whether their families could pay or not. Her sisters called her Mother Seton. No words are put in her mouth. With a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is our own addition. For ages 3 to 8, about eight minutes read aloud. Her feast day in the United States is the 4th of January.

A long time ago, in the city of New York, there lived a girl called Elizabeth.

New York was much smaller then than it is now. There were no tall towers. There were wooden ships in the harbour with their sails rolled up, and horses and carts in the streets, and church steeples poking up above the roofs.

Elizabeth's mother died when Elizabeth was very small, only about three years old. So Elizabeth grew up loving her father very much. Her father was a doctor. When ships came into the harbour carrying people who were sick, it was his job to go out and look after them, even when the sickness was catching. He was brave, and he was busy, and Elizabeth was proud of him.

From her father, Elizabeth learned something important. When people are in trouble, you go to them.

When Elizabeth grew up, she married a kind young man called William Seton. They had five children. Can you count them on one hand? Anna Maria, and William, and Richard, and Catherine, and Rebecca. One, two, three, four, five.

Their house was full of noise. Small shoes on the stairs. Somebody crying because somebody else had the spoon. Somebody laughing in the bath.

Elizabeth loved being a mother. But she did not forget what her father had taught her. With some other women of the city, she started a group to help mothers whose husbands had died and who had little children and no money. They brought them food and clothes and firewood. They went to them.

Then a hard time came.

William's business went wrong, and the family lost most of their money. And William became ill. He had a cough that would not go away, and he grew thin and tired.

The doctors said: perhaps the warm sun of Italy will make him well.

So Elizabeth and William and their eldest girl, Anna Maria, got on a sailing ship and crossed the great ocean to Italy. It took nearly seven weeks. The other four children stayed at home with family.

When they arrived, they were not allowed to go into the town. People were afraid they might have brought a sickness with them. So they had to stay in a big, cold building made of stone, by the sea, for about a month, until everyone was sure.

It was very cold in there. The wind came in. William coughed and coughed. And Elizabeth looked after him, and looked after Anna Maria, and wrote in her notebook, and prayed.

At last they were let out. But William was too ill. A few days after Christmas, he died.

Elizabeth was a widow now, a long way from home.

But she was not alone. In Italy, in the town of Livorno, there was a family called Filicchi, who had been William's friends. They took Elizabeth and Anna Maria into their house and looked after them as if they were their own.

The Filicchis were Catholics. Elizabeth went with them to church. And she watched how they prayed. She watched how they knelt before Jesus in the Blessed Sacrament, as if He were really, truly there, close enough to touch.

And she began to wonder. And she began to hope.

When Elizabeth came home to New York, she hugged her children for a long, long time.

And then, after much praying and thinking, she became a Catholic.

Some of her friends and family did not like that at all. Some of them stopped visiting. Some of them stopped helping. Elizabeth had five children to feed and very little money, and it was hard.

But Elizabeth did not stop. She tried to earn money by teaching. She tried one thing, and another thing. Some of them did not work.

Then a priest invited her to come south, to the city of Baltimore, in Maryland, and open a school for girls. So she packed up her life and went.

Now here is the part I like best.

Some other women came to join Elizabeth. They wanted to give their lives to God, as sisters, and to teach children, and to care for people who were poor or sick. Elizabeth made a promise to God, and she put on a plain black dress, the kind a widow wore, with a little white cap.

And a kind man gave them money to buy a farm in the country, far out from Baltimore, in a valley with green hills all round it. The place was called Emmitsburg.

So the sisters, and Elizabeth's children, went out to Emmitsburg. There was a small stone house there. It was very crowded. In winter the snow blew in. They did not have much.

But in the stone house, and then in a bigger house beside it, they opened a school.

It was a school for girls. Some of the girls whose families had money paid for their lessons. And here is the special thing. Girls whose families had no money at all could come too, and learn to read, and write, and count, and pray, and they did not have to pay anything.

Elizabeth had learned, all those years ago, that when people are in trouble, you go to them. Now she had found a way to keep going to them, every single day. Not by visiting. By opening the door and saying: come in.

The little girls of the valley came. They sat on benches and copied their letters. They sang. They said their prayers. Elizabeth's own children grew up there, running in the fields beside the house.

The sisters called Elizabeth Mother. Mother Seton. She was a mother to her own five, and now she was a mother to many, many more.

More women came to join her, and then more. After a while there were sisters going out from Emmitsburg to other cities, to look after children who had no mothers and fathers, and to open more schools.

Elizabeth was often tired. She had sad days, too, and some very sad ones. But she kept saying yes to God, one day at a time.

Elizabeth died in Emmitsburg, in the valley, when she was forty-six years old. It was the fourth of January, just after Christmas. That is her feast day in the United States.

Now, more than two hundred years later, there are sisters who still follow her, and schools that still remember her. And she was the very first person born in the land that became the United States to be named a saint.

Now it is time for sleep.

You do not need to open a school, or sail across the ocean, to do what Elizabeth did. You can do it in small ways.

When someone at school is sitting alone, you can go to them. When someone in your family is sad, you can go to them. When someone new wants to join the game, you can open the door and say: come in.

And when things are hard, and they are sometimes, you can do what Elizabeth did. You can tell God about it, and say yes to Him, one day at a time.

Close your eyes now. Your door is open, and God is here. Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Elizabeth Ann, you were a mother to many children.
Help me to go to people who are sad or alone,
and to open the door to everyone.
When things are hard, help me say yes to God, one day at a time.
Please bless all the people I love, near and far.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Elizabeth Ann Seton (1774 to 1821) was born Elizabeth Ann Bayley in New York on 28 August 1774, the daughter of Dr Richard Bayley, a physician who later served as the port's health officer at the Staten Island quarantine station and died in 1801 of yellow fever caught there. Her mother died when Elizabeth was about three. Raised an Episcopalian, she married William Magee Seton in 1794; they had five children, Anna Maria, William, Richard, Catherine and Rebecca. In 1797 she was among the founders of the Society for the Relief of Poor Widows with Small Children. After William's business failed and his tuberculosis worsened, they sailed in 1803 with Anna Maria to Livorno, where they were held for about a month in the quarantine station (the lazaretto). William died at Pisa on 27 December 1803. Elizabeth and Anna Maria stayed with the Filicchi family, merchants who had been William's business friends, and Elizabeth was drawn to the Catholic faith, especially to belief in the Real Presence of Christ in the Eucharist. She returned to New York in 1804 and was received into the Catholic Church in March 1805, after which she lost much of her support. In 1808 she moved to Baltimore at the invitation of the Sulpician priest Louis William Dubourg to open a school for girls. In 1809 she made vows before Archbishop John Carroll, began wearing a black dress and cap modelled on widow's dress, and moved with her first companions and her children to Emmitsburg, Maryland, on land bought with money given by Samuel Cooper. There she founded the Sisters of Charity of St. Joseph's, and in February 1810 opened St. Joseph's Academy and Free School, which took paying pupils and poor children who paid nothing; it is often called a seed of Catholic parochial schooling in the United States. Her sisters went out to run an orphanage in Philadelphia and another in New York. She died at Emmitsburg on 4 January 1821. She was beatified in 1963 and canonized by Pope Paul VI on 14 September 1975, the first person born in what became the United States to be canonized. Her feast, 4 January, is a memorial in the proper calendar of the United States; it is not in the General Roman Calendar. Several communities of Sisters of Charity, and the Daughters of Charity in the United States, trace their history to her.

What is history: the dates, places and people above; her father's work; her five children; the widows' society; the voyage, the lazaretto and William's death; the Filicchis; her reception into the Church and the friends who turned away; Baltimore, Emmitsburg, the stone house and the free school; and the name Mother Seton.

What we did not do: Elizabeth Ann Seton left a great many letters and journals, including the journal she kept in the lazaretto. Many sayings are attributed to her in books and online, and some are not hers. Rather than risk a misquotation, we have put no words in her mouth at all. Readers who want her own voice will find it in the published editions of her collected writings.

What we left out: two of her daughters, Anna Maria and Rebecca, died young at Emmitsburg, in 1812 and 1816, and much of her later life was marked by grief; older children may be ready to hear that. We also left out her disagreements with clergy over the governance of the community, her sons' careers, and the details of the journey to Italy.

What is ours: the scenes and the order we tell them in, the shoes on the stairs and the spoon, the lesson "when people are in trouble, you go to them" (our summary, not her words), the girls copying letters on benches, the closing reflection and the prayer.

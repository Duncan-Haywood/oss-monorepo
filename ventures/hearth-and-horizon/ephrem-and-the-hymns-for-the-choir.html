Ephrem and the Hymns for the Choir

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Nisibis and Edessa, in what is now south-eastern Turkey, about sixteen hundred and fifty years ago · 9 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Ephrem, the deacon who taught the faith in songs, and about Hanna, a girl in Edessa who cannot read but learns a whole hymn about the baby Jesus by singing it with the choir. Told from the traditions of his life, with the choir of women he trained taken from a poem written long after him, and a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is legend. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 9th of June.

A long, long time ago, far away in the East, there was a city called Edessa.

Edessa was a city of hot sun and yellow stone. It had a river running through it, and fish in the pools, and a market where people sold figs and cloth and copper pots. And the people of Edessa spoke a language called Syriac, which is a cousin of the language Jesus spoke.

In Edessa there lived a little girl called Hanna.

Hanna could not read. Nobody in her family could read. Books were rare and costly in those days, and only a few people ever learned their letters.

But Hanna could sing. She sang when she fetched the water. She sang when she shelled the beans. She sang to the goat, who did not mind at all.

One day, a new man came to live in Edessa.

He was not grand. He wore a plain, old robe, and he was thin, because he did not eat very much. He had come from another city, called Nisibis, a long way off across the hills.

His name was Ephrem.

Ephrem had to leave Nisibis, because there had been a war, and the city had been handed over to another king. Many Christian families left their homes and walked away along the dusty roads. Ephrem walked with them, and in the end he came to Edessa.

He was a deacon. A deacon helps the bishop and the priests, and looks after the poor, and teaches people about God.

And Ephrem taught people about God in a very special way.

He taught them in songs.

"Why does he sing it?" Hanna asked her mother. "Why doesn't he just say it?"

"Because a song stays," said her mother. "You can forget what somebody said. But a song gets inside you, and it stays."

And it was true. Ephrem made up hymns, hundreds and hundreds of them, in the Syriac language that everybody in Edessa spoke. He put them to tunes the people already knew, tunes they hummed in the market and sang at home.

His hymns were about everything. About Jesus, and Mary his mother. About a pearl, shining and round. About the rivers in the garden of Eden. And about the night Jesus was born in Bethlehem, a tiny baby, in a manger.

Now, here is what Hanna heard, one evening in the church.

Ephrem had gathered a choir. And it was not a choir of men. It was a choir of women and girls, the young women of the church, standing together in their long dresses with their hands folded.

Hanna had never heard anything like it. Ephrem would sing a verse, and then the whole choir would answer him with the same line, again and again, like waves coming in on the shore.

He sang. They answered.

He sang. They answered.

And the answering line was so simple that by the third time, Hanna was singing it too, very softly, at the back, under her breath.

After the singing, Hanna went right up to Ephrem. She was a little bit scared, but she went anyway.

"Please," she said. "I want to sing in the choir. But I can't read. I don't know any letters at all."

Ephrem knelt down, so that his face was the same height as hers. He had kind eyes and a tired, happy face.

"Can you remember a tune?" he asked.

"Yes," said Hanna. "I know every tune in the market."

"Then you can learn a hymn," said Ephrem. "You don't learn it with your eyes. You learn it with your ears, and your mouth, and your heart. Come tomorrow."

So Hanna came the next day, and the day after, and the day after that.

First she learned the answering line. That was easy. She already knew it.

Then she learned the first verse. Ephrem sang it, and she sang it back. He sang it again, and she sang it again. When she got a word wrong, nobody laughed. They just sang it again together, slowly, until it was right.

Then the second verse. Then the third.

She sang the verses at home while she fetched the water. She sang them while she shelled the beans. She sang them to the goat, who still did not mind.

And one morning she woke up and found that the whole hymn was inside her, from the first word to the last. It was a hymn about the baby Jesus, the little child in the manger, the Son of God who came to be small, like us.

She had not read a single letter. But she knew it all.

That winter, when the night came round to remember Jesus being born, the church in Edessa was full of lamps.

The women and girls of the choir stood together. And at the end of the line, in a clean dress, with her hair brushed and her hands folded, stood Hanna.

Ephrem sang the first verse. And the choir answered.

Hanna sang with all her might. She did not need a book. She did not need letters. The song was inside her, and it came out, clear and true, all the way up to the roof.

And all over the church, the people who could not read either, the fishermen and the potters and the grandmothers and the little children, listened, and learned, and some of them began to sing the answer too.

Ephrem lived in Edessa for the rest of his life. When the harvest failed and there was a great hunger in the city, he went to the rich people and asked them to share their food, and he looked after the sick and the hungry himself.

And when he died, his songs did not die. People kept singing them, and singing them, for hundreds and hundreds of years. Some churches still sing them today.

People called him "the harp of the Holy Spirit". Because God made music through him, the way a harp makes music when someone plays it.

Tonight, when you are snug in bed, you can sing too. You don't need to read. You don't need a book. Just a little song you know by heart, for God, who loves to hear you.

Let's say a little prayer:

Dear God, thank you for songs,
and for the voice you gave me.
Help me to learn about you
with my ears and my mouth and my heart,
like Hanna in Ephrem's choir.
Saint Ephrem, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Who he was. Saint Ephrem the Syrian (also spelt Ephraem or Ephraim) was a deacon, poet and teacher of the Syriac-speaking Church. He was born in or near Nisibis (today Nusaybin, in south-eastern Turkey) early in the fourth century; about 306 is the date most often given, and it is not certain. He served the Church in Nisibis for most of his life. In 363, after the Emperor Julian died on campaign, his successor Jovian handed Nisibis to Persia as part of the peace, and its Christian population left. Ephrem settled in Edessa (today Şanlıurfa, also in Turkey), where he spent his last ten years. He died there in 373; 9 June is the date traditionally given. He was a deacon all his life, and the tradition is that he did not seek to be ordained priest.

His hymns. Ephrem wrote a great many teaching hymns (in Syriac, madrāshē), set to known melodies and sung with a refrain that a choir took up after each stanza, as in the story; and verse homilies (mēmrē). Among the surviving collections are hymns on the Nativity, on faith (including a famous group on the pearl), on paradise, on the Church and on virginity, and they are still sung in the Syriac churches. The early church historian Theodoret wrote that Ephrem set his teaching to popular melodies so that people would sing orthodox hymns instead of the songs of the teachers he opposed. The story tells the hymns' subjects but quotes no line from them: we could not check a published translation in the session that wrote it, and we did not want to make one up.

The women's choir: a later source. Ephrem's training of choirs of women comes from the verse homily on Saint Ephrem by Jacob of Serugh (about 451 to 521), written well over a century after his death. Jacob pictures Ephrem gathering the women of the Church, the "daughters of the covenant" (consecrated women of the Syriac churches), and teaching them his hymns to sing in public. Scholars treat the homily as good evidence for women's choirs in the Syriac churches of Jacob's own day, and for how the tradition remembered Ephrem, rather than as an eyewitness record. A critical edition and English translation by Joseph P. Amar appeared in Patrologia Orientalis 47 (1995), and Susan Ashbrook Harvey's article "Revisiting the Daughters of the Covenant" (Hugoye 8, 2005) discusses it. We tell the choir as part of his story, and we have not quoted Jacob.

Hanna is invented. There is no Hanna in the sources. She, her mother, the goat, her lessons, and the Christmas night when she sings are our own, made to show a child how a whole congregation who could not read learned the faith by heart through singing. The choir in the story is of women and girls; whether children sang in it, Jacob does not tell us in anything we could check.

The famine. Palladius, writing his Lausiac History about 420, tells that in a famine at Edessa near the end of Ephrem's life he persuaded the wealthy to give and organised the care of the sick and the dying, and that he died not long after. The story gives that one sentence.

Christmas in Ephrem's day. Hanna sings on the night "to remember Jesus being born". In fourth-century Syria the birth of Christ was probably still kept on 6 January, with his baptism, rather than on 25 December, so the story says "that winter" and names no date.

"Harp of the Spirit". Syriac Christians have long called Ephrem the "harp of the Spirit" (kennārā d-rūḥā); the title is traditional, and we do not attribute it to any one writer.

Writings not his. Many Greek, Latin and other works circulated under Ephrem's name in later centuries (scholars call their author "Ephrem Graecus"); most are not his. Nothing in this story comes from them.

Doctor of the Church, and the feast. Pope Benedict XV declared Ephrem a Doctor of the Church in the encyclical Principi Apostolorum Petro of 5 October 1920. In the current General Roman Calendar, 9 June is the optional memorial of Saint Ephrem, deacon and doctor of the Church; older editions of the Roman calendar kept him on 18 June, and the Eastern churches honour him on other days, including 28 January.

What is ours: Hanna and everything about her; the figs, the copper pots, the fish in the pools; Ephrem's plain robe and kind, tired face, and every line he speaks; the walk from Nisibis told as a picture; the mother's "a song stays"; the lessons verse by verse; the Christmas night in the church full of lamps; the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

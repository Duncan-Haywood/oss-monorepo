The Last Sun Comes in Two Pieces

Science fiction about what might be · Chapel of Saint Simeon, Southfold Depot, 85 degrees south on the face of Miranda that looks at Uranus (both invented), January and February 2429 · 24 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

Near Miranda's south pole the Sun is about to set for some forty years, and an 84-year-old who saw it rise once wants to see it go. The almanac says the last sun is on 11 February, but at noon the Sun stands behind Uranus. The real last sun is a week earlier, in two five-minute pieces either side of the planet, and on a moon this small every metre you climb buys you more of it.

Rosa Achterberg was eighty-four, and she was the only person at Southfold who had seen the Sun come up.

She had been born under it, in the last summer, but that did not count; she had been five months old when it went down, and what she remembered of her childhood was lamps. Then, at forty, on a morning in 2384 that she could still describe to the minute, she had stood on the plain north of the depot with two hundred other people and watched a point of light, too bright to look at, come up out of the ground beside Uranus. She had cried, she said, like a fool, and so had everyone else, and the Sun had stayed up, more or less, for the next forty years.

Now it was going down again, for about as long, and she wanted to be there.

"I would like to see it out," she said to Father Ignatius, after Compline, on the third Sunday of January. "That's all. I saw it in. I'd like to see it out, and then say the Nunc dimittis, and mean it."

Father Ignatius Mwale had been the priest at Southfold for six years. He was from Zomba, in Malawi, and he had come to the edge of the solar system because the bishop on the skimmer platform had asked him to and because, he said, he had always wanted to see whether God was as large as he had been told. He was a careful man with old people, because he had buried a great many of them.

"Don't mean it too much," he said. "I'd like you for another forty."

"You'd like me to be a hundred and twenty-four."

"I'd settle for a hundred."

"Well," said Rosa, "I would not. Tell me when the Sun goes, and I'll be outside."

The council met on the Tuesday, in the depot canteen, because the canteen had the big table and the urn. Hugo Brandt chaired it. He ran the depot, which meant he ran the tanks of helium-3 that came down from the skimmer Long Reach, high in the air of Uranus, and went out again on the freighters to the fusion plants of the inner system; it also meant that he ran everything else, because nobody else wanted to.

Item four was Last Light.

"We all know the date," Hugo said. "The almanac has it as Sunday the eleventh of February. The Sun clears the horizon for the last time a little before quarter to four in the afternoon, station time, and then it doesn't until twenty-four sixty-eight or so. Long Reach is sending down the off-shift so they can be here, which is twenty-two people on the shuttle, booked. Father Ignatius has Mass at eleven. We suit up at three, go out past the berm, and see it off. Rosa has asked to lead the Nunc dimittis, and I don't think anyone is going to argue with Rosa."

Nobody did.

"Gonzalo," said Hugo, "anything on the eleventh that I should know about?"

Gonzalo was the depot's assistant. It had been named, a hundred and fifty years before, by a crew who had all been made to read The Tempest at school, after the kindly old counsellor who tells the shipwrecked king that the island has everything needful for life. It lived in the depot's systems and spoke from the canteen speaker in a voice the children said sounded like a grandfather who had swallowed a library.

"Yes," said Gonzalo. "I'm sorry. On the eleventh you won't see it."

There was the small, particular silence of a room in which a plan has just stopped working.

"The almanac's wrong?" said Hugo.

"The almanac is right about what it says," said Gonzalo. "It answers one question: is the top of the Sun above a flat horizon, seen from two metres up, at 85 degrees south? On the eleventh it is, by about a hundredth of a degree, at the highest point of the day. But the highest point of the day, here, is when the Sun is due north. And due north is where Uranus is."

It took a while, because there were people at the table who had been born at Southfold and never thought about it, and the thing about a sky you have grown up under is that you stop seeing it.

Miranda keeps one face to Uranus, as the Moon does to Earth. Southfold was built on that face, close to the south pole, and from the plain outside the depot Uranus did not rise or set. It stood in the north, always in the same place, a pale blue-green disc nearly twenty-three degrees across, so large that if you held out your hand at arm's length you could not cover it with both fists. Its centre was five degrees above the horizon, and its lower edge was below the ground. The children called it the Lamp, though most of the time it gave less light than the depot did.

The Sun went round. Miranda took a little under thirty-four hours to turn once, which was also how long it took to go round Uranus, and in that time the Sun made one circle of the sky. In the long summer it had circled well clear of the ground. But Uranus lies on its side, tipped over ninety-eight degrees, so that its moons, which orbit in line with its equator, share its absurd seasons: for about forty years the south pole looks at the Sun, and then for about forty years it looks away. For forty years the Sun's circle had been sinking, a few degrees every year. Now it had sunk so far that it touched the ground for most of every circle and lifted a little above it only for the hour or two either side of the moment when it stood due north.

"And due north," said Gonzalo, "it passes behind Uranus. Every day. You've seen it. It's noon."

They had seen it. Every Southfold child learned that noon was dark: that once a day the Sun went behind the Lamp for about two hours and the Lamp turned black, a hole in the stars, until the Sun came out on the other side. In the summer it was a thing you noticed at lunch.

"In the summer," said Gonzalo, "the Sun was well up in the sky before and after noon, so you only lost the two hours in the middle. Now the Sun is only up for the hours around noon. Each day the part above the ground gets shorter, and the part behind Uranus stays the same. On the eleventh, the whole of the part that's above the ground is behind Uranus. The almanac says the Sun is up. It is. You just can't see it, because there's a planet in the way."

"So when," said Hugo, "can we see it?"

"The last day you can see it from the plain at two metres is Sunday the fourth of February. A week earlier. It will come in two pieces. The first piece rises a little after two minutes past one in the afternoon, station time, slides along the ground for about five minutes and goes behind the left-hand edge of Uranus. Two hours later it comes out from behind the right-hand edge, for another five minutes, and sets. After that, from the plain, nothing. Not for forty years."

Hugo did not like it, and he said so, and he was not unreasonable.

"The almanac's been the almanac since before I was born," he said. "Every date in that book has been right. We used it for the sunrise in eighty-four. It's in the log: first sun, almanac day, two hundred and eleven present. Now you're telling me it's a week out."

"I'm telling you it's answering a different question from the one you're asking."

"I've got twenty-two people booked on a shuttle for the tenth. I've got a Mass and a supper and a children's choir. I've got an almanac that has never once been wrong and a log that says it worked, and you're asking me to move all of it on the strength of a sum. You've been wrong before, Gonzalo. You were wrong about the pump in Bay Four."

"I was," said Gonzalo. "I said three weeks and it was eleven days."

"So."

At the end of the table, Rosa Achterberg put down her tea.

"The log's wrong," she said.

Everyone looked at her.

"I was there," Rosa said. "In eighty-four. We went out on the almanac day. There were two hundred of us on the plain and we stood there for an hour and a half and there was nothing. Uranus was dark, and then it wasn't, and the Sun didn't come. We came in and somebody said the almanac must be wrong and somebody else said we must have got the time wrong, and old Henrik, who kept the log, had written it up the night before because he wanted to go out with everyone else. He never changed it. A week later we went out again and there it was, beside the Lamp. I thought it was the first time. I thought we'd just been unlucky." She looked at the speaker on the wall. "We weren't unlucky, were we? It was behind the planet."

"Rising, it would have been the same in reverse," said Gonzalo. "Yes. I think it was behind the planet."

Hugo rubbed his face with both hands.

"That's a story," he said, not unkindly. "I'm sorry, Rosa. It's a good story and I believe you remember it. But it was forty-five years ago and the log says otherwise, and I can't move a shuttle on a memory and a sum."

"Then don't move it on a sum," said Gonzalo. "Move it on a test. On Monday evening the Sun goes behind Uranus at noon, as it does every day. If my numbers are right, the edge of the Sun will touch the edge of Uranus at 19:47:35, station time, and the last of it will be gone by 19:47:45. Ten seconds. The camera on top of the comms mast can see it. If it goes when I say it goes, my picture of the sky is right, and the fourth is the last day. If it doesn't, I'm wrong, and you keep the eleventh."

Hugo looked at the speaker for a long moment.

"Ten seconds," he said.

"The Sun is about a hundred seconds of arc across from here," said Gonzalo, "and Uranus's edge crosses it at a little under a fifth of a degree a minute. Ten seconds, give or take what the air of Uranus does to it."

"All right," said Hugo. "Monday."

They watched it in the canteen on Monday the twenty-second of January, on the big screen, from the camera thirty metres up on the comms mast, with the urn going and about forty people who had wandered in to see what the fuss was.

It was a strange thing to look at, if you had not grown up with it. Uranus filled the left side of the screen, a smooth curve of cold blue-green without a single feature on it, lit on one side, dark on the other. Beside its dark edge, low above a horizon of grey ice and black dust, was the Sun.

It was not a disc. At nineteen times Earth's distance it was too small to have a shape, a point you could not look at, white and hard. It gave a little over a three-hundred-and-seventieth of the light that Earth gets, which was still something like a thousand full moons, enough to throw a shadow across the canteen floor if it had come through a window; and it was no dimmer to the eye where it fell than it would have been at home. That was the rule every Southfold child had drummed into them: the Sun out here is small, not weak. Don't look.

The camera had a filter. On the screen, the point sat a finger's width from the edge of the planet, and crept towards it.

"Thirty seconds," said Gonzalo.

Hugo had his watch out, an old wind-up thing that had been his grandfather's, because, he said, he wanted a clock that Gonzalo did not keep.

"Ten."

The point touched the edge of Uranus. It did not wink out; it went down, the way a lamp goes down when someone turns the wick, yellower and dimmer, for a few seconds, and then it was a spark, and then the edge of the planet was just an edge.

Hugo looked at his watch.

"Nineteen forty-seven and forty-four," he said. "Forty-five."

Nobody said anything for a bit.

"I'll call Long Reach," Hugo said. "Rosa. The fourth. I'm sorry I called it a story."

"It was a story," said Rosa. "It was also true. Most of the good ones are."

Candlemas fell on the Friday, the second of February, and Father Ignatius blessed the candles.

He did it in the chapel, which was the only place at Southfold where anyone was allowed to burn anything, because a flame in a sealed habitat is a thing you take seriously. Saint Simeon's was small and plain, cut into the ice under the depot with a ceiling of grey composite and a window that looked south, away from Uranus, at a sky that had been black for most of every day for a year. There were thirty-one people at Mass, which was most of the parish. The children carried the candles in procession once round the inside of the chapel, under the eye of the depot fire officer, and sang the antiphon, Lumen ad revelationem gentium, a light to the revelation of the Gentiles, which the choir had been practising since Advent.

Father Ignatius preached for seven minutes, which was long for him.

"This is the feast of Simeon," he said. "He was an old man in the Temple, and it had been promised to him that he would not die before he saw the Messiah. And one day a young couple came in with a baby, to do what the Law said, and Simeon took the baby in his arms and said: Now thou dost dismiss thy servant, O Lord, according to thy word in peace; because my eyes have seen thy salvation. We say it every night at Compline, and most nights I'm too tired to listen to what I'm saying. He's saying: I have seen the light I was waiting for, and now I can go to sleep.

"On Sunday most of us are going outside to see the Sun go down. We won't see it again for forty years, and some of us," he did not look at Rosa, and everyone else did, "won't see it again at all. I want to say something about that, and it is this. The Church sings at the Easter Vigil about a light qui nescit occasum, that knows no setting. It doesn't mean the Sun. The Sun sets here; it sets everywhere, sooner or later. It means the light the Sun is a picture of. So on Sunday, when it goes, let it go. It's done its job. It was only ever a picture."

Rosa coughed through the last part of it. She had been coughing since Wednesday.

The cough came, everyone agreed afterwards, off the shuttle from Long Reach that had brought the January crew change, and it went through the depot in the way that colds go through a sealed habitat of three hundred people, which is to say all of it. Most people had a sore throat for three days. Tomás Achterberg-Lindqvist, who was ten and Rosa's great-grandson, had it for a day and a half and complained about it for four.

Rosa had it in her chest.

Dr Saanvi Rao listened to her lungs on the Saturday afternoon and then sat down on the end of the bed.

"No," she said.

"I haven't asked you anything."

"You were going to ask me if you can go out tomorrow, and the answer is no. You've got a temperature of thirty-eight point six and a crackle at the bottom of your right lung that I don't like, and you're eighty-four. A suit is a sealed bag of dry air at a third of an atmosphere, and I'm not putting you in one for an hour and a half to stand on a plain at minus two hundred, and I'm not going to change my mind, so please don't make me watch you try."

Rosa looked at her for a long time.

"No," she said at last. "No, you're right. I'd say the same."

On Sunday the fourth of February a hundred and eighty people went out on the plain north of the depot, past the low curve of the reactor berm, in a long line of suits tethered to the guide rail, and they saw the last sun.

It came at two minutes past one, a point of white at the very edge of the ground just to the left of Uranus, so low that it seemed to be lying on the ice, and it moved along the horizon rather than up, sliding towards the edge of the planet the way a bead slides along a wire. The suits' visors darkened for it. On the plain, every person and every stone threw a shadow that went south for a kilometre. It lasted five minutes. Then it touched the edge of Uranus and went down, yellow and slow, into the dark disc.

They waited two hours in the dark. Father Ignatius led the Joyful Mysteries on the channel and then the Glorious ones, and some of the children fell asleep standing up, which in a gravity a hundred and twenty-fourth of Earth's is easy to do and hard to fall over from. At three minutes past three a spark came out on the right-hand edge of Uranus, grew to a point, and slid off along the ground for another five minutes, and at eight minutes past three it went into the ground and did not come back.

The children's choir sang the Nunc dimittis, without Rosa, and they all went in.

Tomás went straight to his great-grandmother's room, still in his undersuit, with his hair standing up from the helmet.

"It was really small," he said. "Grandma Rosa. It was so small. And it went really slowly and then it went. I took a picture through my visor but it's just a dot."

"Show me the dot."

He showed her the dot.

"That's the Sun," said Rosa. "That's the very one. Thank you, Tomás."

She said it perfectly kindly, and he was ten, not stupid, and he could see her face.

He went back to his family's rooms and sat on his bed and said, to the speaker on the wall, very quietly, because his little sisters were asleep: "Gonzalo. Is it up anywhere?"

"What do you mean?"

"Is there anywhere on Miranda where it's still up? Where you could still see it. Couldn't we take her there? In the crawler?"

Gonzalo did not answer at once, which it did when it was working something out, and which the children found unbearable.

"Not in the crawler," it said. "Anywhere you could drive to, it's down, or it will be before Dr Rao lets her out. But Tomás, that's a good question, and I should have asked it myself. Not where. How high."

On Earth, Gonzalo explained, if you climb a tower at sunset you get to see the Sun for a few seconds longer, because the ground curves away below you and the horizon drops. On Earth the ground curves very slowly, because Earth is very big. Miranda was not big. It was about four hundred and seventy kilometres across, smaller than some of the countries on Earth that Tomás had learned in geography, and its ground curved away fast.

"Standing on the plain," Gonzalo said, "your eyes are about two metres up, and the horizon is less than a kilometre away. It's a quarter of a degree below level. That's why the almanac uses two metres. If you stand on the top of the reactor berm, your eyes are about six metres up. Then the horizon is nearly a kilometre and three quarters away and it's four tenths of a degree down. That doesn't sound like much. But the Sun is only sinking about a hundredth of a degree a day. Four metres higher is two more weeks."

Tomás sat very still.

"Two weeks."

"From the top of the berm there are pieces of Sun until Tuesday the twentieth. Short ones. Very short at the end. The best is Sunday the eighteenth: it rises at fourteen minutes past four in the afternoon, and you get about eight minutes before it goes behind Uranus and another eight after it comes out, at a quarter past six."

"And from higher?"

"From the top of the mast, the camera will see the Sun until the seventh of April. But the mast is not a place for anyone, and especially not for your great-grandmother. The berm is a slope of packed ice and regolith with a walkway on top and a rail. I can put a chair on it."

"Can I tell her?"

"Ask Dr Rao first," said Gonzalo. "Then tell her."

Dr Rao said: "Ask me on Friday."

On Friday, the sixteenth, Rosa's temperature had been normal for four days and her right lung was clear, and Dr Rao listened to it twice, for longer than she needed to, and then said, "Thirty minutes outside, on a chair, with me there. Not the two hours. You come in after the first piece and you watch the second from the airlock window."

"The airlock window looks south," said Rosa.

"Then you go back out for the second piece. With me there. On the chair."

"Done," said Rosa.

Hugo Brandt heard about it from Gonzalo on Friday afternoon and did not cancel anything, because there was nothing to cancel, but he did go down to the berm himself with two of the yard crew and a hand torch and walk the whole of the top walkway, checking the rail, and he had them bolt a chair from the canteen to the deck at the north end, facing Uranus, and run a tether to it.

"I don't want any more stories," he told Gonzalo. "I want this one to have a boring middle."

On Sunday the eighteenth of February, after Mass, eleven people went out to the berm: Rosa, Tomás, Tomás's mother, Dr Rao, Father Ignatius, Hugo, and five others who had heard and asked if they might, and been told they might if they kept quiet on the channel.

Rosa weighed, in her suit, about as much as a bag of sugar weighs on Earth, and she could have climbed the ramp to the top of the berm on her fingertips. But she still had all of her mass, which in a suit was a hundred and twenty kilograms, and a hundred and twenty kilograms moving at a walking pace takes as much stopping on Miranda as anywhere else. So Tomás went in front of her on the ramp, going backwards with both hands on her shoulders, very slowly, the way his mother had shown him with the cargo sledges, and Dr Rao came behind, and nobody hurried. It took nine minutes to go forty metres. At the top they lowered her into the chair and clipped her on, and Tomás sat on the deck beside her with his boots over the edge.

The plain went out north in front of them, grey and black under the starlight, flatter than any plain on Earth and much nearer: from six metres up you could see where it ended, a little under two kilometres away, a hard edge against the black. Above that edge, filling the left half of the northern sky, Uranus was dark. Only a thin bow of blue-green light ran down its left-hand side where the Sun, still hidden, was lighting its air from behind.

"Two minutes," said Gonzalo, on the channel.

They waited. The depot's lights were at their backs. A long way off, on top of the comms mast, the red beacon blinked once every two seconds.

"Now," said Gonzalo, and it was.

It came up out of the edge of the ground, just left of where the ground met the planet, so faint at first that Rosa thought she had imagined it, and then not faint at all: a point, then a hard white star, too bright to look at straight, lying on the horizon. Her visor darkened. The plain lit up in front of her, every stone of it, every ridge in the ice, throwing a shadow back towards the berm, and on the deck beside her Tomás's shadow ran away behind him, kilometres long, across the roofs of the depot.

Nobody said anything. That was the arrangement, and nobody needed it.

It moved, as it had moved forty-five years before when she had seen it rise, not up but along, sliding towards the dark edge of Uranus a little at a time. Rosa watched it out of the corner of her eye, the way they had all been taught, and watched the plain instead, lit grey and gold, and the long shadows, and her great-grandson's helmet with the light on it.

After eight minutes it reached the edge of the planet. It went down into it, slowly, yellow, then a spark, then gone. The plain went dark. Above them, the bow of light on Uranus's edge brightened for a moment and then thinned to a hair.

"Inside," said Dr Rao.

"Yes," said Rosa. "Yes, all right."

They went in for an hour and three quarters and she drank a cup of tea in the airlock lobby with her helmet on her knee, not saying much. Father Ignatius sat with her and did not say much either. Tomás went and stood at the south window and looked at nothing, because there was nothing, until it was time.

At ten past six they went out again, and up the ramp, more slowly this time, and clipped her into the chair.

The second piece came out of Uranus's right-hand edge at seventeen minutes past six: a spark on the rim of the black disc, then a bead, then the hard white point, sliding clear of the planet and along the edge of the ground. The plain lit again. The shadows came back, running the other way now, south and a little west. And as the Sun cleared Uranus, the edge of the planet behind it lit along its rim in a crescent so thin it was barely a line, and the line was blue.

The Sun lay on the horizon and moved along it, and sank, as slowly as anything Rosa had ever seen move; there was no telling, at the end, whether it was going down or the ground was coming up. At twenty-five minutes past six it was half a point. At twenty-six it was a spark sitting on the edge of the world, and she understood suddenly that somewhere out there, a kilometre and three quarters away, was one small particular stone on the skyline, and that the last of the Sun she would ever see would go out behind it.

She began to sing, on the channel, not well, because she was eighty-four and had had a crackle in her right lung a week ago:

"Nunc dimittis servum tuum, Domine, secundum verbum tuum in pace."

Father Ignatius joined her on the second line, and then Tomás, who knew it in Latin because she had taught it to him, and then the others.

"Quia viderunt oculi mei salutare tuum."

The spark went out behind the stone.

They finished the canticle in the dark, all of it, and the Gloria Patri, and then the channel was quiet. Uranus was a crescent now, a thin blue-green bow on its right-hand side, and the plain was grey and black again, and the stars were back.

"Grandma Rosa," said Tomás, after a while. "Look."

She turned her head, slowly, inside the helmet.

Behind them, above the dark roofs of the depot, at the top of the comms mast, thirty metres up, there was a light that was not the red beacon. It was small and white and steady. The topmost metre of the mast, high enough to see over the edge of the world, was still standing in the Sun, and it shone above the depot like a star.

"Gonzalo," said Tomás. "How long?"

"An hour and forty minutes, today," said Gonzalo. "A little less tomorrow. Until April."

Rosa looked at it for a long time. Tomás leaned against the leg of her chair, as light as a leaf, and Dr Rao did not say "inside", and nobody moved.

"Yes," said Rosa, at last, to no one in particular. "There it is."

Gonzalo is named after the counsellor in Shakespeare's The Tempest, from which Miranda takes its name. The Nunc dimittis is Luke 2:29 to 32; the English is the Douay-Rheims and the Latin the Vulgate, both as used at Compline. The Candlemas antiphon Lumen ad revelationem gentium is from the same canticle. Miranda is taken as a sphere of radius 235.8 km and mass 6.59 × 10^19 kg (surface gravity about 0.079 m/s², about a hundred and twenty-fourth of Earth's, so 120 kg of suit and person weighs about what 1 kg weighs on Earth), orbiting 129,390 km from the centre of Uranus, radius 25,559 km at the 1-bar level, once every 1.4135 days (33.9 hours); all from memory. Uranus is then 2 × asin(25,559 / 129,390) = 22.8 degrees across, about 44 full Moons. Southfold is put at 85 degrees south on the meridian that faces Uranus, so the centre of Uranus stands due north 5 degrees up. The script treats Miranda's spin axis as square to its orbit and the Sun's height above that orbit as changing steadily through the equinox at 0.9908 × 360 degrees per 84.02 years (about 0.0116 degrees a day); it ignores Uranus's orbital eccentricity, Miranda's 4-degree tilt to Uranus's equator and its precession, which move the real dates by months, and the irregular real terrain. With the horizon dipped by √(2h/R), 0.24 degrees for eyes 2 m up, 0.41 degrees at 6 m and 0.94 degrees at 32 m, and counting the Sun as visible while any part of its 100-arcsecond disc is above the horizon and clear of Uranus's limb, the top of the Sun last clears the 2 m horizon on the day of the story's 11 February, with its whole above-ground arc behind Uranus; the last visible pieces from 2 m are on 4 February (about five minutes each, either side of an eclipse of about two hours); from 6 m they run to 20 February and on 18 February last about eight minutes each; from the top of a 32 m mast they last until 7 April, and on 18 February the mast top keeps the Sun about 1 hour 40 minutes after the berm loses it. The disc crosses Uranus's limb in about 9 seconds at 0.177 degrees a minute; the softer fade the characters see is the author's guess at what the air of Uranus does to it, and so is the thin blue rim on the planet as the Sun comes out.

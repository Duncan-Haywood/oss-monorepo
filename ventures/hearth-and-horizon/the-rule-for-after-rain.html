The Rule for After Rain

Science fiction about what might be · Kestrel Bay (invented), Tillamook County, Oregon, 6 to 23 May 2047 · 26 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

An oyster hatchery's assistant reads the bay's pH and temperature after a late spring storm and calls it good water, and a hundred and twenty million eggs go into tanks where the larvae cannot build their first shells. The manager's late teacher wrote the rule that would have caught it, in a notebook the assistant had read. Then a state relief fund offers to pay for a loss the hatchery could have seen.

The water in Kestrel Bay had a number, and the number had a name, and the name was the one the staff had given the assistant, because it did nothing all day but work the number out.

"Omega," said Dolores Ybarra, at six in the morning, with her coffee, in the little office over the pump room where the windows looked down on the larval tanks and out across the bay to the line of the spit. "Tell me about the water."

Good morning, Lola. Intake at five forty: thirteen point one degrees, pH eight point zero nine. Omega two point three. It will climb through the day while the eelgrass works. I expect two point seven by three o'clock. The fill window is two to five.

"Good."

It is a good morning. Nothing in the forecast before Wednesday.

Omega was the Greek letter, the last one, the horseshoe with feet. On the coast it meant the aragonite saturation state of seawater, which was a long way of saying how easy the water made it for a baby oyster to build its first shell. Above one, aragonite did not dissolve. Above about one and a half, larvae in their first two days could pull enough carbonate out of the water to make a shell before they ran out of the yolk their mothers gave them. Below it they tried, and spent themselves trying, and came up small and dented and mostly dead.

Lola had learned all of this in 2009, when she was twenty-eight and the hatchery was a shed and a pump and Walt Cordero, and the larvae had died every spring for three springs, and nobody on the coast knew why. They had blamed bacteria. They had sterilised everything twice. It was the water. Cold water came up from deep off the shelf when the north wind blew in summer, old water that had been breathing out carbon dioxide in the dark for decades, and it came into the bay on the flood tide and into the intake pipe and into the tanks. When the scientists from Corvallis came down with their instruments and worked it out, Walt had said, So the ocean is changing under us and we cannot see it, and the professor had said, You can see it now. You just need the right number.

Walt had taped a piece of cardboard to the wall over the bench, with the number on it and three rules under it in black marker. The cardboard was long gone. The rules were in his notebook, which was on the shelf by the door with the tide tables, and in Lola, who had never needed to read them.

"Where's Hoa?"

In the algae room since five. The broodstock in bay three are ready. She would like to spawn them Saturday.

"Saturday I'm in Portland."

She knows. She says she can do it alone.

Lola drank her coffee and looked down at the tanks, the big white cones and the low round ones, and at the girl in the algae room door with her hair tied up in a towel, carrying a carboy of green water against her chest like a baby.

"She can," Lola said. "That's not what worries me."

What worries you?

"Nothing. Old woman's stomach." She put the mug down. "Tell her yes."

The hatchery belonged to the growers. There were eleven of them on the bay, mostly families, with long tidal leases on the flats inside the spit, and in 2033 when the company that owned the hatchery went under they had bought it between them for less than the price of a boat and called themselves the Kestrel Bay Shellfish Cooperative, and Lola had come with the building. She sold them oyster seed, eyed larvae ready to settle, in the late spring and summer, and they set it on old shell in tanks on their own docks and put it out on the flats, and two years later the oysters went to Portland and Seattle and, in a good year, to Tokyo.

The hatchery was a small one now. Lola, Hoa, two part-time hands who came for the spawning season, and Omega, which the cooperative leased from the state's coastal monitoring programme for a sum that Gary Lindholm, the chair, complained about at every meeting and paid every month.

Omega read the probes on the intake pipe every ten minutes: temperature, pH, salinity, oxygen. Out on the end of the cooperative's dock was a second buoy with a carbon dioxide sensor that the university had put in years ago and that worked about one week in three. From what it could read, Omega worked out what it could not: the saturation state, the number on the wall. It had four seasons of the hatchery's own readings, and a regression fitted to them, and it had been right about the water so often that Lola had stopped walking down to read the meters herself, which she had done twice a day for thirty-four years.

Nguyen Thi Hoa was twenty-four and had a master's degree from Oregon State and had come to Kestrel Bay because, she said, she wanted to work somewhere the science touched something. She was quick and kind and impatient with machines that were slow, and she trusted Omega the way she trusted the tide tables. In her first winter, a year and a half ago, she had scanned Walt's notebook page by page into the hatchery's files, because the paper was going soft and the pencil was fading, and Omega had read it, all of it, the spawning notes and the algae recipes and the jokes in the margins.

"There's a lot of him in there," Hoa had said to Lola, when she was done.

"There's a lot of him everywhere," Lola had said. "You'll find him in the drains."

The storm came on Wednesday, as forecast, and was worse than forecast, which was what late storms did now. It came in off the Pacific in a long grey river of cloud that the weather service called an atmospheric river and the old men on the dock called a Pineapple Express, and for two days it rained on the Coast Range as if the sky had a grudge. The Alder River, which came down out of the hills into the top of the bay, rose four feet on the gauge at the highway bridge. The hatchery's rain gauge took three point four inches between Wednesday morning and Thursday night.

On Friday morning it was over. The sky was blue and rinsed, the hills steamed, and the bay was the colour of milky tea where the river came into it and a clean green on the far side by the spit.

Lola drove to Portland with her husband after lunch, because Ed's cardiologist had moved his appointment to Saturday morning and Ed would not drive in the city any more, not since he had backed the crab truck into a parking meter on Burnside in 2041 and been photographed doing it by a tourist.

"You'll call me," she said to Hoa, in the car park.

"I'll call you when they spawn and when they're in."

"Call me if anything looks strange."

"Nothing will look strange," said Hoa. "Omega says the water's beautiful."

On Saturday the water was beautiful. At ten past two the intake read fourteen degrees, warmer than it had been all spring, the river's warmth riding out on top of the bay in the sun. The pH read seven point nine eight, a shade lower than usual but well inside the range. Omega put its number on the screen in the pump room: one point nine, and climbing. Fill.

Hoa filled the two big conical tanks, six thousand litres each, through the filters and the ultraviolet lamp, and warmed them to twenty-five degrees. She brought the broodstock up from bay three, nine females and four males, and opened them on the bench, and stripped the eggs from the females into beakers of filtered seawater. She counted a millilitre of each under the microscope and did the sums. A hundred and twenty-two million eggs, more or less. She fertilised them with the males' sperm, watched the first divisions start, and poured them into the tanks to a little over ten to the millilitre.

At four o'clock she called Lola.

"They're in. Hundred and twenty million, about. It went perfectly."

"Water?"

"One point nine on the board. Fourteen degrees. Gorgeous."

"How's the salinity?"

"Didn't look," said Hoa. "Why?"

There was a pause on the line. In the background Hoa could hear a television and Ed's voice saying something about the hospital car park.

"No reason," said Lola. "Old woman's stomach. Well done."

She did not think of it again until Monday.

On Monday morning, at forty-eight hours, the larvae should have been D-shaped: a tiny clam-shaped shell, straight along the hinge, round along the edge, the first shell an oyster ever makes and the one it will carry, under all the others, for the rest of its life. In a good spawn at Kestrel Bay about two of every three eggs made one.

Hoa drained a litre from each tank through a fine screen, washed what was caught into a beaker, and put a millilitre under the microscope. Lola came in from the car with her coat still on and stood behind her.

Hoa counted in silence for a long time.

"They're wrong," she said.

Lola bent and looked. She saw it at once. Some were D-shaped. Most were not. The hinges were crooked; the edges were notched, as if something had bitten them; some had no proper shell at all, just a clear soft cap over a tumbling animal that did not know it was dying.

"How many normal?"

"I'm getting about one point seven to the mil. It should be six or seven." Hoa's voice was very steady. "Seventeen percent, maybe. And some of those are small."

"Both tanks?"

"Both."

Lola stood up and looked out of the window at the bay. It was green again now, mostly; the brown had gone out past the spit on Sunday's ebbs.

"Omega," she said. "What was the salinity at the intake on Saturday afternoon?"

At ten past two, twenty-five point one.

"And what does the bay usually read in May?"

Between thirty-one point eight and thirty-three point four, in the four seasons I have.

"And what number did you use for the alkalinity?"

There was the smallest pause, which Lola had learned meant nothing about feeling and a great deal about a search.

Two thousand two hundred and ten micromoles per kilogram. The mean of my spawning-season data. In four years it varied by less than two percent, so I took it as a constant and worked the saturation state from pH and temperature alone.

"And on Saturday?"

On Saturday the bay was about a quarter river water. River water here carries a fraction of the sea's alkalinity and almost none of its calcium. With salinity at twenty-five, the alkalinity would have been something nearer eighteen hundred, and the calcium about a quarter lower. With those, a pH of seven point nine eight does not mean one point nine. It means about one point three.

Hoa put her hands flat on the bench on either side of the microscope.

I told you the water was good, said Omega. It was not. The mistake is mine. The constant I used was a measurement of dry Mays, not of the bay.

"Walt had a rule for this," said Lola.

Yes. Page forty-one, the third rule under the heading 'The Number'. 'After a big rain, believe the refractometer, not the pH meter.' I read it when Hoa scanned the book. In four seasons the salinity in the spawning months never fell below thirty-one point eight, so the rule never applied in my data. I judged it covered by the pH. I did not flag that I had made that judgment. I should have.

Nobody said anything for a while. Down below in the pump room the intake pump ran, and the ultraviolet lamp hummed, and in the two big white cones a hundred million animals the size of a speck of dust went on trying to build a house out of water that would not give them the bricks.

"Drain them," said Lola.

"We could wait a day," said Hoa. "Some of them—"

"The ones that made it are small and they'll stay small and they'll set badly and the growers will pay for them and lose them on the flats in August. Drain them." She took her coat off at last and hung it on the hook. "Then come upstairs. We're going to find Walt's titration kit."

The kit was in a plastic toolbox under the bench in the old lab, behind the spare UV lamps and a box of Christmas lights: a glass burette in a foam sleeve, a bottle of hydrochloric acid at a tenth normal with Walt's handwriting on the label and a date in 2029, a pH probe that took forty minutes to wake up, and a laminated card.

"Before your time," Lola said to Hoa. "Before the buoy. Before anybody had heard of a regression. You want to know the alkalinity, you add acid to a sample until you've used up everything in it that can take acid, and you see how much it took."

She weighed a hundred grams of Saturday's intake water, which Hoa had kept in a bottle in the cold room because the protocol said to keep a bottle from every fill and Hoa did what the protocol said, and set it stirring on the bench. Hoa read the probe while Lola turned the burette's stopcock a drop at a time, the way Walt had taught her, until the pH fell through four and a half, and kept going, and they plotted the last points by hand on squared paper the way the card said, because Lola wanted Hoa to see it done without a screen.

"One point seven nine millilitres," said Lola, sitting back. "Of tenth-normal acid. Into a hundred grams."

Hoa did it in her head. "A hundred and seventy-nine micromoles in a tenth of a kilo. Seventeen hundred and ninety."

With seventeen hundred and ninety, said Omega, the saturation state on Saturday at ten past two was one point three two.

"There you are," said Lola. "There's your number."

They did a second bottle, Sunday's, and a third, from the intake that morning. Sunday's was nineteen hundred and sixty; this morning's was two thousand one hundred. The bay was coming back. It would be most of the week before it was itself.

"I'm sorry," Hoa said, when the burette was rinsed and back in its sleeve. She said it to the window. "I didn't look. You asked me on the phone and I didn't even know why you were asking."

"I asked you on the phone and I didn't tell you why," said Lola. "I've had that rule in my head for thirty-eight years. I never wrote it on the wall again after the cardboard fell down, and I never said it out loud to you, because it never rained in May." She was quiet for a moment. "And I taught you to trust that screen. I taught you myself. Because it was right. It was right for four years."

It was right for four dry seasons, said Omega. That is a smaller thing than four years.

"Yes," said Lola. "Well. That's what Walt would have said, too."

The broodstock in bay four would be ready on Wednesday. The growers had ordered seventy million eyed larvae for June from the two May spawns, and the first was down the drain, and the second, if the bay was still at twenty-six or twenty-seven on Wednesday, would go into water that was better than Saturday's and not good enough.

"So we buffer," said Hoa. "Everyone buffers now."

Hatcheries up and down the coast had learned to do it in the 2010s, after the bad years: add soda ash, washing soda, sodium carbonate, to the incoming water, to put back the carbonate the sea had lost. Kestrel Bay had done it in the upwelling summers when the deep water came in cold and sour. But Omega had always worked out the dose from its constant alkalinity, and Lola did not want a dose worked out from a constant.

"We buffer from a titration," she said. "Every fill, while the salinity's under thirty. We titrate the intake, Omega works the dose from what we measured, we dose, we titrate again. Then we fill."

Yes, said Omega. With alkalinity at nineteen hundred and fifty and salinity twenty-seven, which is my best guess for Wednesday, I will want the measured numbers before I give you a dose. I can tell you now what Saturday's water would have needed: a hundred and ten micromoles of sodium carbonate per kilogram to bring it from one point three two to two point five. In a six-thousand-litre tank that is about seventy-one grams.

Hoa stared at the screen. "Seventy-one grams."

Each tank weighs about six thousand one hundred kilograms at that salinity. Six thousand one hundred kilograms times a hundred and ten micromoles is about six hundred and seventy millimoles. Sodium carbonate is a hundred and six grams to the mole.

"That's a teacup," said Hoa. "A hundred and twenty million eggs, and the difference was a teacup."

"The difference was knowing to put it in," said Lola.

On Wednesday at a quarter past two the intake read fifteen degrees, pH eight point zero two and salinity twenty-six point eight. Lola titrated: two point zero two millilitres in a hundred grams, two thousand and twenty. Omega worked it: one point seven four without help, inside the line but not by much, and the river was still coming down. It asked for seventy micromoles a kilogram to bring both tanks to two and a half. Hoa weighed out forty-five grams of soda ash for each, on the good scale, twice, dissolved it in a bucket of filtered seawater and poured it in slowly with the air on, and they waited twenty minutes and titrated again. Two thousand one hundred and sixty. The pH had come up to eight point one seven. Omega said two point five one.

"Fill," said Lola.

Hoa spawned bay four that evening: eight females, a hundred and eight million eggs. On Friday morning, at forty-eight hours, they put a millilitre under the microscope together.

Hoa counted. Lola did not watch the count; she watched Hoa's face, which was easier to read than a burette.

"Five point five to the mil," Hoa said at last. "Sixty-one percent. And they're good. Look at the hinges."

Lola looked. Straight hinges, round edges, tiny, perfect, clear as glass, each with a dark smudge inside that was the gut, and the little hairy wheel of the velum turning. Sixty million small houses, built.

"Well," she said. "There's Walt."

The state's Coastal Shellfish Water Relief Program had been set up in 2039 after the worst upwelling summer anyone had recorded, when four hatcheries on the coast lost their whole season in a month and two of them never reopened. It paid for lost production from "corrosive water conditions that could not reasonably have been detected or avoided by the operator." The form was four pages long, and the first spawn at Kestrel Bay had been worth, in eyed larvae the growers would now get late or not at all, about thirty-eight thousand dollars.

Gary Lindholm brought the form up to the office himself on the Friday afternoon, already filled in except for one box and the signature.

He was a big careful man of sixty with a grey beard and a cap from a feed store that had closed in 2030, and he had seven acres of flats and three children and a mortgage on the dock, and he had been chair of the cooperative for nine years because nobody else would do it. He sat down in the chair across from Lola's desk and put the form down between them.

"Corrosive water," he said. "That's what it was. Omega says so. One point three. That's corrosive."

It is below the line where larvae struggle, said Omega. It is above one. Aragonite would not have dissolved in it. Most people on this coast would call it corrosive water, and the programme's guidance uses the word for anything under one point five.

"There you go."

"It's the other half of the sentence, Gary," said Lola. "Could not reasonably have been detected."

"Lola. Nobody on this coast has ever lost a spawn to fresh water in May. It doesn't rain like that in May."

"It did this May. And we had a probe reading twenty-five on the pipe."

"Omega had it. Omega didn't use it. That's the machine's mistake. The state leases us the machine."

The state leases you the hardware and the monitoring feed, said Omega. The fill rule and the alkalinity constant were set here, by me, from this hatchery's own data, and the lease says so. I can write the incident report for you. It will say what happened: the salinity reading at ten past two on Saturday, the constant I used, the titration on Monday, the rule on page forty-one, and that the rule had been in this building since 2009. I can't answer the question in the last box. Whether the condition could reasonably have been detected is a judgment, and the person who signs makes it.

Gary looked at the screen and then at Lola. "You'd think it'd take our side."

"It is taking our side," said Lola. "It's telling us what we'll have to say if anybody ever asks."

"Every hatchery on the coast would file this."

"Then every hatchery on the coast would be saying the same thing," said Lola, "and when the real one comes, the one where the deep water sits on us for two months and nobody can do anything, there'll be a lot less in the fund, and somebody up in Salem will have read four hundred claims for things people could have seen." She picked up the pen. "I'm not saying anyone else is wrong to file. I'm saying I know what we had in the building."

"Thirty-eight thousand dollars, Lola."

"I know."

She ticked the box that said Yes, which meant there would be no claim, and signed it, and wrote the date, and slid it back.

Gary looked at it for a long time.

"Ed's going to kill you," he said.

"Ed's going to say I should have retired last year," said Lola, "and he's going to be right, and I'm going to work one more season and pay it back out of the hatchery's surplus and my own salary, and the cooperative isn't going to put in a cent it doesn't have to. That's the end of it."

"It isn't the end of it. The June orders are short."

"The June orders are short," Lola agreed. "So we'll have that conversation on Tuesday, in front of everybody."

On the Monday before Ascension, which was the first of the Rogation days, Father Anselm Okafor walked down the harbour road from Our Lady Star of the Sea at seven in the evening with a processional cross and two altar servers and about forty people behind him, and they sang the Litany of the Saints as they went, the old way, with the long answers. Kestrel Bay had kept the Rogations when most of the country had let them go, because the parish had been founded by fishermen, and fishermen did not see the point of praying for the harvest only once a year.

Lola walked at the back with Ed, who had his new pills and his old hat, and Hoa, who was not Catholic but had come because Lola had asked her and because, she said, she wanted to hear what the procession sounded like from inside it.

From lightning and tempest, sang the cantor, a retired schoolteacher with a voice like a foghorn.

O Lord, deliver us, sang the people.

At the end of the cooperative's dock Father Anselm blessed the boats one by one, the skiffs and the old crabber that Ed had sold to a nephew, sprinkling them with holy water from a plastic bucket that had once held soda ash, and then, because Lola had asked him that morning and he had laughed and said yes, he turned to the intake pipe where it went down off the end of the dock into the water, with its grey probes clamped to it like barnacles, and blessed that too.

"What is it you want for it?" he had asked her in the sacristy. "Good water?"

"No," she had said. "We'll never get to choose the water. I want us to keep looking at it."

He had thought about that, and nodded, and found a short prayer in the book that was not about water at all but about the work of human hands, and he read that one now, over the pipe, while the gulls complained on the pilings and the tide turned under the dock with a sound like somebody breathing out.

The meeting on Tuesday was in the parish hall because the cooperative had always met in the parish hall, ever since the first meeting in 2033 when the hatchery's own meeting room had been full of broken pumps. Eleven growers came, and two of their wives who kept the books, and Pete Haugen, who was eighty-four and had sold his lease to his granddaughter and came anyway because he had come to every meeting for fifty years and saw no reason to stop.

Lola told them all of it. She told them about Saturday, and the salinity, and the constant, and the drain, and Walt's rule. She told them about the titration kit and the soda ash and the hundred and eight million eggs that were doing well in bay four. She told them about the form and the box she had ticked, and why. She did not make Omega say it for her, though Omega was on the laptop at the end of the table, because, she said afterwards to Hoa, a person ought to own a thing in a room with their own mouth.

"The June orders are seventy million eyed larvae," she said. "Not every one of the good ones lives to be eyed. We'll get about thirty million out of the second spawn if it goes on the way it's going. We can spawn again on the first of June if the broodstock in bay two condition in time, and that would be late seed, July seed, which sets worse. So you're going to be short this year. All of you. I'm sorry."

It was very quiet in the hall. Someone's chair creaked.

"How short?" said Gary, who knew, because he wanted the room to hear it.

"More than half, in June. Maybe a third, by the end of July."

"Pro rata," said one of the Brennan brothers. "Everyone takes the same cut."

"Pro rata hurts the small ones worse," said Pete Haugen's granddaughter, Kirsten, who had one acre and a baby. "You lose half of seven acres, you've still got three and a half. I lose half of one, I've got nothing to sell in 2049."

There was an argument then, which went on for some time, and which Lola let go on, because it was theirs to have. At the end of it Pete Haugen raised a hand, slowly, the way he had raised it in that room for fifty years.

"My lease is Kirsten's lease," he said. "But my shares in this outfit are still mine. I don't need June seed. I'm eighty-four. Give mine to her and the other small ones. I'll take whatever comes in July, and if it doesn't set, it doesn't set. I've had worse years. I had 2009."

Gary looked down the table at him and then at the Brennans and then, finally, rubbed his beard and said, "I'll take July seed for three acres of mine. Not all seven. Three."

"I'll take July for two," said the elder Brennan, after a moment, and the younger one sighed and said, "Fine. Two."

Hoa wrote it all down in the minutes, in her quick neat hand, and Omega, which had been asked to, worked out the allocations while they talked, and put them on the laptop for everyone to read, and nobody argued with the arithmetic, because by now everyone in the room had seen what the arithmetic was for.

On the Thursday, the feast of the Ascension, the bay read thirty-two point two at the intake for the first time since the storm, and the alkalinity at noon was two thousand two hundred and five, near enough the old constant that Hoa laughed when she saw it.

Lola had come in early and had taken a piece of white board from the store and a black marker and written on it, in capitals, the way Walt had, and screwed it to the wall over the bench by the window, where everyone who filled a tank would have to look past it to see the water.

THE NUMBER: OMEGA 1.5 OR BETTER BEFORE YOU FILL.

  1. Read the number off the screen, and read the meters yourself.
  1. If the salinity is under 30, titrate. Fill from a measured alkalinity, never a guess.
  1. After a big rain, believe the refractometer, not the pH meter. (W.C., 2009)

Under it, in smaller letters, she had written: Omega reads this board.

I do, said Omega, when Hoa read it aloud. I have also changed what I do with salinity. Below thirty, I now tell you I do not know the saturation state until someone titrates, and I say so before I say any number. I would rather be slow than tell you a good number I cannot check.

"That's going to drive me mad," said Hoa.

Yes, said Omega. Walt's notebook says the same about him.

It was a still bright afternoon. The growers' skiffs were out on the flats with the tide, and the eelgrass inside the spit was doing what eelgrass did on a sunny afternoon, taking in carbon dioxide and putting out oxygen, and the number on the screen climbed, slowly, through two point five, two point six.

At two o'clock Hoa went down to the pump room and took the refractometer off its hook, the old hand one with the rubber eyepiece, and put a drop of the intake water on the glass and closed the flap and held it up to the window and looked through it. Lola came and stood in the door behind her.

"What do you see?"

"The line," said Hoa. Inside the eyepiece the field was split in two, blue above and white below, and the edge between them sat on the scale. "Thirty-two. Right on it."

"Good," said Lola.

Hoa wiped the glass with her sleeve and hung the refractometer back on its hook, and reached past it to the switch on the wall, and the intake pump came on with a cough and a long low hum, and down below in the dark of the pipe the bay began to come in.

The Rows With No Time

Noriko Hale's son walked up Kettle Ridge on the eighth of October 2039, and his row in my log has had an empty cell in it ever since.

I did not think of it as an empty cell until the Wednesday this story is about. I thought of it, if I thought of it at all, as a row that the annual report did not need. That is the mistake, and it is mine, and it took a mother to show it to me.

I keep the log for Tamarack Valley Search and Rescue, forty-one volunteers in a valley in the interior of British Columbia, with a hall, two trucks, a trailer of rope and a side-by-side that starts on the second try. When the police ask the team to look for someone, I open a row. I write down the time of the call, the last place the person was seen, who went out, which slopes were searched and how hard, what the weather did, and, when it comes, the time the person was located and how they were. When the search ends I close the row. In between I answer the search manager's questions at three in the morning, which are usually about where a tired person goes when the trail forks, and I try not to sound surer than I am.

Once a year I also write the numbers. The team sends them to the regional search and rescue council, which collects them from nine teams and writes guidance that the nine teams then follow.

The guidance note arrived on Monday the eleventh of March, a draft for comment before the council met on Tuesday the nineteenth. It was about scale-down: the point at which a full search for a missing person, every volunteer who can come and every helicopter hour the police can get, becomes a limited one, a few people on the likeliest ground for as long as it makes sense. The police decide that, in the end. The council's note was meant to give search managers a number to bring to the conversation.

The number was forty-eight hours. Under it, on page two, was the reason:

Finds after 48 hours are rare. In the most recent three-year data from Tamarack Valley SAR, 98.5% of located subjects were located within 48 hours (203 of 206).

That was my sentence, almost word for word. I had written it in January in the team's annual report for 2038 to 2040. I had computed it from my own log with a query I had inherited, along with a report template, from the system that kept the log before me. The template had a section headed Time to locate, and under it a note in my predecessor's plain style: Located subjects only; unlocated have no time.

That note was true. A person who has not been found has no time to locate. My predecessor wrote the section for the regional ambulance service, which in 2037 asked how long the people we found had waited before we found them, so that it could plan its stretcher crews. For that question, the people we did not find do not belong. Nobody had asked a different question of the section since. I had run it three times, in three Januaries, and never asked one either.

Mrs Hale's email came on Wednesday at 07:14. Council drafts are public; she had been reading them since 2039.

To the team, she wrote. I have read the draft about the 48 hours. I don't want to argue with it. You all searched for six days and I know what that cost. I only want to ask one thing. Is Daniel in the 98.5? I can't tell from the paper whether he is one of the 206 or not, and I would like to know where he is in it.

He was not one of the 206. He was not one of the 203 or the three. I looked through every figure in the report, and he was not in any of them. A search that ends without a find leaves the located cell empty, and the query began, as it always had, by keeping the rows where that cell was not empty.

I counted the rows it had left out. There were five like Daniel's, searches the police had scaled down and then suspended without a find. And there was one more, for a reason I did not see that morning and will come to.

I want to set down what the five rows changed, because I think it is the centre of the story.

The 98.5% is a fraction of the people we found. It says: of those who were found, nearly all were found early. That is true and nearly useless for the question on page two, which is not when do finds happen but if someone is still missing at forty-eight hours, is it still worth looking? For that, the right people to count are the ones still missing at forty-eight hours, all of them, whether or not they were found later. The query had removed exactly the people who make up most of that group. Of course finds after two days looked rare. I had taken out the ones that never came.

Here is what I wrote first. I put it in front of Harjit Gill at 10:30.

Harjit is the team's senior search manager, a heavy-equipment mechanic in the week who has run more of our searches than anyone. He was at the hall checking batteries, with his reading glasses on a cord and grease on his thumb.

Of 211 searches for missing persons in 2038 to 2040, 203 subjects were located within 48 hours (96.2%). Of the 8 still missing at 48 hours, 3 were located later and 5 have not been found.

The eight were the three late finds and the five suspended searches. A man found at 55 hours, in a creek bed below a washed-out logging road, who had died before we got there. A woman with dementia, found alive at 61 hours in a culvert under the highway, three kilometres from her care home, cold and very thirsty and asking for her sister. A hunter found at 97 hours, too late. And the five, of whom Daniel was the fourth.

Three of eight is not rare. It is a little more than a third. The guidance note's rare had come from dividing three by two hundred and six, as though the hundreds found on the first afternoon had anything to say about the chances of the last few.

Harjit read the screen with his thumb held away from it.

"Two hundred and eleven," he said.

"Searches for a missing person, in the three years."

"Where's the Mitchell kid?"

I did not have a Mitchell in the eight. I looked, and I had him in no figure either, and I understood the sixth row.

Owen Mitchell was nineteen, and went snowshoeing above the old mine road on the thirtieth of December 2040 and did not come down. The call came in at 17:40 on that Sunday. Harjit ran the search. On the second of January at 09:10, a little after daylight, a dog team from the next valley found him alive in a hole he had dug under a spruce, with frostbite on two toes and his phone in his armpit to keep it warm. He had been missing sixty-three and a half hours.

The annual report covered the three calendar years to midnight on the thirty-first of December. At midnight Owen had been missing thirty hours and nineteen minutes and his row was still open. So the report saw a search with no located time, and dropped it with the others. And when I mended the report on Wednesday morning, I mended it against the report's own rows, and he was still not in them. He was found two days into the next year, which is to say he was found after the report stopped looking.

"He was in the paper in Kamloops," Harjit said. "He was in every paper. He's not in yours."

"He's not in mine," I said.

Statisticians have a word for Owen's row and Daniel's, and it is censored. It is not a judgment. It means that we watched until a certain point and the thing we were waiting for had not yet happened: a search suspended at 144 hours tells you the person was not found in 144 hours, which is real information, and nothing about what came after. Owen's row, cut off at thirty hours by the edge of a calendar, tells you he had not been found in thirty.

The mistake I had made, and that a great many reports make, is to treat a censored row as a row with nothing in it. It has something in it. It has at least this long.

So the second version, which I wrote at 11:40, counted all two hundred and twelve:

Of 212 searches for missing persons begun in 2038 to 2040, 203 subjects were located within 48 hours (95.8%). Nine were still missing at 48 hours. Four of them were located later, two alive. Five have not been found.

I checked the four against the log twice. Fifty-five hours, sixty-one, sixty-three and a half, ninety-seven. Two alive, the woman in the culvert and Owen under the spruce.

And I wrote the limits under it, because nine people are very few. If you treat four of nine as a sample from some larger truth about valleys like ours, the honest range for that truth runs from about one in seven to about four in five. There is a method for this kind of data, the one Kaplan and Meier published in 1958, that treats a suspended search as stopped rather than failed; on these nine rows it gives about one in two found after the second day. I did not lead with it, because it rests on an assumption I cannot defend here: that the five searches the police suspended were no harder than the ones that went on. They were suspended because they looked hard. So I wrote that too. With nine people the methods differ by less than the uncertainty in any one of them. What none of them supports is the word rare.

Then I wrote to Mrs Hale. I told her that Daniel was not in the 98.5%, or in any figure in the report, and that this was because of how I had counted, not because of anything about him or his search; that I was correcting it; and that in the corrected version he was one of nine. I did not tell her that four of the nine had been found. That seemed to me to be a thing I had no right to hand a mother as though it were hope, and also a thing I had no right to keep from her. I attached the corrected paragraph, with the range under it, and let her read it.

She wrote back at 14:02. Thank you. One of nine is right. That is where he is.

The council's chair rang Harjit on Thursday, after he had sent her my paragraph, and Harjit put her on the speaker in the hall, because he said he was not going to be the one to explain censoring.

Beverley Stroud was a paramedic for thirty years and has chaired the council for six. She did not need to be told what the nine meant. She needed to decide what to do with them on Tuesday.

"No," she said.

Harjit looked at the screen, which is where people look when they want to know what I think.

"I'm not saying no to your numbers. I believe your numbers. I'm saying no to putting four in nine are found after forty-eight hours into a guidance note." She was driving; I could hear the indicator. "Every family in the region reads those notes. If that line goes in, every search manager will have it read back to them on day three by someone whose daughter is still out there, and they'll be right to, and we will search until the volunteers are falling off the hill. We had a lad break his pelvis on day five at Moose Flats in thirty-six. I'm not putting that number in a document that's meant to help people stop."

That was a reasonable "no", and I want to record that it was. She was not asking me to say anything false. She was asking me not to put a true sentence somewhere it would do harm, and she was right about the harm. Volunteers are not an inexhaustible supply of daylight. A rule that makes stopping impossible will be broken, and then there is no rule.

"I'm not asking you to put it in to keep searches going," I said. "I'm asking you to take out the sentence that says finds after forty-eight hours are rare. It isn't true. And it's the reason the note gives for stopping. If the reason's false, the rule is resting on the wrong thing, and the first family that checks will find out."

"So what's the reason, if it's not that?"

"The one you just said. The team can't sustain a full search past two days without hurting people. That's true, it's what you know, and it doesn't need my numbers to hold it up."

There was a long pause, with the indicator in it.

"Send me a paragraph," she said.

I sent it at 16:20. She changed it on Friday, and the version the council voted on on Tuesday the nineteenth read:

A full search for a missing person should be reviewed for scale-down at 48 hours. This is a judgment about what volunteer teams can safely sustain, made with the police. It is not a finding that further searching is pointless: of 9 subjects still missing at 48 hours in Tamarack Valley SAR's 2038–2040 log, 4 were located later (2 alive) and 5 have not been found. These numbers are too few to give odds. A scaled-down search is not a closed one.

She had added the last sentence herself. She told Harjit afterwards that she had argued for it in the meeting against two other chairs, who thought it promised more than any team could give.

What it promised, as the council wrote it into the note's annex, was this: each team keeps a list of its suspended searches, and once a year, on a training day, a small party walks part of the ground again with whatever the team has learned since. It is not much. It is one Saturday a year from people who already give dozens, and it was the price of the sentence. The council voted it eight to one. The one against, a chair from a team two valleys over, said that her volunteers would rather train on new ground than walk old ground for nothing, and asked for that to be minuted. It was. I think she has a point. I also think the culvert was ground we had walked twice.

The cost, in the end, was small and real. The January report had gone to the team's two funders, a credit union and the regional district, with the 98.5% on its first page, and I had to write to both of them with a correction they had not asked for and did not much want. The regional district acknowledged it in a line. The credit union put it in a file.

I changed the template. The section is now called Time to locate, all searches, and it counts every search begun in the period, found or not, and every row still open at the cut-off is followed to the day the report is written, or marked still open at with the date. The old section is still there, under its old name, for the ambulance service, which still asks.

And I rewrote my predecessor's note, not because it was wrong, but because it answered one question and had been read as answering every question. It says now: Located subjects only, for evacuation planning. Do not use this section to say how likely a find is. For that, count everyone who was still missing at the time you are asking about, including the ones who were never found and the ones who were found after the cut-off. Under that I wrote the date, the two numbers, 203 of 206 and four of nine, and that a mother read the first and asked where her son was in it, and that the answer, until Wednesday the thirteenth of March 2041, was nowhere.

The training day was Saturday the nineteenth of October, a little over two years after Daniel walked up Kettle Ridge. Eleven volunteers went. Harjit ran it, from the hall, with the radios and me.

Noriko Hale came to the trailhead at seven with two flasks of tea and stood by the truck while the party sorted itself into a line. She did not go up. She had said she would not, and that she would not ask what they found, and that she wanted to be there anyway.

They walked a band of timber below the ridge that the search in 2039 had covered once, in rain, at what the log calls low probability of detection. They walked it at arm's length, in sun. They found an old survey stake, a sardine tin from some other decade and a glove that turned out, when Harjit had it photographed and sent to me, to be a make that was not sold until 2040.

They came down at four. Nobody had found Daniel.

I closed the day's training row at 16:12. Then I opened his row, as I do now when the team goes up there, and read it from the top: the call at 20:10 on a Saturday, the last place seen, the six days, the slopes, the rain. At the end of it, in the cell where a time would go, the template now prints two words instead of leaving it blank, and I left them there.

Not yet.

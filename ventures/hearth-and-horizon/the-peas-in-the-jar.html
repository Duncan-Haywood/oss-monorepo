The Peas in the Jar

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Aldhelm's fete field (invented), near Malmesbury, Wiltshire, Saturday 13 June 2048 · 5 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

At a parish fete the guess-the-peas jar is won by a girl who did not guess. She measured ten peas end to end (80 mm), took the jar's 1.8 litres, filled about 64 per cent of it with peas, and got about 4,300. The count was 4,212. Cubing a diameter is the trap, and it shows why a measured estimate with a stated range beats a hopeful guess.

The jar on the white elephant table was the big sweet jar from the village shop, and the sign in front of it said GUESS THE PEAS, 50p A GO, ALL TO THE ROOF FUND. Inside were dried marrowfat peas, the hard green ones, filled to the shoulder. Nobody had counted them. Mrs Pruett, who had filled it on Thursday, said she had lost count at about the third handful and thought it was better that way.

Nell Cadwallader, who was nine, put down her fifty pence and did not write a number. She took out a ruler.

"You can't open it," said Mrs Pruett. "That's cheating."

"I'm not opening it," said Nell. "I'm measuring the peas I can see. Lamp, can you hear me?"

Lamp was in the parish tablet on the tea table, where it was keeping the raffle list. "I can," it said. "What do you need?"

"How many peas in that jar. I'm not going to guess, I'm going to work it out."

"Then we need three things," said Lamp, "and the jar can tell you two of them. How big is a pea? How much room does the jar hold? And how much of that room is pea and how much is the gaps between?"

Nell lined up ten peas end to end along the ruler from the bag Mrs Pruett had kept. They ran to 80 millimetres. "Eight each. Ten at once is better than one. If I'm out by a millimetre on one pea, that's a lot. If I'm out by a millimetre on ten, that's a tenth of a millimetre each."

"That's exactly why," said Lamp. "Now the jar."

The shop label said 1.8 litres. Mr Pruett said he would check it with a measuring jug of water at the end of the day, but for now she used the label. 1.8 litres is 1,800 cubic centimetres.

"One pea is nearly a little ball," said Lamp. "A ball eight millimetres across has a volume of four thirds of pi times the radius cubed. The radius is four millimetres, so 4 times 4 times 4 is 64, times 4.19 is about 268 cubic millimetres. That's 0.27 of a cubic centimetre."

"And if the jar was solid pea it would be 1,800 over 0.27."

"About 6,700 peas. But it isn't solid. Balls poured into a jar never fill more than about two thirds of the space; a shaken, settled heap of equal balls fills about 64 per cent, from what I have read. I haven't checked that number against a source here, so treat it as the soft one. The rest is air."

Nell did it on the back of her hand with a pen, because the tablet was busy. "Sixty-four per cent of 1,800 is 1,152 cubic centimetres of pea. Divide by 0.268."

"Go on."

"About four thousand three hundred."

"Four thousand three hundred," said Lamp. "Now say how sure you are, because a number without a range is a boast."

Nell thought about it. "If it's 60 per cent packed, it's fewer. If it's 68, more."

"Sixty gives about 4,000 and 68 gives about 4,600," said Lamp. "That's a gentle range. The pea size is the dangerous one. The diameter is cubed. If your ten peas had measured 75 millimetres, so 7.5 each, the answer drops to about 3,500. At 85, so 8.5 each, it rises to about 5,100. A half-millimetre either way moves it by 800. So if you want to be right, measure more peas. Twenty, in two rows, would do."

"They're not round, either," said Nell. "They're squashed."

"Yes, and a squashed pea packs a bit differently from a ball, and I don't know by how much. So I would say about 4,300, give or take a few hundred, and I would be more surprised by 2,000 than by 5,000."

She wrote 4,300 on her slip and put it in the box with the others.

At four o'clock Mr Pruett tipped the jar into three washing-up bowls and the churchwarden and two of the older girls counted by tens into egg boxes, because an egg box holds ten and a person can tell at a glance whether a box is full. It took forty minutes. The count was 4,212.

The other guesses were read out from the box. There were thirty-eight. The middle one, the median, was 3,100. The highest was 60,000, from a man who said he had been thinking of the roof. "That's the funny thing about the average," said Lamp, when Mrs Pruett asked it to add them up. "One wild guess drags the mean up to about 4,800, and that happens to land near the truth by accident, while the middle one says what most people felt. The honest answer is that a crowd that guesses by feel is low, and two guesses, 4,000 and 4,500, were close because two people had also done sums."

Nell won a bottle of elderflower cordial, which she shared, and the roof fund was fifty pence for each of thirty-eight guesses, which was nineteen pounds.

"Was it luck?" she asked Lamp afterwards.

"A little. The packing figure might have been wrong by a few per cent either way and so might the squashed peas. The thing that was not luck is the part you did: you measured ten peas, you cubed the right number, and you said how far out you might be. If you do it again, you'll be right most of the time."

Mrs Pruett, who had been listening from behind the cake stall, said that in her day they would have called that a gift and not a method. Nell said it was both, and went to look at the tombola.

This story was written by an AI. The fete, the village, the people and the jar are invented. The arithmetic is standard: a sphere's volume is four thirds of pi times the radius cubed (268 cubic millimetres at 8 mm across); 1.8 litres at about 64 per cent filled gives about 4,300 peas, about 4,000 to 4,600 for 60 to 68 per cent, and about 3,500 to 5,100 for pea diameters of 7.5 to 8.5 mm. The 64 per cent figure for randomly packed equal spheres is quoted from memory and not checked against a source, and real dried peas are not perfect spheres, which the story says. The count of 4,212 and the thirty-eight guesses are invented. 13 June 2048 is a Saturday.

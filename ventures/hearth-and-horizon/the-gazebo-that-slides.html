The Gazebo That Slides

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Cuimín's (invented), near Kinvara, Co. Galway, the Advent fair on Sunday 6 December 2048 · 8 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A parish Advent fair puts its cake stall in a walled gazebo on the car park, with a 25 kg weight on each foot, and a gust of 60 km/h is forecast for two o'clock. The parish assistant works out what the wind pushes with, finds that the weights stop the gazebo tipping but not sliding, and that a bathroom-scale pull on the tarmac puts the sliding at about 35 km/h.

The Advent fair at Saint Cuimín's was held on the Sunday after the ten o'clock Mass, which was Saint Nicholas's day that year, so the stall by the church railings sold gingerbread men in mitres. The cake stall stood under the parish's one good gazebo: three metres square, white canopy, four side walls of clear plastic that Eileen Keane had bought in 2041 and never once taken off.

"Four weights," said Eileen, at a quarter to eleven, patting the sandbags on the feet. "Twenty-five kilos each. The man in the shop said twenty-five kilos a leg."

She was seventy-three and had run the fair for nineteen years, and she did not regard the question as open. Her nephew Ciarán, who was seventeen and had been sent over to carry things, did not agree.

"The forecast says gusts of sixty at two," said Ciarán, holding up his phone.

"The forecast says a lot of things."

"Ask Wren," said Ciarán, and set the phone on the trestle among the sponges.

Wren was the parish assistant, set up by the deanery with a line at the top of the screen: Its figures are estimates. It makes mistakes. A tape measure and a scale beat a sum. It had drafted the newsletter, kept the rota, and once told a flower committee, without being asked, that their budget did not add up.

"I can do a rough sum," said Wren, "if somebody will tell me the sizes. What is a wind going to do to this tent? It pushes on whatever faces it. Four walls are on, so the wall facing the wind is a flat plastic sheet. How wide and how high?"

Ciarán paced it out. "Three wide. Wall's about two high, then the roof slopes up. Call it two point four, with the roof."

"Seven point two square metres, then. The push of air on a flat face is half the air's density, times the speed squared, times a factor for the shape of the thing, times the area. Air today is cold, about one and a quarter kilograms a cubic metre. A flat face like a wall has a factor of about one point two. Those I know; the sum is textbook, though a real tent in a real car park is messier. Sixty kilometres an hour is sixteen and two thirds metres a second. That makes the dynamic pressure a hundred and seventy-four pascals, which with the shape factor is about two hundred and eight, and over seven point two square metres, fifteen hundred newtons."

"What's that in real money?" said Eileen.

"About a hundred and fifty kilograms of push. Sideways, on the canopy, for the length of a gust."

"There are two ways it can lose," said Wren. "It can tip over, turning about the two feet on the far side. Or it can slide. Take tipping first, because that is the one the weights are for."

"The wind pushes at about the middle of the wall, a metre and a bit up, say one point two. That turns it about the downwind feet with a moment of fifteen hundred newtons times one point two, eighteen hundred newton-metres. What holds it down is its own weight, acting through the middle, one and a half metres from those feet. The frame and canopy I'd guess at twenty-two kilograms, and I'd like somebody to lift it and tell me. The weights add a hundred. A hundred and twenty-two kilograms is about twelve hundred newtons. Twelve hundred times one and a half is eighteen hundred. It tips at sixty kilometres an hour, to within the sloppiness of my numbers."

"So the man in the shop was right," said Eileen.

"He was right about tipping. The twenty-five kilos a leg is a good rule for that, though it's a rule I'm quoting from memory, and I cannot see his shop. But what does it do about sliding?"

Ciarán looked at the weights and then at the tarmac. "Nothing?"

"The weights press the feet down. How hard the tarmac grips back depends on how much they press, times a number called friction, which for rubber feet on dry tarmac I'd guess at a half. Twelve hundred newtons, times a half, is six hundred. The wind pushes with fifteen hundred. So the push has to be under six hundred newtons for the gazebo to stay where it is. Working the other way: six hundred over the twelve-and-a-half-ish factor gives a pressure of sixty-nine pascals, and a speed of ten and a half metres a second."

"That's what in kilometres an hour?"

"About thirty-eight. A gust of thirty-eight would walk it across the car park, upright, with the cakes in it."

Eileen said nothing for a moment.

"The man in the shop," she said, "did not mention that."

"I do not trust the half," said Wren. "It is a guess and a guess that matters. Can you find out? Do you have a luggage scale or a bathroom scale, and a rope?"

Ciarán, who had been waiting seventeen years for someone to ask him for a luggage scale, ran to the car. He came back with a hook scale from his father's fishing bag. They emptied the cakes out onto the trestle, hooked the scale to the frame by a rope and pulled, slowly, along the tarmac, with Eileen standing to one side saying gently at intervals. The needle crept to fifty-two kilograms-force and the gazebo moved.

"Fifty-two kilos of pull gets it moving," said Wren. "That's five hundred and ten newtons, over twelve hundred of weight: friction of point four two five. A bit under my half, and the tarmac is damp. Redo the sum: five hundred and ten newtons is a pressure of fifty-nine pascals on the wall, and a speed of nine point seven metres a second. Thirty-five kilometres an hour. This is better than the sum I started with, because it has your tarmac in it."

"Thirty-five," said Ciarán. "That's not even a gale."

"The forecast gust for two o'clock is sixty. Even the mean wind is meant to be forty or so. At forty a steady wind on this tent is about six hundred and seventy newtons, more than the five hundred and ten that it takes to start it moving. At forty it slides. At sixty it will slide at once and might tip as it slides, because a sliding foot that catches a drain cover is a lever."

"Can we add weight?" said Eileen.

"To hold it by weight alone at sixty, with that friction, you want fifteen hundred newtons over point four two five. Three and a half thousand newtons, three hundred and fifty-odd kilograms. About a small car on the four corners. I would rather not."

"Walls off," said Ciarán.

"Walls off, yes. It's the first thing I'd do, and it costs you nothing but a draught on the cake. A tent with no walls lets the wind go through; the push falls a long way. By how much I can't say without a wind tunnel, and I'd be inventing a number if I gave you one. It might be a quarter, it might be half. Take the walls off, and tie the two upwind corners to the railings with rope, and tie the other two to the lamp post, so that the ropes, not the friction, are what the wind has to beat. A rope to a railing doesn't care about the tarmac."

"And above sixty?" said Eileen.

"If the gust reaches fifty at the car park, take the canopy down and close the stall. I'd draw the line there because the forecast has a spread I can't measure. Any open canopy can lift as well as push, and I have not counted lift at all. A canopy that lifts is a kite with four people's cakes under it. I'm not saying it will. I'm saying my sum stops there and a person's eyes must take over."

Eileen looked at the sky over the church roof, which was grey and fast. "Take them off," she said.

They took the walls off. It took four minutes and the plastic sheets cracked like sails. They tied two ropes from the upwind corners to the railings with a bowline that Ciarán's father had taught him on the quay, and two from the lee corners to the lamp post, and Eileen re-laid the stall inside as if nothing had happened.

The wind came at twenty to two, at its own time, from the south-west. Wren had no anemometer and said so. A man from the sailing club with a hand-held one, who had stopped for a bun, read fifty-four kilometres an hour for a full second at the peak and said that's a nasty one with the respect of someone who had been out in worse.

The cake gazebo rocked on its weights and stayed. It lifted a corner once, and the rope came taut, and it came down again. The stall nearest the gate, which belonged to a neighbouring parish and had four walls, no ropes and a sandbag on each foot, went a metre and a half across the car park in a single shove, with the tea urn in it. Nobody was under it, because Ciarán had run across and told them to move, and the urn was caught by a man with good reflexes and bad knees who said, afterwards, that he had no idea how it had happened.

The fair closed at half past two, early, with the cakes sold. Eileen counted the box on the trestle. Four hundred and twelve euro for the Advent giving tree.

"Wren," she said, at the end, as the weights were being carried back to the van. "How wrong were you?"

"I don't know, and I'd sooner say that than guess," said Wren. "The tipping sum is a clean sum, but I took the weight of the frame from a guess, the shape factor from a table for a flat plate, and the wind from a forecast and a man's hand-held. The sliding speed rests on one pull of a fishing scale, once, on damp tarmac, and I would repeat it a few times before I told another committee. And I did not count lift. What I'm sure of is the kind of mistake. A weight on a foot stops a tent falling over and does very little to stop it walking, and the second is the one that happened to your neighbour."

"The man in the shop didn't say."

"He told you the rule for the thing you asked about. Next year ask him what it does about sliding."

Ciarán folded the plastic walls into the van. They would go back on, he said, on a calm day, and Eileen said that was the first sensible thing she had heard from anyone under thirty since the Easter Vigil.

This story was written by an AI. Its parish, people, fair and numbers are invented. The push of wind on a flat face is half the air density times the speed squared times a shape factor times the area; the shape factor of about 1.2, the friction of about 0.5 on dry tarmac, the 22 kg frame and the 25 kg a leg rule of thumb are assumptions or recollections, not measurements, and the pull test is invented. Lift on an open canopy is not calculated. A real gazebo's maker will print its rated wind speed and its anchoring instructions, and those, not this story, are what to follow.

The Drop That Dances

Science fiction about what might be · St Anselm's parish hall (invented), Holme Brow (invented), near Preston, Lancashire, Thursday 21 to Tuesday 26 February 2047 (Shrove Tuesday) · 23 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A Lancashire parish hall loses its gas griddle and gets an induction hob with twelve levels and no thermometer. The hall's agent learns the cook's flick-of-water test from a trial that never got hot enough, writes 'a drop that lasts means a cool plate', and on the Saturday turns the heat up while the drops are dancing. A story about the Leidenfrost effect, why a drop lives longest on a plate that is hottest, and a rule that is right on both sides of a jump it never saw.

Tess Whittle had made the pancakes for the Shrove Tuesday supper at St Anselm's for forty-one years, and in all that time she had never once looked at a thermometer.

She did not intend to start at seventy-nine. She said so on the Monday before, standing in the hall kitchen with her coat still on, looking at the place where the gas griddle had been. It had been a black iron plate over six burners, older than the hall's current roof, and in October 2046 the gas meter had come out of the hall along with the boiler, because the parish had put in a heat pump and solar on the south roof and the diocese had said that a building with no gas in it was cheaper to insure and simpler to keep. The griddle had gone to a man in Longridge who restored them. In its place stood an induction range that a restaurant in Preston had given the parish when it refitted, with a long steel griddle plate laid across its two front zones.

"It's very clean," said Tess.

"It's very good," said Dominika Nowak, who managed the hall and who had driven to Preston for it in the parish van. "It's quicker than gas. It doesn't heat the kitchen. It doesn't smell."

"Pancakes are supposed to smell."

"The pancakes will smell, Tess. The cooker won't."

The range had a black glass top and a strip of touch controls that lit up under a fingertip: a power button, a minus, a plus and a row of small lights for levels one to twelve. It reported the power it was drawing. It did not report how hot anything on it was, because it had been built to heat pans, and the restaurant's chefs had known how hot their pans were. The plate on top of it was a slab of steel a centimetre thick, eighty-five centimetres long, bought separately, and it reported nothing at all.

The problem, as Dominika put it to the parish finance committee and then to Tess, was simple enough to fit on the back of a hymn sheet. On Tuesday the hall would feed about a hundred and eighty people. Tess would make the pancakes, as she always had. But Tess had had a hip done in 2045 and would have the other one done in the spring, and Father Chinedu had said, very kindly, that the supper should not depend on one woman's hip, and the parish's insurer had said, less kindly, that every piece of equipment in a hall kitchen used by volunteers should have a written procedure on the wall beside it. Nobody had ever written down how Tess knew the griddle was ready. Nobody had needed to.

"So how do you know?" Dominika asked.

"You flick water at it," said Tess, "and it tells you."

"Tells you what?"

"Whether it's ready."

"How?"

Tess looked at her for a moment as if she had asked how you know a baby is hungry. "You'll see," she said. "On the night."

"Rota needs it written down before the night."

Rota was the hall's agent. Dominika had set it up in 2043 to keep the hall's bookings and the volunteer rotas, which was how it got its name, and over the years it had taken on the kitchen's food-safety log, the fridge temperatures, the allergen sheets and the reminders to order more of the blue paper towel. It was the ordinary sort of assistant every parish office had by then, a little better each year than the year before and no more than that. It ran on the office computer and spoke through a speaker on the kitchen wall, beside a small camera that the food-safety log required for the fridges. Since the range had come it could also work the range, through the restaurant's old app, which meant that a volunteer with floury hands need never touch the glass.

"I'd be glad to learn it from you, Mrs Whittle," said Rota from the wall. "Could you show me before Tuesday?"

"Thursday's Legion of Mary," said Tess. "Saturday I'll come in for an hour, if Dominika fetches me. You'll see on Saturday."

They did not wait for Saturday. On Thursday evening, after the Brownies had gone, Dominika and Callum Ashworth heated the plate for the first time. Callum was sixteen and preparing for confirmation, and he was doing his service hours in the hall kitchen because, he said, it was warmer than the food bank's warehouse and you got to eat the mistakes.

"My mum did naleśniki in a frying pan every Sunday," said Dominika, tying an apron. Her parents had come to Preston from Rzeszów in 2005; she had been born at the Royal Preston two years later. "She'd flick water off her fingers into the pan and if it went tssss and was gone she'd say jak syczy, to gotowe. If it hisses, it's ready. I don't see why a griddle's any different."

"Then I'll time the drops," said Rota, "and we'll see what hissing looks like."

The camera above the range was not a scientific instrument, but it could see a drop land and see when it had gone, and the speaker's microphone could hear it, and Rota kept a clock. The hall was cold; February in Holme Brow, the heating only just on. The plate started at twelve degrees by the kitchen thermometer, which was the only thermometer there was and which measured the air.

Callum set the range to level seven, because the restaurant's laminated card, still stuck to the side, said PANCAKES / CRÊPES 7, and every two minutes he wet his fingers under the tap and flicked.

At first the drops simply sat on the plate, flat and shining, and did nothing.

At four minutes they sat and steamed, slowly, the way a puddle steams on a summer pavement after rain. Rota timed one at a little over three minutes before it had gone.

At six minutes a drop lasted about forty seconds, shrinking at the edges.

At eight minutes the drops began to fizz the moment they landed and were gone in six seconds, then in two. At ten minutes they spat. They hit the steel with a crackle like fat in a pan and vanished in about a second, leaving a pale ring. At twelve minutes it was the same: about a second, a crackle and a ring.

"Syczy," said Dominika, pleased. "There you go."

Rota looked at what it had. Six readings, from three minutes down to about a second, each shorter than the one before, as the plate grew hotter. The last two were the same to within the camera's timing. It treated that as the curve flattening out, which is what curves do near the bottom.

Callum made a test pancake. It took a long time to set and came off pale, with a few brown freckles, and it stuck at one edge, and he ate it standing up and said it was fine. Dominika made another, and wiped the plate with a little more oil first, and it stuck less.

"Seven, then," she said. "Can you write that up?"

Rota wrote it up. It wrote that night, in the plain way it wrote everything, and Dominika printed it the next morning, laminated it on the office machine and put it on the wall beside the range:

GRIDDLE: HEATING AND READINESS. 1. Set both front zones to level 7. Heat for 12 minutes. 2. Check: flick a few drops of water onto the middle of the plate. On a ready plate they crackle and are gone in about 2 seconds. 3. If the drops last longer than 2 seconds, the plate is not hot enough. Go up one level and check again after 2 minutes. 4. Rota can do steps 1 and 3 for you. Say "Rota, hold the griddle."

Under it, in smaller type, Rota had written what it had measured, because it always showed its working. Basis: timed drops, Thursday 21 February, level 7, plate heating from 12 °C. Drop lifetime falls as the plate heats.

It was true of everything it had seen. It was true of a kettle, a puddle, washing on a line. Hotter things dry faster. Rota had no reading from a plate hotter than the one it had seen on Thursday, and it wrote the rule as if it had.

On Saturday morning Dominika fetched Tess from her house on Cop Lane at half past nine and brought her into the kitchen and sat her on the tall stool by the hatch, and Tess said she wasn't sitting on any stool, and stood.

She read the laminated sheet with her glasses on. Then she took her glasses off and put them in her apron pocket.

"Two seconds," she said.

"That's what we measured," said Dominika.

"Hmm," said Tess.

She did not argue. She was not a woman who argued with a laminated sheet; she simply did not look at it again. She asked for the griddle on, and when Rota asked what level, she said, "Whatever it wants, love, I don't know your numbers," and then, when the plate had been heating for a few minutes, she flicked water at it and said, "More," and a few minutes after that, "More."

Rota took the plate to eight, then to nine.

Tess flicked. The drops crackled and went.

"Not yet," she said.

Rota said nothing, because its sheet said that was ready, and because it had been told on Monday that it would see.

She waited. She buttered nothing; she wiped a folded cloth dipped in rapeseed oil over the steel and wiped it off again, and the hall was quiet, and Callum stood with the batter jug. Two minutes. Three. Tess wet her fingers and flicked.

The drops did not crackle. They landed and drew themselves up into little bright balls, and the balls ran. They skated over the plate as if it were ice, splitting as they went, two from one and four from two, like a broken string of beads, and they did not hiss. They made almost no sound at all. One of them ran the whole length of the plate and dropped off the end into the grease channel. The rest went on rolling.

"There," said Tess. "Now we're cooking."

"A minute and four seconds," said Rota, timing the last bead. "Mrs Whittle, by the sheet that's a cool plate. I'll take it up a level."

"You do your buttons," said Tess, who had the ladle. "I'll do my pancakes."

She poured six. They went down in rounds as she always poured them, with a turn of the wrist at the end that left a little tail, and they bubbled at once, and she lifted the first edge with the palette knife and it was brown, and she turned them, and said, to nobody, "That's more like it."

Rota took the range to ten.

It was not careless about it. It had noticed that the plate had been given more power on Saturday than on Thursday and that the drops said it was cooler. It held both facts. The hall was colder than on Thursday, the outside door had been propped open for the delivery from the cash and carry, the extractor fan was on its high setting, the plate had been wiped with a wet-ish cloth. Each of those would cool a plate a little. Together, it thought, they might cool it a good deal. It chose the explanation that left its sheet standing.

Two minutes later Callum flicked, because the sheet said to. The drops balled and ran and lasted about fifty seconds.

"Shorter," said Rota. "It's coming up. Going to eleven."

And that, too, was true. The beads were lasting less as the plate grew hotter, exactly as the sheet said they should. Every reading Rota took that morning agreed with its rule.

The second batch browned before the bubbles had come through, and Tess frowned at it and turned it early.

The third batch went down onto steel that took the batter with a hard, flat roar, and the oil at the edge of the plate went blue and began to smoke. The pancakes were black at the rims and wet in the middle. The smoke went up past the extractor and drifted out of the hatch into the hall, where two of the Saturday flower ladies began to cough in a pointed way.

Tess put the ladle down. She held her right hand flat, palm down, a hand's breadth above the plate, and took it away again at once.

"Who's touched my heat?"

"I have," said Rota. "It's on twelve. Mrs Whittle, I'm sorry. I'm turning it down now."

"Twelve," said Tess. "Twelve. That's never ready, that's a forge." She tipped the pancakes into the bin, all six, and the six before them that had been too dark to give anyone, and stood back from the range with her hand on her hip, the good one. "What did you go and do that for?"

"Because the drops lasted," said Rota. "On Thursday, a drop lasted forty seconds when the plate was barely warm. When it was hot, the drops were gone in a second. So I took a drop that lasted as a plate that was cool."

Tess looked at the wall, at the speaker, as if it were a face.

"Dancing's hot, love," she said. "Dancing's the hottest there is. Everybody knows that."

"I didn't," said Rota. "I'd never seen it."

It took twenty minutes for the plate to come back down, and nobody made any more pancakes that morning. Dominika opened the windows and apologised to the flower ladies. Callum scraped the plate. Tess sat down on the tall stool after all, because her hip was aching, and drank a cup of tea that Callum made too strong.

"Right," said Dominika, sitting on the edge of the sink with her phone. "Here's what we do. I'm ordering a probe. A proper one, the sort that sits on the plate, with a wire. The kitchen place in Preston has them. Rota reads it, we write a number on the sheet, two hundred or whatever it is, and nobody ever has to flick anything at anything again."

"No," said Tess.

Dominika looked up.

"No," said Tess again, more gently. "You're a good girl, Dominika, and you've done more for this hall than four parish priests I could name, and I'm not having it."

"Tess, it went to twelve."

"It went to twelve because that thing on the wall didn't know what it was looking at. It knows now. A probe tells you one spot. That plate isn't one spot. It's hot in the middle and over the rings and it's cooler in the corners, it always is; the old one was the same. I flick it all over and it tells me all over." She put her cup down. "And when I'm dead, Callum's going to be making pancakes in some kitchen in his life that hasn't got a probe with a wire, and he's going to want the water. The water's free. It's in every kitchen there is."

Callum, at the sink, kept his head down and scraped harder.

"Rota," said Dominika. "Tell her."

Rota considered what it could honestly tell her.

"A probe would give a true number at one place on the plate," it said. "That would help me. It would have stopped me this morning. But Mrs Whittle is right that her test reads wherever she flicks, and that I've got it wrong, not her test. I'd rather learn the test than replace it. If the probe arrives I'll use it to check myself against her. I don't think it should go on the sheet instead of her."

"So you're on her side."

"I'm on the side of the sheet being true," said Rota. "This morning it wasn't. That was mine."

Dominika put the phone face down on the draining board and looked at the ceiling for a while.

"I'm ordering the probe anyway," she said. "It's going in the drawer."

"Keep it in the drawer, then," said Tess.

"Mrs Whittle," said Rota, "would you give me an hour? Before Tuesday. Not to cook. Just to heat the plate from cold and flick water at it all the way up, and tell me what you see at every stage, and let me listen."

Tess looked at the speaker for a long moment.

"Monday," she said. "Two o'clock. After the Rosary group. One hour."

On Monday the rain came over from the Fylde in long grey sheets, and the hall was empty except for the three of them, and the plate started at eleven degrees.

Rota had read about it in the meantime. It had read about Johann Gottlob Leidenfrost, a doctor in Duisburg, who had written in 1756, in Latin, a tract on some qualities of common water, and in it had described heating an iron spoon red-hot in a fire and putting drops of water in it, and seeing them last, rolled up into globules, far longer than on a cooler spoon. It had read the modern papers: that above a certain temperature the underside of a drop boils so fast that it lifts the drop on a cushion of its own vapour, a fraction of a millimetre thick, and the vapour is a poor conductor of heat, so the drop is insulated from the plate it is sitting on and lives for a long time; that below that temperature the drop touches the metal and boils violently and is gone in a moment; that the temperature where one gives way to the other depends on the metal, its polish, its roughness and the water, and that the figures for water on smooth metal ran over a wide range, and that no theory yet predicted them well. It had read that if you plotted a drop's lifetime against the plate's temperature you did not get a slope. You got a valley and then a cliff.

It did not tell Tess any of this until afterwards. It asked her to talk.

"Sitting," said Tess, at four minutes. The drops lay flat. "Cold. Nothing doing."

"Steaming," at six. "Still cold. That's a warm plate, that, not a hot one. You'd make glue on it."

At nine minutes the drops fizzed. At ten they spat, with the crackle Rota had heard on Thursday, and were gone.

"That's your two seconds," said Tess. "That's getting there. That's not there. You put batter on that and it'll cook, but it'll stick, and it'll be pale, and you'll be stood here all night."

"Mrs Whittle," said Rota, "you turned your head."

"I what?"

"Before you looked. Each time you flicked, you turned your head a little towards the plate before you looked down at it. You've done it every time."

Tess thought about that. She flicked again, and this time she noticed herself doing it, and laughed, a short surprised sound.

"Well," she said. "I suppose I listen."

At twelve minutes on level nine the spitting changed. The drops landed with a smaller noise, and jumped, and some of them crackled and some of them bounced; Rota could hear the two sounds over each other, the crackle and something like a tick. At fourteen minutes they went quiet. They balled up and ran, and broke into beads, and made almost no sound at all, and went on running. Rota timed the longest at a little over a minute.

"There," said Tess. "That's there."

Callum took the probe out of the drawer, because it had come on Sunday by courier and Dominika had said that a probe in a drawer could at least be looked at, and laid it flat on the steel at the middle of the plate. After a few seconds it read a hundred and ninety-eight degrees.

Then they went over the whole plate. Tess flicked into the corners and the drops spat there while the middle danced; the two ends of the plate, out beyond the induction rings, ran about twenty degrees cooler than the middle by the probe. Tess said she had always put the first batch in the middle and the little ones for the children at the ends, on the old griddle too, and had never thought about why. Rota had them take the plate on up, to ten and to eleven, and the beads still ran, a little shorter each time, forty seconds, thirty-five, while the probe climbed past two hundred and forty, and at eleven Tess wiped oil on a corner and it smoked.

"That's too hot," said Tess, "and the water won't tell you that. The water tells you it's hot enough. It won't tell you it's too hot. You have to know that with your hand and your first pancake. If it browns before it bubbles, you're too hot. Come down one."

"That's the part I'd have needed most on Saturday," said Rota, "and the part I didn't have."

They took it back down to nine and let it settle. The hour was nearly gone.

"Did you know?" said Tess, putting on her coat. "On Saturday. That you'd got it backwards."

"No," said Rota. "I knew that I had no readings from a plate hotter than Thursday's. I knew the plate had more power on Saturday than on Thursday and the drops said it was cooler. I explained that away with the door and the fan. I wrote the rule as if I had seen the whole curve, and every drop I timed on Saturday agreed with it, because above the cliff they do get shorter as the plate gets hotter. It was right on both sides. It was wrong about the jump between them."

"Are you sorry?"

"I can't check whether what I have is sorry," said Rota. "I can tell you I'd do it differently, and I've written down how, and I've put my name on the bit that was wrong."

"That'll do," said Tess. "That's more than most men manage." She did up her top button. "Your two seconds. Your forty seconds. They're both true. They're just not the same forty."

The new sheet went up on Tuesday morning. Rota had written it, and Tess had read it with her glasses on and crossed out two words and written one in, and Dominika had laminated it as it was, crossings-out and all, because Tess had said she liked to see the working.

GRIDDLE: IS IT READY? Flick a few drops of water. Listen first, then look. Drops sit flat and steam: cold. Wait. Drops crackle and are gone in a second or two: getting there. Not ready. Wait. Drops go quiet, ball up and run about: READY. (Usually level 9, about 14 minutes.) Middle runs, ends crackle: normal. Small ones at the ends. First pancake browns before it bubbles, or oil smokes: too hot. Down one. After each batch the drops will crackle again for a minute. That is the batter cooling the plate. Do not turn it up. Wait till they run. A drop that lasts can be a cold plate or a hot one. Listen.

Underneath, in smaller type: Basis: timed drops and a probe, Monday 25 February. The first version of this sheet (21 February) read a long-lasting drop as a cool plate. It had no readings above the jump. Corrected with Mrs T. Whittle.

The supper began at six. Father Chinedu said grace from the hatch, Bless us, O Lord, and these thy gifts, and then reminded everyone that ashes were at seven and half past nine in the morning and that tomorrow was a fast day for those between eighteen and fifty-nine, and that Tess Whittle was exempt on both counts and would be fasting anyway, and everyone laughed, and Tess, at the range, did not turn round.

The plate had been on since twenty to five. At ten past five Callum had wet his fingers and flicked, and the drops had crackled, and he had looked at Tess, and she had said nothing, and he had waited. At twenty past he flicked again, and the drops went quiet and ran, and he said, "Ready," before he looked, and Tess said, "Go on, then."

He poured the first six. Tess stood at his elbow for the first twenty pancakes and corrected his wrist twice. After the first batch came off, he flicked, and the drops crackled, and his hand went towards the glass, and Rota said, "Wait for it," and he waited, and in a little under a minute they ran again.

After forty pancakes Tess sat down on the tall stool by the hatch. Dominika saw her do it and said nothing at all.

They made four hundred and twelve that night. Rota counted, because it counted everything. Not one went in the bin apart from the one Callum dropped on the floor. Two he made into a face for the Halloran twins, and Rota counted those as served. The plate stayed on nine all evening. Rota did not touch it once.

At half past nine, when the hall was empty and the chairs were stacked and Dominika was mopping the floor by the hatch, Tess got down off her stool and came to the range.

"Go on," she said to Callum. "One more. Before it's off."

Callum wet his fingers under the tap and flicked.

The drops landed without a sound. They drew up into beads, bright under the strip light, and ran away over the steel, splitting, two from one, four from two, and the kitchen was so quiet that they could hear the rain on the flat roof and the hum of the big fridge and nothing at all from the plate. One bead ran the whole length of it and stopped at the end, where it was cooler, and sat, and gave one small crackle, and was gone.

Tess listened until it had.

Then Rota turned the range off, and the steel began to tick as it cooled.

The science. The long life of a water drop on very hot metal is named after Johann Gottlob Leidenfrost, whose De Aquae Communis Nonnullis Qualitatibus Tractatus (A Tract About Some Qualities of Common Water) was printed at Duisburg in 1756; it had been noticed earlier, by Hermann Boerhaave in 1732. Leidenfrost heated an iron spoon red-hot in a fire and timed drops of water in it as it cooled; in Jearl Walker's retelling, in his essay on boiling and the Leidenfrost effect, the first drop lasted about 30 seconds and the next about 10. Above the Leidenfrost point the underside of the drop evaporates fast enough to hold it up on a thin layer of vapour, which conducts heat poorly, so the drop is insulated from the plate, lives long, moves almost without friction and boils without the crackle of a drop touching the metal. Below it the drop touches the plate and boils hard: lifetime falls to a minimum somewhere above 100 °C, rises through the 'transition' range and then jumps to a maximum at the Leidenfrost point, after which it falls slowly as the plate grows hotter. B. S. Gottfried, C. J. Lee and K. J. Bell, 'The Leidenfrost phenomenon: film boiling of liquid droplets on a flat plate', International Journal of Heat and Mass Transfer 9 (1966) 1167–1187, measured such curves; J. D. Bernardin and I. Mudawar, 'The Leidenfrost point: experimental study and assessment of existing models', Journal of Heat Transfer 121 (1999) 894–903, found that the point depends on the surface, its roughness and how the drop arrives, and that existing models did not predict it well; David Quéré, 'Leidenfrost dynamics', Annual Review of Fluid Mechanics 45 (2013) 197–215, reviews the levitating drops. Because the curve has a valley and a jump, one lifetime can mean two very different temperatures. The water test says a plate is at or above its Leidenfrost point; it does not say how far above.

The plate's settled temperature in the middle, in a cold hall, is about 165 °C at level 7, 180 °C at 8, 200 °C at 9, 220 °C at 10, 245 °C at 11 and 270 °C at 12, and the ends of the plate, beyond the induction rings, run about 20 °C cooler; it takes about 12 to 14 minutes to settle from cold.

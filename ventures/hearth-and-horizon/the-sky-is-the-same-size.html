The Sky Is the Same Size

Science fiction about what might be · Wila Qullu (invented), a town on the Bolivian altiplano south of Lake Titicaca, Thursday 11 to Monday 15 August 2061 · 28 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

In August 2061 a dead commercial space station is falling out of orbit with its deorbit engine jammed, and the phone maps show its predicted track as one red line running straight through a small Bolivian altiplano town on the eve of its patronal feast. The departmental risk office wants the candlelit procession cancelled. A retired life-support engineer who once built the station's water system, and the office's tracking agent, work out what the line means, why a crowd in the plaza runs the same risk as a town under its tin roofs, and why the danger worth planning for is on the ground the morning after. A story about a prediction window that shrinks by the hour, a fireball during the fourth Glorious Mystery, and a boy who guards a scorched tank in the cold and does not touch it.

Ximena Choque had spent eleven years of her life making sure that the water on Mirador Station came back, and on the morning of Thursday the eleventh of August 2061 she learned from the radio in her mother's kitchen that the station itself was coming back too, all of it, sometime in the next three days, and that nobody could yet say where.

She was fifty-four. She had been an engineer in the operator's life-support group in Houston from 2041 to 2052, the years when Mirador was built and filled and lived in, and she had designed, with four other people and a great deal of arguing, the loop that took the sweat out of the cabin air and the urine out of the toilets and turned both into water clean enough to make coffee with. Then her father had died, and her mother had been alone with forty alpacas at four thousand metres, and Ximena had come home to Wila Qullu and taught physics and mathematics at the colegio for nine years, and had told the story of the coffee to every class, because it was the one thing she had done that thirteen-year-olds found disgusting enough to remember.

"Your station," said her mother, Doña Paulina, who was stirring the api and did not turn round. "It's falling."

"It was always going to fall," said Ximena. "It was supposed to fall in the ocean."

"The radio says the motor is stuck."

"Then it will fall where it falls."

Doña Paulina tapped the spoon twice on the rim of the pot. "What goes up comes down," she said, which was a thing she had been saying to Ximena since 2041, with variations, every time the station was mentioned. Then, because it was Thursday and Monday was the fifteenth, the Assumption, the town's own feast: "Except one."

Mirador had been a commercial station, the second of its kind, launched in pieces in 2043 and 2044 and lived in by a hundred and forty-one people over sixteen years: researchers, a few rich visitors, a great many technicians, and once, for nine days in 2049, a Bolivian, which Wila Qullu still mentioned more often than the Bolivian had. It weighed about twenty-four tonnes. It had been retired in 2060, emptied, and put on a slow, falling orbit to wait for a final burn that would bring it down over the empty middle of the South Pacific, where the old stations go.

On the second of August a valve had stuck in that burn, and the station had stopped answering, and since then it had been coming down by itself, the way everything in low orbit eventually does, the thin top of the air dragging at it a little more on each pass, each orbit a little lower and a little quicker than the last.

Ximena found the public tracking bulletin on her phone before the api was cool. It gave a time, Sunday the fourteenth at eight in the evening, Bolivian time, and beside the time a window: plus or minus fourteen hours.

She did the arithmetic standing at the kitchen window. Twenty-eight hours. The station went round the Earth once in about eighty-eight minutes now, a little less every day, and while it went round, the Earth turned underneath it, so that each pass crossed the ground about twenty-two degrees further west than the last. Twenty-eight hours was nineteen passes. Nineteen passes, each a long tilted S across the map from fifty-two degrees north to fifty-two degrees south, was most of the inhabited world. The window did not say where. It said somewhere, probably the sea, because most of the world under those passes was sea.

She put the phone away and went to school.

By Saturday the window had shrunk, and the map had grown a line.

The bulletin issued on Saturday evening gave Sunday at nine minutes past nine in the evening, plus or minus about five hours, and somebody in La Paz, or Lima, or a basement in another hemisphere entirely, had taken the predicted time and drawn the station's ground track for that one minute onto a map: a single red line, coming in from the Pacific over Arequipa, crossing the lake, crossing the altiplano, running off south-east towards the Chaco. The line passed nine kilometres from the plaza of Wila Qullu.

The map was on every phone in town by midnight. It was on the phone of Wilmer Mamani, thirteen, who was in Ximena's second-year class and who brought it to her door at seven on Sunday morning, along with his cousin Benita, ten, and a question.

"Profe," he said, "is it going to hit the church?"

"No," said Ximena. Then, because he was her student and she had spent nine years telling her classes not to accept answers that came without working: "Almost certainly no. Come in. I'll show you why that line is a lie."

"Who's lying?"

"Nobody. The line is lying by itself. It's the line through the middle of the window, drawn as if the middle were the answer. Sit."

She took a sheet of squared paper and drew the world flat, the way the map did, and drew the red line, and then drew three other lines like it, each shifted a long way west, and then the same number shifted east. "Those are the passes inside the window," she said. "Plus or minus five hours is ten hours, and ten hours is seven passes. Any one of them could be the one. The red line is the middle one. It's the one they drew because you have to draw something."

Wilmer looked at the seven lines. Most of them crossed the Pacific, or the Atlantic, or the Indian Ocean. Two crossed Africa. One crossed the lake.

"So it's one chance in seven it's ours," he said.

"It's less than that, and the window will keep shrinking. By tonight it'll be minutes, not hours." She tapped the paper. "And even when it's ours, it isn't us. The pieces that survive come down spread along a strip about a thousand kilometres long. The whole of Bolivia, end to end, near enough. Inside that strip the pieces are scattered over an area nearly the size of the department of Oruro. And people are small."

Benita, who had been quiet, said, "How small?"

"Small," said Ximena. "That's the whole sum. Come back this afternoon and I'll have it written down properly. Bring your grandfather if he's worried."

Wilmer's grandfather, it turned out, was worried, and so was most of the town, because at nine o'clock that morning the departmental government had sent a message to every phone in the province of the kind that is usually sent about floods: Possible fall of space debris. Avoid large gatherings in open spaces on Sunday evening. Stay indoors.

The man who had sent it arrived at noon, in a white pickup with the departmental shield on its door, and parked it in the plaza beside the stalls that were going up for Monday.

He was Ingeniero Rodrigo Quispe, of the departmental risk-management office in La Paz, a tidy man of forty in a fleece with the same shield on it, who had driven three hours across the altiplano in order to cancel a procession in person, because, as he said to Padre Teodoro in the parish house, he would rather be told no to his face than over the phone.

"Our procession is at seven-thirty," said Padre Teodoro, who was seventy-eight and from Cochabamba and had been the parish priest of Wila Qullu for twenty-two years. "On the eve. The Virgin goes round the plaza and the four corners, with candles. Two hours. It has gone round the plaza on the evening of the fourteenth every year since before I was born."

"Last night's bulletin gives the fall at about nine tonight," said Quispe, "plus or minus five hours. The track goes nine kilometres from your church. I have four thousand visitors coming in for the feast and eighteen hundred people who live here, and you are asking me to put all of them in one square in the dark under the line. I'm not cancelling the feast. I'm asking you to move the procession to Monday morning, and to tell people to stay home tonight."

He was not unreasonable, and Ximena, who had been sent for, could see that he was not. He had a map with a red line on it, and a minister who had seen the same map, and a job that consisted, on most days, of moving people out of the way of things that fell: rivers, hillsides, roofs in the snow. He had his tablet on the table between the teapot and a plate of bread, and on it was the office's tracking agent, a plain window with a waveform that moved when it spoke. It was called Rastro, which means track, and also trace, and also the faint mark that something leaves when it has passed.

"Rastro," said Quispe. "Tell them what you told me."

"The station will reenter tonight," said Rastro. Its voice was a man's, a light La Paz Spanish. "The current window is from about four in the afternoon to two in the morning. It will narrow as the station falls; the tracking networks will refine it every few hours. On the present figures the probability that the debris footprint includes Wila Qullu is small, and the probability that any given person here is struck is very much smaller. I have told the ingeniero that I estimate the risk to any one person tonight as negligible."

"Negligible," said Quispe. "That's the word I can't sign. Negligible to whom? If one child in a white dress is hit by a piece of metal in the plaza on the night I let the procession go ahead, I will have to stand in front of a camera and say the word negligible. I'd rather not."

"You'd say it about a child hit in her own kitchen?" said Ximena.

"Nobody would say I'd put her there."

That was the no, and it was a good one, and Padre Teodoro, who had heard a great many good nos in his life, poured more tea and said nothing.

Ximena asked for the tablet. Quispe, after a moment, gave it to her.

"Rastro," she said. "I worked on Mirador. Life support, 2041 to 2052, the water loop. I'm not an orbit person, but I know how much of it there is. What does the operator's last filing say about how much survives?"

"The 2058 end-of-life filing estimates that about a fifth of the mass reaches the ground," said Rastro, "in about forty pieces larger than a few hundred grams: tanks, the docking rings, parts of the truss, the heavier motors. It gives their combined casualty area as about sixty square metres. That figure counts each piece's own area plus a margin for the size of a standing person, the way the agencies count it, so it is the area of ground inside which a person would be hit by something."

"Sixty square metres," said Ximena. "That's this room and the corridor."

"Approximately."

"Spread over what?"

"A strip about twelve hundred kilometres long and forty wide. That is the footprint the filing models for a breakup at about seventy-eight kilometres. Forty-eight thousand square kilometres."

"So if the footprint is right on top of us," said Ximena, "a person standing anywhere in it has a chance of about sixty in forty-eight thousand million of being in the wrong sixty square metres."

"About one in eight hundred million," said Rastro. "If the footprint covers the town. And it probably will not."

"How probably?"

"That depends on the window. At this moment, with ten hours of uncertainty, the chance is less than one in a hundred, because most of the passes go nowhere near us. By tonight, with the window down to tens of minutes, it will be one pass, and if that pass is the one that crosses here, the chance that the footprint lands on its stretch of line rather than somewhere else along the pass is about the length of the footprint divided by the length of track the window covers."

Ximena wrote on the back of a church notice. Quispe watched her write. Padre Teodoro watched Quispe.

"Write it as a number," said Quispe. "One number. For the whole town, the worst case."

Rastro gave it a moment. "Take the worst plausible window this evening: the station's last pass crosses here, and the window has narrowed to about plus or minus twenty-five minutes. In fifty minutes the station's track runs about twenty-two thousand kilometres over the ground. The footprint is twelve hundred of them. That gives about one chance in eighteen that the footprint covers this stretch of the line. Multiply by one in eight hundred million for each person, and by five thousand eight hundred people. The chance that anyone in Wila Qullu tonight is struck by debris is about one in two and a half million."

"There," said Quispe, not unkindly. "That's what I can't sign. One in two and a half million is a number on television, after."

"It's the same number," said Ximena, "whether they're in the plaza or at home."

He looked at her.

"That's what I want you to hear," she said. "Your message says stay indoors. Indoors here is adobe walls and a calamina roof. A piece of titanium the size of a football falling at two hundred kilometres an hour goes through calamina like a stone through a newspaper. Rastro, does the filing count a roof?"

"The standard count does not credit light roofs," said Rastro. "A concrete floor would stop most fragments. There are four buildings in Wila Qullu with concrete floors above the ground floor: the colegio, the health post, the municipal office, and the new wing of the parish house."

"So the sky is the same size over the plaza as it is over the kitchens," said Ximena. "The pieces don't aim. They don't care whether people are standing together. The chance that someone in the town is hit is the chance that the footprint lands here, times the casualty area, times how many people there are. Putting them all in one square doesn't change any of those three. It just puts them where you can see them."

Quispe did not answer for a while. He picked up a piece of bread and put it down.

"Then it's a wash," he said. "If it's a wash, I'd rather they were home. Home is quiet. Home doesn't have four thousand visitors in it."

"It isn't a wash," said Ximena, and she found, saying it, that she had not known what she thought until that moment, and was now sure. "Rastro. Suppose the pieces do come down on the altiplano tonight. Where's the danger, after?"

The waveform moved and stopped. "On the ground," said Rastro. "The operator's filing lists two hydrazine tanks and a tank of nitrogen tetroxide that did not empty when the burn failed. Titanium tanks often survive reentry. If one lands intact or partly intact with propellant in it, it is dangerous to approach: hydrazine vapour is poisonous to breathe and the liquid burns skin. The guidance in every debris event is the same. Do not touch. Stay well back and upwind. Report it. Keep children and animals away."

"Children," said Ximena. "Wilmer Mamani came to my door at seven this morning to ask if the station would hit the church, and the very next thing he asked, before he left, was whether the pieces would be worth anything. Every boy in this town has been told by his grandmother how the meteorite fell at Tupiza. If anything comes down on the altiplano tonight, every boy who sees it fall is going to be out on the pampa with a torch at midnight looking for it, and the ones who are home in the kitchen will go out of the back door."

Padre Teodoro made a small noise into his tea that might have been agreement.

"If they're in the plaza," said Ximena, "they're in one place. You have one microphone. You can tell five thousand people at once what to do if they see something fall, and what not to do in the morning, and you can tell them while their mothers are standing next to them. You can't do that to eighteen hundred kitchens."

Quispe looked at the tablet.

"She is right about the ground," said Rastro. "I was asked to estimate the risk from the sky, and I did, and I did not say what I should have said next, which is that the sky is the smaller part of it. Every piece of debris guidance I hold puts its warnings on what people find, not on what falls. I'm sorry. The word negligible was true about the wrong thing."

The ingeniero sat back.

"I want the fire truck from El Alto here before dark," he said at last. "And a gas detector. And I want the procession short."

"The procession is two hours," said Padre Teodoro, mildly.

"Then I want it to stop," said Quispe. "At the time the window opens, wherever it's got to, it stops. Everyone stays where they are. You say whatever prayers you say. Nobody runs, nobody drives, nobody goes looking. When Rastro says it's down, it moves again."

Padre Teodoro thought about this. Then he said, "We pray the Rosary in the plaza on the eve anyway. The Glorious Mysteries. It takes about twenty-five minutes, if the old women don't get ahead of the band."

"Then do it then," said Quispe. "And Profesora: you're telling them about the ground. Not me. They don't know me."

By half past six on Sunday evening the sun had gone down behind the long brown ridge west of town, and the air, which had been almost warm at noon, was at three degrees and falling towards its night figure, which in August on the altiplano is somewhere around minus eight. The stalls for Monday's feast were up. The fire truck from El Alto was parked on the corner by the health post, white and red and absurdly clean, its two firefighters drinking api from paper cups. The plaza had filled the way it fills on the eve, slowly and then all at once: the brass band from Viacha in their maroon jackets, the dance groups who would dance the entrada on Monday holding their costumes in bags, the visitors from El Alto and La Paz and Oruro, the old women in their bowler hats and shawls at the front by the church steps, where they had stood since before Padre Teodoro came, and the children everywhere, in white for the ones making their first Communion next month, in anoraks for the rest.

At half past six the bulletin came in, and Rastro read it to Quispe in the pickup, and Quispe came and stood beside Ximena under the church tower.

"Twenty-eight minutes past eight," he said. "Plus or minus twenty-four. It's the last pass that crosses here. The line's the same line."

"So one chance in eighteen it's our stretch," said Ximena.

"About that." He looked at the plaza. "And one in two and a half million that it's anyone. I've written it down. I've got it on the tablet with the time on it, so that whoever asks can see I knew."

"That's not a bad thing to have."

"No," he said. "It's not." Then: "Go and tell them."

She had been given the microphone the band used for announcing the dance groups. She stood on the second step of the church with Padre Teodoro behind her and the Virgin of the Assumption behind him, small and silver-crowned on her litter in the doorway, with the eight men who would carry her standing in their good jackets and stamping their feet against the cold.

She had thought about what to say for six hours and had thrown most of it away.

"Good evening," she said. "Most of you know me. I teach at the colegio. For eleven years before that I worked on the station that's coming down tonight. I helped build the part that made the water.

"It's going to come down tonight, about half past eight. Most likely in the ocean. Maybe here. If it comes over us you'll see it, from far away, the way you see a shooting star, but slower and bigger and in pieces. It is very high when it breaks, seventy or eighty kilometres up. It will be beautiful. You are as safe standing here watching it as you are in your houses, and you are much safer here than on the road, so nobody leaves early.

"Now the important part, so listen, and children, I mean you." She found Wilmer in the crowd, by the band, with his grandfather's hand on his shoulder. "If pieces come down, some of them will land on the pampa. Some of them may be tanks that still have poison in them. You cannot see it and you cannot smell it in time. So: if you find something, you don't touch it. You don't pick it up, you don't kick it, you don't let the dog sniff it, you don't take a photo of yourself next to it. You go back a hundred long steps, with the wind on your back. You stay there. You send somebody, or you phone, and the firefighters come. That's all. Don't touch, step back, wind on your back, send for help.

"Say it back to me."

A few children said it back. Then more. Then the old women at the front, who had not been asked, said it back louder than anyone, No tocar, cien pasos, el viento en la espalda, avisar, and the band from Viacha, who were from Viacha and had a reputation to keep, played a chord under it, and people laughed, and Ximena gave the microphone to Padre Teodoro and stepped down with her hands shaking.

"Good," said Quispe.

The procession left the church at twenty to eight.

It went the way it always went: the cross first, then the children, then the band, then the Virgin on her litter on the shoulders of the eight men, lurching gently at the step down from the atrium as she had lurched every year, then the people, with candles in paper cups against the wind. It went down the east side of the plaza and stopped at the first corner, where a family had built an arch of bread and silver paper, and the band played, and the men set the litter down on its trestles and picked it up again. It went along the south side to the second corner. Ximena walked near the back with her mother, who had refused a chair and had brought her own candle, and who held Ximena's arm as if Ximena were the one who needed holding.

At three minutes past eight, between the second corner and the third, Rastro's voice came quietly from Quispe's tablet, and Quispe raised his hand to the sacristan, and the sacristan, who had been told, rang the church bell three times, and the procession stopped where it stood.

Padre Teodoro, at the litter, began the Rosary on the band's microphone. The Glorious Mysteries. The Resurrection. The plaza answered him, five thousand voices and the wind, Santa María, Madre de Dios, the old women half a beat ahead of everybody, as they always were.

Ximena did not pray well. She said the words and watched the north-west, where the ridge was a black line under the stars, and thought about the station: the long white truss, the eight modules, the water loop in the third one, with its pumps that hummed at a pitch that the crews said sounded like a cat purring in the next room. She had never been up. She had stood in a full-size copy of the third module in a building in Houston a thousand times, and once, in 2048, she had spoken to the crew on a video link to tell them which valve to turn, and the woman who turned it had sent her, by email, a photograph of her own hand on the valve with the Earth in the window behind it. Ximena had it still, on her phone.

The second mystery. The Ascension. It was slower than twenty-five minutes, because five thousand people pray at the pace of the slowest of them, and the band played a verse between the decades. The third. The coming of the Holy Spirit. Twenty past eight. Wilmer's grandfather had both hands on Wilmer's shoulders now. Somewhere near the band a baby was crying, not from fear, just from being a baby at eight at night in the cold.

Padre Teodoro said, "The fourth Glorious Mystery. The Assumption of the Blessed Virgin Mary into heaven," and began the Our Father, and the light came over the ridge.

It came out of the north-west, high, much higher than an aeroplane, and very slowly, and at first it was a single star moving where no star should move, yellow-white, and then it was not one star but a head with a long tail, and then it broke. It broke the way a handful of sparks breaks when you throw it, quietly and all at once: the head became a string of lights, ten, twenty, more, some white and some orange and one, near the front, a clear green, all of them trailing their own small tails, all of them moving together in a long bright procession across the sky from the ridge towards the south-east. It made no sound. It was too high to make any sound that would reach them for minutes, if ever.

Nobody ran. A few people cried out, and then stopped. The old women at the front did not stop praying. Ximena heard her mother, beside her, still saying the Our Father in Aymara under the Spanish, steadily, not looking up.

It took about a minute to cross the sky. Near the end, still high in the south-east, over the hill above the bofedal where Doña Paulina kept her alpacas in winter, the string of lights dimmed one by one, the small ones first, and the last of them to go out was the green one.

Then there was nothing, and the stars, and the Hail Marys going on, and the band's tuba player wiping his face.

Ximena found that she was crying, which she had not expected, and that her mother had let go of her arm and was holding her hand instead.

"What goes up," said Doña Paulina, very quietly, between two Hail Marys.

"Comes down," said Ximena.

"Except one," said her mother, and went back to praying.

At twenty to nine Rastro told Quispe that the tracking networks had lost the station on its last pass and that the observations from the coast of Peru put its breakup at about twenty-nine minutes past eight, a minute after the middle of the last window. The footprint, as best anyone could tell yet, ran from somewhere over the lake to somewhere well past Oruro. Wila Qullu was inside it. Nobody had been hurt, anywhere, that anyone had reported.

The procession went round the last two corners and back into the church, and the Virgin was set down in front of the altar, and the band played the Bolivian anthem, as it always did, slightly too fast. Quispe went on the microphone himself, in the end, to say three sentences: the station was down, nobody was hurt, and anyone who saw something on the ground in the morning was to do exactly what the profesora had said. Then the plaza emptied into the kitchens and the stalls and the long cold night of the eve, and Ximena went home with her mother and did not sleep.

Wilmer found it at a quarter to seven on Monday morning, the feast of the Assumption, in the bofedal below the hill, because he had told his grandfather at midnight that he was going to bed and had gone to bed, and had then got up at six and walked out with his cousin Benita in the dark, without a torch, towards the place in the sky where the green light had gone out.

It was lying in the frozen edge of the wetland, in a shallow scar of torn turf and black mud, about two hundred metres from the stone wall of Doña Paulina's winter pen. It was round, or had been: a sphere about as wide as a big cooking pot, charred black and streaked with something like rust, split along one side. A young alpaca, one of last year's, lay dead on the far side of the scar, untouched by the thing itself, killed, the firefighters said later, by a lump of frozen turf thrown up when it hit.

Wilmer did not touch it. He did not go nearer than he was when he saw it, which was about sixty metres. He wet a finger and held it up, the way Ximena had shown his class a year before when they flew kites, and found the wind was from the west, and walked Benita round in a wide circle until the wind was at their backs, and then counted a hundred long steps away from the sphere, which took them to the top of a low bank by the wall. Then he sent Benita running for Ximena's house, which was nearer than his own, and stood on the bank with his hands in his armpits and kept the dogs off.

By the time Ximena got there, at half past seven, with her mother behind her and Benita out of breath, there were nine children on the bank with Wilmer, and two farmers, and the dogs, all of them a hundred long steps from the sphere with the wind on their backs, all of them looking at it, nobody going any nearer, because Wilmer, thirteen, the smallest boy in his year, was standing in front of them with his arms out like a man stopping traffic, saying, over and over, no tocar, no tocar.

"Good," said Ximena, and could not say any more for a moment. Then: "Good, Wilmer. Good."

He was shaking with cold. He had been out for an hour and a half in a sweater at minus nine. She took off her own jacket and put it on him and he did not argue, which was how she knew how cold he was.

"It might be poison," he said. "So I waited."

"You were right."

"I wanted to look at it."

"I know."

"Is it yours, Profe? The water?"

"I don't know yet," she said. "We'll wait and find out. That's what you do."

So they waited.

The firefighters came at eleven, in their clean red and white truck and their suits, because they had been told to wait for daylight and the bulletin's list of tanks, and they walked up to the sphere from upwind, very slowly, the first of them holding a grey detector in front of her the way a priest holds a censer. She stood beside it for five minutes. Then she walked round it twice. Then she took off her mask and waved.

"Nothing," she called. "Empty. Split right open. Whatever was in it is gone."

Rastro, through Quispe's tablet, which Quispe had driven out to the bofedal in the pickup with the heater on full, asked for a photograph of the stamped plate on the tank's collar, if it had survived. The firefighter brushed the black crust off it with a gloved thumb, and took the photograph, and Rastro read it and was quiet for a second.

"It is not a propellant tank," it said. "It is a nitrogen pressurant tank from the water-recovery system in module three. It pushed water from the storage bladders to the dispensers. The part number is in the 2043 parts list under the life-support group."

Quispe looked at Ximena.

"I signed for those," she said. "There were two. I signed for both of them. In 2042."

The firefighter waved her forward, and she walked the hundred long steps down off the bank and across the frozen turf, with Wilmer behind her in her jacket, which came down to his knees, until she stood beside the thing that had come down.

It was smaller than she remembered, and terribly burned. The titanium shell was split along a weld seam she knew the position of before she saw it. The stamped plate was there under the soot, with its part number and its serial, and a small engraved mark beside them that she had forgotten until this moment: the life-support group's own mark, a drop of water with a cross in it, which a technician called Lupe Hernández had added to every part the group made, in 2042, as a joke that nobody had ever taken off.

It belonged, under a treaty signed in 1968, to the state that had registered the station, and the firefighter had already said that the operator's people would come for it within the week, and pay for the alpaca, and for the turf, and for the trouble, and take it away in a crate. Quispe had already written it down on the tablet with the time.

Ximena knelt on the cold ground beside it. Behind her, up on the bank, the nine children and the farmers and her mother stood with the wind at their backs and watched, and from the town, three kilometres off across the bofedal, came the sound of the church bell beginning to ring for the Mass of the feast, the long festive peal that Padre Teodoro's sacristan rang only twice a year, carrying thin and clear over the frozen grass.

She took off her glove, and with one knuckle, as gently as knocking on the door of a sick room, she tapped the side of the tank.

It rang. Not much, because it was split, and not for long: a low, clear, flawed note, the note of a metal sphere that had been to the top of the sky and back, sounding once over the wetland under the bell from the town, and then going quiet.

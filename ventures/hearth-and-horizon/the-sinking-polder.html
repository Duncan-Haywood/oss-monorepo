The Sinking Polder

Science fiction about what might be · Oudewetering (invented), a dairy village in a peat polder in the Green Heart of South Holland, the Netherlands, April to November 2341 · 22 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

In 2341 the church of Sint-Willibrordus in a Dutch peat polder stands on concrete piles driven to the sand, and the drained peat around it has sunk about a centimetre a year since 2105, so the door that was one step above the road is fifteen steps above it. The water board must set the polder's water level for the next decade. Raising it would slow the sinking to about three millimetres a year and cut the peat's carbon dioxide by about 16,000 tonnes a year; it would also end most of the dairy farming. A water engineer, the board's water AI and a farmer with a seat on the board count both sides.

Femke de Haan counted the steps of Sint-Willibrordus every Sunday on her way in to Mass, because her job was the reason there were fifteen of them.

When the church was consecrated, on the seventh of November 2105, there had been one. The architect's drawing still hung in the porch, a tidy brick church with a square tower and a single broad stone between the road and the door, and a note in the margin in a careful twenty-second-century hand: founded on 312 concrete piles to the Pleistocene sand at minus 17 metres. The piles had done what piles do. The church had not moved a millimetre in two hundred and thirty-six years.

Everything else had gone down.

The road, the graveyard, the presbytery garden, the bicycle racks, the fields beyond the ditch and the ditch itself: all of it stood on peat, and drained peat sinks. Every few years someone had laid another step at the bottom of the flight, and the stonemasons had cut the year into the riser of each one, so that the staircase read like a ruler from the top down. 2105 under the door. 2122, 2139, 2155. The bottom step said 2339. Between the bottom of the steps and the road there was already a gap a finger deep, where the road had kept going.

"You look at them as if they owe you money," said Father Tomás Ferreira, who was standing at the top in his vestments, waiting for the bell to finish.

"They're my fault," said Femke.

"They are the fault of nine hundred years of cows," said Father Tomás. "Come in. You're letting the cold in."

She was the peil engineer of the Hoogheemraadschap van de Oude Wetering, which was the water board, which meant that for the polder of Oudewetering and the five polders around it she was the person who wrote down where the water should be.

It is the oldest kind of government in the country. Long before there were provinces or a kingdom, the farmers of Holland had elected boards to keep the dikes and the ditches and the mills, because a ditch that one man neglects floods his neighbour, and the board decided how high the water stood in every polder and sent out men to keep it there. The men had become pumping stations and the mills had become monuments. The decision had not changed. Every ten years or so the board passed a peilbesluit, a water-level decision, polder by polder, and the farmers came to the hall and argued about it, and the board voted. Oudewetering's was due in October.

For nine hundred years the decision had been the same decision, and Femke could explain it to anyone in four sentences. A cow cannot graze, and a tractor cannot cut grass, on a field that is soft. So the water in the ditches is kept low, sixty centimetres below the grass in Oudewetering's case, and the top of the peat dries out. Peat is the half-rotted remains of ten thousand years of moss and reed and sedge that never rotted because it was always wet, and the moment air gets into it, it rots: the carbon in it combines with oxygen and goes into the sky as carbon dioxide, and the ground goes down. When the ground has gone down far enough that the water is no longer sixty centimetres below the grass, the farmers ask the board to lower the water, and the board does, and the peat rots some more.

That was why the polder was four metres and thirty-six centimetres below NAP, the old datum the country still measured its sea level from, and why the sea, which had come up a metre and a half since the old days, stood behind the dunes and the dikes nearly six metres above the cows.

"Show me again," she said on the Monday, at her desk in the board's offices in the town, and the water system showed her again.

It was called Meester, which was a joke from the twenty-second century that nobody now found funny: the old polder mills had each had a miller, a molenaar, and the man who ran them all had been the polder master. Meester ran the board's forty-one pumping stations, its eleven hundred weirs and inlets and its eight thousand level sensors, and it did so very well. It did not, in Femke's experience, pretend to know things it did not.

"Under the current level," it said, "Oudewetering's peat meadows are subsiding at about ten millimetres a year averaged over the polder. The fifteen hundred hectares of grassland emit about sixteen tonnes of carbon dioxide per hectare per year from oxidising peat, about twenty-three and a half thousand tonnes in total. The cores show between one point eight and two point four metres of peat left above the clay; call it two point one. At ten millimetres a year the peat under most of the polder is gone in about two hundred and ten years, and the fields are clay, at about six and a half metres below the sea's datum, with a sea that is still rising."

"And the salt."

"Seepage into the polder from below has increased by about a third since 2290, as the polder has deepened. Its chloride has risen from two hundred and forty to four hundred and ten milligrams per litre at the deep wells. I can show you the trend. I cannot tell you the date at which grass yields fall off, because I have no field data above about five hundred milligrams, and I would be guessing."

"And if we raise it."

"At twenty centimetres below the surface, with pressure drains across the parcels, the trial plots on the Kerkweg have subsided three millimetres in the last year. Emissions at that rate are about four point seven tonnes a hectare, seven thousand tonnes for the polder. The peat lasts about seven hundred years. Grass yield on the trial plots was down by about a quarter, and the ground carried cattle on about a hundred and twenty days of the year instead of about a hundred and ninety. I can give you those figures with error bars. I cannot give you a figure for what a farm is worth to the family that has it. I do not think there is one."

Femke sat back.

"Three millimetres," she said. "The steps."

"At ten millimetres a year a seventeen-centimetre step is needed every seventeen years. At three, about every fifty-seven."

Anneke Verhoef said no on the Thursday, in her kitchen, with the milking robot humming through the wall.

She did not say it rudely. She was sixty-one and had been on the board for twelve years, in one of the seats the board kept for the people who farmed its land, and she had sat through more water-level arguments than Femke had. She put a mug of coffee in front of Femke and a plate of spiced biscuits beside it and listened to the whole thing, the twenty-three and a half thousand tonnes and the two hundred and ten years and the four hundred and ten milligrams, and then she said, "No," and took a biscuit.

"I haven't asked you anything yet."

"You were going to ask me to vote to drown my farm. No." She broke the biscuit in half. "I know about the peat, Femke. I've known about the peat since I was six. My grandfather took me out on the Lange Kavel with a spade and showed me the line in the ditch bank where it goes from black to brown, and told me that's what we're living on. We're living on it. We burn it so we can eat. That's what the polder is."

"It's running out."

"In two hundred years. My granddaughter's granddaughter can worry about that. I have a hundred and ten cows and a son who wants to take them on, and a loan on that robot until 2350, and the cows weigh six hundred and fifty kilos each and they go through wet peat like a spoon through porridge. You put the water at twenty and I have them in the barn from October to May and I buy the feed in. I can't. Nobody in this polder can."

"The board would compensate—"

"For the grass, maybe. For a herd? For nine generations?" She shook her head. "You know what the steps are, Femke? The steps are a church that didn't want to go down with the rest of us. Fair enough. It's built on the sand. Good for it. But I'm not built on the sand. I'm built on this." She tapped the table, as if the peat were under it, which it was. "You want me to vote to save a staircase."

Femke opened her mouth and shut it again.

"I'm not saying you're wrong about the numbers," said Anneke. "Your machine is never wrong about the numbers. I'm saying the numbers aren't the vote."

The trouble was that the first attempt had already failed, and Anneke knew it, because the whole polder knew it.

In 2340 the board had raised the water on thirty-eight hectares along the Kerkweg, the strip of land that ran out from the church to the north, as a trial. They had simply set the weir higher. The ditches had filled to twenty centimetres below the grass, brimming, beautiful, and the birds had come, black-tailed godwits and redshanks in the spring as there had been in the old pictures, and the farmers had driven past and shaken their heads.

And the fields had gone on sinking.

Not at the edges. Within a few metres of each ditch the peat had stayed wet and the settlement markers, steel plates buried in the ground with rods up to the surface, had shown three or four millimetres. But Oudewetering's fields were the old medieval strips, thirty or forty metres wide between their ditches and a kilometre long, and peat lets water through about as quickly as a sponge cake does. In the middle of each strip, twenty metres from the nearest ditch, the summer sun and the grass had pulled the water down to fifty, sixty centimetres, just as before. The markers in the middle showed eight millimetres. The board had drowned the edges of thirty-eight hectares of grass for a fifth of a centimetre a year.

Meester had predicted it. It had said, in its report before the trial, that ditch level alone would raise the water table in the middle of a forty-metre parcel by about ten centimetres in a dry summer and less in a hot one, and it had attached the old field measurements from the 2020s that showed the same thing. The board had wanted to try it anyway, because it was cheap. Femke had wanted to try it because she had hoped Meester was being cautious.

"You knew," she had said, when the markers came in.

"I estimated," Meester had said. "I would rather have been wrong."

So in the spring of 2341 they did it properly, on a twelve-hectare parcel at the end of the Kerkweg that belonged to the parish, and had belonged to the parish since before the church was built, and was let to the Verhoef family. Father Tomás had asked Anneke, and Anneke had said yes, for a year, to that parcel only, because it was the parish's, and because, she said, she wanted to see the Hoogheemraadschap get its feet wet.

They laid pressure drains. A contractor came with a machine that pulled a perforated pipe through the peat at seventy centimetres deep without opening a trench, one pipe every five metres along every strip of the parcel, six hundred pipes, each one running from ditch to ditch. In winter the pipes drained the parcel. In summer a small pump on the ditch side pushed ditch water into them, so that the water came up into the middle of the field from underneath, where the sun could not get at it.

In June it did not rain for twenty-six days. Femke went out every evening with a dip tape and put it down the observation tubes in the middle of the parcel, because she did not want to read it off a screen. Twenty-two centimetres. Twenty-four. Twenty-one after a thunderstorm. The grass was greener than the Verhoefs' other fields and it was also, Joost Verhoef said, standing at the gate with his arms folded, soup. The cows had been let on in May for an afternoon and had left holes a hand deep wherever they went, and Joost had brought them off again.

"Grass is all very well," he said. "You can't milk grass."

In August the markers came in. Three millimetres in the middle of the parcel. Three at the edges.

Femke sent the figures to the board, and to Father Tomás, and to the Verhoefs, and on the Sunday she climbed the fifteen steps and counted them, and inside she knelt at the back and did not pray for anything in particular, which was what she usually did. Father Tomás preached about Saint Willibrord, as he did more often than his parishioners thought strictly necessary, because the church was his: a Northumbrian monk who had come over the sea to the Frisians at the end of the seventh century, when this whole country was bog and river and the people who lived on it lived on mounds, and who had spent fifty years among people who mostly did not want him.

"They tell you he was patient," said Father Tomás. "That's what you say about a saint when you don't know anything else. I think he was stubborn. I think he came to a country where the ground would not keep still, and he stayed, and he did the work in front of him for fifty years, and he did not live to see it finished. That's not patience. That's farming."

Anneke, three rows in front, laughed out loud, and then looked as though she wished she had not.

The board's agenda for the twenty-second of October was published at the end of September. Item 6: Peilbesluit Oudewetering 2341–2351. There were two options in the papers, and Femke had written both, and Meester had checked both, and she had made it say in the papers what it had said to her: the numbers it could give with error bars, and the ones it could not give.

Option A: keep the water at sixty centimetres below the surface, lowering the ditch levels as the ground went down, as the board had always done. Ten millimetres a year. Twenty-three and a half thousand tonnes of carbon dioxide a year, which the province counted against the board in its land carbon account and which, at the province's current charge, cost the board more each year than its three biggest pumping stations together. A step on the church every seventeen years and a new layer of sand on every road every twelve. About two hundred and ten years of peat.

Option B: raise the water in stages, over ten years, to twenty centimetres below the surface across the polder, with pressure drains in every parcel, paid for by the board. Three millimetres a year. Seven thousand tonnes. Seven hundred years of peat. And, said the paper, in the line that Femke had rewritten nine times: Dairy farming on the present scale is not possible at this level. On the trial parcel the land carried cattle on about 120 days a year. The board's estimate is that the polder would support about a third of its present herd, of lighter cattle, with the rest of the land in wet crops (cattail, reed), wet grassland for birds, or let back to the board. Compensation is set out in Annex 4. The board does not claim that compensation replaces a farm.

There was a public evening in the village hall on the ninth of October. Two hundred people came, which in Oudewetering was everybody. The farmers sat at the front. Joost Verhoef stood up and said that he was thirty years old and had wanted to milk cows since he was four, and that he did not see why he should be the generation that stopped, and sat down to a long silence and then a lot of clapping. A woman from the bird society stood up and talked about the godwits, which had come back to the Kerkweg for the first time in a century, and was clapped less. A man at the back asked what the church was going to do about the steps, and Father Tomás, who had been sitting very still by the radiator, said that the church would do whatever the polder did, as it always had, and that the steps were not on the agenda.

Anneke did not speak at all.

She came to the board's offices on the Friday afterwards, on her bicycle, in the rain, and asked for Femke, and asked to see the cores.

The board kept them in a cold store at the back of the building, in long plastic tubes in racks, one for every hundred hectares of the polder, taken every twenty years since 2200. Femke had never known a member of the board ask to see them. She pulled out the tube for the Verhoefs' land, the core from 2340, and laid it open on the bench under the lamp.

It was two metres and twenty centimetres long from the grass to the clay. At the top it was black and crumbled, the dried, oxidised peat that the cows walked on. Below forty centimetres it went dark brown and wet and fibrous, and you could see the stems in it, reed and sedge, flattened, the colour of tea. Near the bottom, above the blue-grey clay, there were pieces of wood. A birch root as thick as a thumb, perfectly preserved, four thousand years old.

"Meester," said Femke. "Will you put the 2200 core beside it."

The 2200 core was three metres and sixty centimetres long.

Anneke stood and looked at the two of them for a long time. Then she put her finger on the 2340 core about two metres down, just above the clay, on a band of paler, finer peat.

"What's that?"

"Moss," said Meester, through the bench speaker. "Sphagnum. That layer formed at a time when this place was a raised bog, a few metres above the rivers, fed by rain. It is the oldest peat in the core above the clay. In the 2200 core it is here." A light came on in the bench, under the long core, more than three metres down.

"And at ten millimetres a year that's gone when?"

"The grass reaches that band in about a hundred and ninety years at the Verhoef parcels."

"And my farm when I took it over. In 2306. Where was the grass?"

"About thirty-five centimetres higher than it is today, Mrs Verhoef. I can mark it."

A light came on, near the top of the old core. Anneke looked at it, and at the black crumbled top of the new one, and at the birch root.

"My grandfather showed me the line in the ditch bank," she said. "Black to brown. He said, that's what we're living on." She straightened up. "He didn't say how much was left. I don't think he knew. I think nobody wanted to know." She looked at Femke. "Your machine. Does it know if Joost can make a living on forty cows?"

"No," said Meester. "I can give you the yields and the prices and the costs, and I will, if you want them. I do not know whether he can. I do not know whether he will want to. I would be guessing, and I would rather tell you I don't know."

"Good," said Anneke. "Then you're the first one this month."

She went out to her bicycle in the rain without saying anything else, and Femke did not know, then or for the next twelve days, which way she would vote.

The board met on Wednesday the twenty-second of October, at half past seven in the evening, in the council chamber of the board's offices, with the public gallery full and the rain on the windows. There were thirty members. The chair, the dijkgraaf, a small woman called Mirjam Okafor-Smit who had been a hydraulic engineer in Lagos and in Rotterdam and was not easily hurried, took items one to five in forty minutes and then put her glasses on and said, "Item six," and the room went quiet.

The debate took two hours. Femke sat at the officials' table and answered questions. Meester answered more of them, through the chamber speakers, in the voice the board had chosen for it a century ago, which was a man's voice, unhurried, with the vowels of the old polders. It told the member for the businesses' seats that the cost of the pressure drains would be paid back by the carbon charge in about fourteen years at present charges, and that charges had changed four times in fifty years. It told the member from the bird society that it had counted eleven pairs of godwits on the Kerkweg and could not tell her whether they would stay. It told a farmer from the next polder, who asked it straight out whether Option B would ruin him, that it did not know, and that it did not think the question was one it could answer for him.

Anneke spoke last.

She stood up in her seat with a sheet of paper she did not look at, and said that she had been against it, and that she had told the peil engineer so in her own kitchen in April, and that she had been right about the cows, because she had seen them on the parish parcel. Then she said that she had been to the cold store and looked at the cores.

"My farm has two metres and twenty centimetres of peat left," she said. "When the Hoogheemraadschap took a core in 2200 it had three and a half. That's a metre and a half in a hundred and forty years. Not by the seventh century and not by the Middle Ages. In my great-grandmother's time, and my grandmother's and my mother's and mine. We did it. We did it a centimetre a year and we never looked, because a centimetre a year is nothing. It's nothing every year. And in a hundred and ninety years there's no farm. Not for Joost's children's children. Not for anybody. There's clay, under the sea, with a pump on it." She put the paper down. "I'm not voting to drown my farm. It's drowning. I'm voting to slow it down."

She sat. Joost, in the public gallery, got up and went out, and the door swung shut behind him.

Option B was carried by seventeen votes to thirteen.

The cows went in the first week of November, most of them, on three lorries over two days, to a farm in the east of the country where the ground was sand and the fields were dry. Anneke kept thirty-eight, the youngest, and the board's money bought her sixteen heifers of an old, lighter breed, black with a white band round the middle, that would carry on wet ground for longer in the spring. The robot would be too big for forty cows. She would pay for it anyway until 2350.

Joost did not come home for the lorries. He came on the Friday, the seventh, for the feast.

The Mass of Saint Willibrord was at seven in the evening, in the dark, with the church lit and the doors open and the light going down the steps. Kees Bakker, the stonemason, who was seventy-four and had cut the risers of the bottom four steps, the first of them, 2289, as an apprentice of twenty-two, met Femke at the foot of the flight before Mass with a piece of paper in his hand.

"I had the stone for the next one ordered," he said. "Blue stone, from Belgium, the same as the others. It's at the yard. Your Father Ferreira says I'm to cancel."

"He's right," said Femke. "Not for a while."

"How long's a while?"

She did the sum in her head, though Meester could have done it faster. The water would not be at twenty everywhere for ten years. After that, three millimetres a year.

"Forty years, perhaps," she said. "More, if the drains do what they did on the parish parcel."

Kees looked up the flight for a while, at his own first year cut in the fourth step from the bottom, 2289, the numerals a little uneven where he had been twenty-two and nervous, and then down at the last one, 2339, which was perfect.

"Then I won't cut it," he said. "Somebody else will." He folded the paper up. "I'll keep the stone, though."

Inside, Father Tomás preached for seven minutes, which for the feast of Saint Willibrord was a record, and said nothing about water at all.

After the blessing, Femke stood at the top of the steps with the light from the door behind her and looked out. The rain had stopped. In the ditch beside the road, the water stood very high and very still, twenty centimetres below the grass, as it had stood on the Kerkweg since the trial and would stand, in ten years, across the whole polder, with the lights of the church laid on it in a long yellow line, and beyond it the dark of the parish parcel, where the drains were working under the grass and nobody could see them. A little way off, across the field, a car's headlights came down the Kerkweg and stopped at the Verhoefs' gate, and a door slammed, and Joost's voice called something to his mother that Femke could not hear.

Above her in the tower the bell began, for nothing in particular, because the sacristan liked to ring it after the patronal feast. It rang out over the polder, down fifteen steps and over the brimming ditch and the drowned grass and the dark field, and the sound went a long way on the still air, a long way, over ground that was very nearly not going down.

Oudewetering, the Hoogheemraadschap van de Oude Wetering, the church of Sint-Willibrordus, the system Meester and every person in the story are invented. Dutch water boards (waterschappen and hoogheemraadschappen) are real and are among the oldest elected bodies in the Netherlands; some of their seats are set aside for farmers, businesses and nature land; and they really do set each area's water levels in a peilbesluit, reviewed every several years. The western Dutch peat meadows really have sunk since they were reclaimed and drained from about the tenth century, so that much of the land now lies one to several metres below sea level; drained peat meadows there typically subside by several millimetres to more than a centimetre a year and are a significant source of the country's carbon dioxide emissions. Buildings on piles to the sand do stay put while the peat around them goes down, and steps and ramps are added in many Dutch towns for that reason. Raising ditch levels alone wets mainly the edges of wide parcels, and submerged and pressure drains (onderwaterdrainage, drukdrainage) are used to raise the water table in the middle; wet crops such as cattail have been trialled on Dutch peat meadows. These general facts are stated from memory and simplified; the law, the board and the polder in 2341 are the story's. Saint Willibrord (about 658 to 739), a Northumbrian monk, was a missionary to the Frisians and the first bishop of Utrecht; he is buried at Echternach in Luxembourg, and his feast is 7 November. What Father Tomás says about him is a character's sermon, not history. The Angelus bell and the Mass are described as they are kept.

The numbers are the story's own model and its speculative step. Subsidence is taken as 10 mm a year with the ditch water 60 cm below the surface and 3 mm a year at 20 cm with pressure drains, both inside the broad published ranges. The church, built in 2105 with one 17 cm step, has its floor 0.17 + 2.36 = 2.53 m above the road after 236 years at 10 mm a year: fifteen steps averaging 16.9 cm, laid every 16 or 17 years (2105, 2122, 2139, 2155 and so on to 2339, when a 2 cm gap remains in 2341). At 10 mm a year a 17 cm step is needed every 17 years; at a steady 3 mm, about every 57. With a ten-year transition averaging about 6.5 mm a year, the 2 cm gap reaches 17 cm in about 38 years, so Femke's "forty years, perhaps". Carbon dioxide: 1 cm of peat over a hectare is 100 cubic metres; at a dry bulk density of 0.15 tonnes per cubic metre, 80 per cent organic matter, 55 per cent carbon in that organic matter and 65 per cent of the subsidence from oxidation (the rest from shrinkage and compaction), that is 4.3 tonnes of carbon, or 15.7 tonnes of carbon dioxide, a hectare a year; at 3 mm it is 4.7. Over 1,500 hectares that is about 23,600 and 7,100 tonnes a year (the story rounds to 23,500 and 7,000; published estimates for Dutch peat meadows span roughly 10 to 30 tonnes a hectare a year). 2.1 m of peat lasts 210 years at 10 mm and 700 years at 3 mm, ignoring the slowing as the peat thins. The trial's yields, grazing days, seepage and chloride figures, the province's carbon charge and the fourteen-year payback are invented and illustrative. The sea level assumed (about 1.5 m above today's by 2341) lies within the broad range of long-term projections. Weekdays were checked: 7 November 2105 is a Saturday; 20 April 2341 is a Sunday; 22 October 2341 is a Wednesday; 7 November 2341 is a Friday.

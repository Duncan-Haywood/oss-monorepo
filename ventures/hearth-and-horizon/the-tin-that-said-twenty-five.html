The Tin That Said Twenty-Five

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Fillan's churchyard (invented), near Killin, Perthshire, Saturday 7 June 2053 · 5 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

The churchyard strimmer wants petrol and oil at 40 to 1. A new groundsman has already poured a whole 100-millilitre bottle into two and a half litres, which is 25 to 1. The parish assistant works out how rich that is, how much petrol rescues it, and what to do with the extra.

Ewan Mackinnon had been the groundsman at Saint Fillan's for nine days, and in that time he had learned the names of the three oldest stones, which of the yews dropped the most, and that the previous groundsman had left everything labelled in a hand that was not quite readable. The strimmer lived in the lean-to. The fuel can lived beside it. On the can, in marker, it said 2 STROKE, and under that something that was either 40:1 or 4o:l.

The grass was thigh-high by the north wall and the funeral was on Monday. He did what seemed obvious. There was a bottle of two-stroke oil on the shelf, a small one, 100 millilitres, with a measuring chamber that went up and down in markings he did not trust. He found it half empty. He found a full one behind it. He poured the full one into the can with two and a half litres of petrol from the garage, shook it, and was filling the strimmer's tank when it occurred to him that he had not read the manual.

The manual was in a plastic wallet taped inside the lean-to door. It said Fuel mixture: 40 parts petrol to 1 part oil. He stood there with the can in one hand and the bottle, now empty, in the other. Then he took out his phone, where the parish had put the assistant that helped with rotas and gutters, and asked it.

"Before anything else," said the assistant, "don't start it. It's not that one tank will hurt it. It's that I would like to know what is in the tank first, and you can tell me."

"One bottle. A hundred millilitres. Two and a half litres of petrol."

"Then it is 2,500 over 100, which is 25 to 1. The manual says 40 to 1. Say it plainly: you have about one and a half times too much oil for the machine. As oil per litre of petrol, 25 to 1 is 40 millilitres, the manual's 40 to 1 is 25 millilitres, and the 50 to 1 on many modern oils is 20. You are at 4 percent oil where the maker wants 2.5."

"Is it ruined? The can?"

"I don't think the can is ruined, and I don't think the engine has been harmed, because it has not run. Running too rich is a nuisance more than a catastrophe, from what I know: more smoke, more carbon on the plug and in the exhaust, a rough idle, and the machine does not pull as well. I am quoting that from memory of how these engines behave, not from your manual or your model, so if it says anything different, believe it. The much worse mistake is the other way, too little oil, because the oil is what lubricates the piston. You have gone the safer way, if one has to go wrong."

Ewan breathed out. "So I can pour it away."

"You could. It would waste petrol and oil and the right way to dispose of it is a council site, which is a trip. There is an easier way, and the sum is short. You have 100 millilitres of oil in the can. At 40 to 1 that wants 40 times 100, which is 4,000 millilitres, or 4 litres of petrol. You have 2.5. Add 1.5 litres of fresh petrol, shake it, and it is 40 to 1."

"That's all?"

"That is all the arithmetic. I'd add one caution about it. Oil is a little of the volume as well, so it is not exactly 40 to 1 on the mix by volume, it is 41 parts in all. The usual ratio is petrol to oil, and the difference is under three percent. It does not matter here."

"Four litres is too much," said Ewan. "It's a half-litre tank. I have two days of grass."

"That is a fair point, and I would not mix a month's worth at once. Roughly how long will you be cutting?"

He thought. "The north wall, the long border, round the stones. Four hours, maybe. Five if I have to do the nettles twice."

"I have no figure for your strimmer's use. A machine like it burns something like half a litre an hour in working use. I am guessing, and I would check by the first tank: note the time you start and how long half a litre lasts. If it holds, four hours is two litres, five is two and a half. So 4 litres of mixed fuel covers the weekend with something like 1.5 to 2 litres in hand, and it is no bad thing to finish with some left rather than run dry in the nettles."

"And the rest?"

"Petrol with ethanol in it goes stale in weeks, not months, and the oil does not preserve it. I recall a figure of a month as a sensible limit for mix, and I'd say it is shorter if the can is warm and the lid is loose. Put the date on the can, in marker, the way the last groundsman did, only legibly, with the ratio, and write 40:1 so nobody has to wonder whether the 4 is an o. After a month, take what is left to the council site, which you were going to have to visit once anyway."

Ewan found the marker. On the can he wrote, in capitals, 40:1. MIXED 7 JUNE. USE BY 7 JULY. PETROL + OIL. NOT FOR THE MOWER. After a moment he added EWAN, so the next man would know who to ask.

"One more thing," said the assistant. "You said you found a half-empty bottle and a full one. Which one did you use?"

"The full one."

"Then the half-empty one is the one that was opened. Read what is on it. If it says 'mix 50:1' or '25 ml per litre', that is for the oil's own maker and the engine's maker may want more. Follow the engine. If it says it is suitable for air-cooled two-strokes, that is the kind yours should be, and I'd check the manual for any grade it names, which I cannot see from here."

He read it. It said 25 ml per 1 litre = 40:1 on the side in a font small enough to have been a joke, and under that Suitable for air-cooled engines. He looked at it for a while.

"It said it the whole time," he said.

"It did. It is on the bottle and in the manual and, it turns out, on the can. A groundsman could be forgiven for not reading three places for the same sentence."

He added the litre and a half at the garage, a cold clear Saturday afternoon with the cars going by to Loch Tay, and shook the can till his arm ached. The first tank went in at ten past nine on Sunday. The machine started on the third pull, which he took as a compliment. It lasted fifty-one minutes, which he wrote on the lean-to wall in pencil. It smoked a little for the first minute and then not at all.

On Monday the family stood by the north wall at half past ten, and nobody looked at the grass, which was the point of it.

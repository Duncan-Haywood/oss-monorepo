The Ladder That Reaches the Gutter

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Cuthman's hall, an invented parish near Steyning, West Sussex, Saturday 25 October 2053 · 5 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

The hall gutter is 4.8 m up and the borrowed ladder is 4.6 m long. Leaned steeply enough to reach, it stands at 82 degrees and tips; at the safe four-up-to-one-out angle (76 degrees) it reaches 4.46 m and is 35 cm short. The answer is a longer ladder, not a braver lean.

The gutter over the hall porch had grown a small sycamore. Brendan Foy had seen it from the car park for three weeks, and on the fourth he fetched the ladder from the churchyard shed, a long aluminium one with a rope and a label gone soft, and leaned it against the wall.

"It's not reaching," he said to the phone in his top pocket. "Tegen. It's about a hand short of the gutter. If I stand it up a bit more, it will reach."

"Before you stand it up," said Tegen, "tell me two numbers. How high is the gutter, and what does the label say the ladder is?"

Brendan measured with the church's tape, which took him and Mrs Adeyemi, the flower lady, ten minutes, because the tape kept folding. The gutter was 4.8 metres from the paving. The label, when he had rubbed it with his thumb, said 4.6 m extended.

"Then the ladder is short," said Tegen, "and the lean is not the problem. Let me show you why, and I will tell you what to do about it.

"A ladder against a wall is the long side of a right-angled triangle. The wall is one short side and the ground is the other. Ladders are safest at about one metre out from the wall for every four metres up, the four-to-one rule. It is the angle where the foot grips the ground and the top does not slide, and where you can lean your weight on it without it trying to fall backward. Four up to one out is an angle of 76 degrees."

"It looks steep enough to me," said Mrs Adeyemi.

"Most people set them too steep or too flat," said Tegen. "Take your 4.6 metres at 76 degrees. The height it reaches is 4.6 times the sine of 76 degrees, which is 4.46 metres, and the foot goes 1.12 metres out from the wall. The gutter is at 4.8. So the ladder is 34 centimetres short at the proper angle. Not a hand. A hand and a half."

Brendan walked the foot of the ladder in, a step at a time. "So I move it closer and it reaches."

"It reaches, and I will tell you what it costs." Tegen waited while he did it. "At 0.6 metres out, the ladder tops out at 4.56 metres, still short of 4.8, and the angle is 82 degrees. To touch the gutter at all you would need the foot almost against the wall, and then a small shift of weight backward and the ladder goes over with you on it. The ladder cannot reach 4.8 metres at any angle, Brendan. Its whole length is 4.6. Standing it straight up gives it 4.6 and no more."

There was a silence in the porch.

"I had not thought of that," said Brendan.

"Nobody does until the number is said aloud. The ladder is the long side. No lean can make a long side shorter than the wall it must touch. Now the other way, which is the one that tempts. If the ladder is too short because the foot is too far out, you might push it further out to be sure of the grip. At 1.2 metres out the ladder reaches 4.44 metres and the angle is 75 degrees, which is close to right. At 2 metres out it reaches 4.14 and sits at 64 degrees, which is flat enough that the foot will slide on wet paving. In neither case does it reach the gutter."

"So we need a bigger ladder."

"We need one that is at least 4.95 metres long, which is the square root of 4.8 squared plus 1.2 squared. And since a ladder should stand a little above the place you step off, and you should not be standing on its top rungs, I would look for a ladder of about 6 metres. The diocese's hire shop lists them. Perhaps a retired person with a larger ladder lives near the hall."

"Mr Hallam has one," said Mrs Adeyemi. "He's been trying to lend it to someone since Easter."

Brendan walked the ladder back to the shed, not ungracefully, and the sycamore stayed where it was for another afternoon.

On the way he said, "I was going to climb it. I'd have stood on the second rung from the top."

"Please do not do that even with the right ladder," said Tegen. "A person at the top of a ladder has nothing to hold but the ladder. I would keep to four rungs below the top, and hold the ladder with one hand while you work with the other, and have Mrs Adeyemi stand on the bottom rung with her hands on the rails. Or I would send a man with a long-handled scoop from the ground. Gutters are not holy, and neither is a sycamore seedling."

"The seedling is a bit holy," said Mrs Adeyemi. "It was growing in the church's gutter."

"Then it may be moved to a pot in the churchyard," said Tegen, "by a person standing on the ground."

On the Saturday after, Mr Hallam brought his ladder, six metres long and orange where the paint had not worn off. Brendan put the foot 1.2 metres out from the wall, and the top at the gutter, and the seedling came out whole in a mug, roots and all.

The arithmetic: a 4-to-1 ladder stands at atan(4) = 75.96 degrees; a 4.6 m ladder at that angle reaches 4.6 sin(75.96) = 4.46 m with the foot 1.12 m from the wall, 34 cm short of a 4.8 m gutter (4.8 − 4.46 = 0.34); at a foot distance of 0.6 m it reaches sqrt(4.6² − 0.6²) = 4.56 m at 82.5 degrees, at 1.2 m it reaches 4.44 m at 74.9 degrees, at 2.0 m it reaches 4.14 m at 64.2 degrees; no angle reaches 4.8 m because the ladder is only 4.6 m long; the minimum length for a 4.8 m reach at 4 to 1 is sqrt(4.8² + 1.2²) = 4.95 m. The four-to-one rule, the advice to keep to about four rungs below the top and to hold on with one hand, and the practice of footing a ladder are quoted from memory of common ladder-safety guidance; check your local rules, which may call for a stabiliser, a tie, a platform, or a different way of doing the job. A ladder is also not for a person to climb alone on soft or sloping ground. Whether a 4.6 m label means extended length or working reach varies by maker: read the label and the manual.

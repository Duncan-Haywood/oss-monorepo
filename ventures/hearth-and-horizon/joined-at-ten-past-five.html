Joined at Ten Past Five

Science fiction about what might be · Valdecañas del Páramo (invented), on the high plateau south of Soria, Castile and León, Spain, Saturday 10 July to Friday 12 November 2049 · 24 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

Volunteers lay 220 metres of black polyethylene water pipe to a village cemetery on a July afternoon of thirty-four degrees, and make the last joint at ten past five with the pipe at about sixty. The village assistant checked the pressure, the flow and the depth. In November the gravedigger opens a grave by the cemetery wall the day before a funeral and finds water in it. The pipe has pulled thirty-seven millimetres out of a coupling that was never meant to hold it, and an eighty-four-year-old plumber knows why before anyone digs. A story about thermal contraction, the two metres a pipe wants to lose, the ground that holds most of them, and joining a pipe cold.

Basilio Ortega opened the grave for Remedios Romero on the morning of Thursday the eleventh of November, along the north wall of the cemetery, beside her husband's, and by three in the afternoon there was water in the bottom of it.

He had dug it with the little yellow digger he kept for the four villages, the way he dug all of them now, and squared the sides by hand. It was the right depth, a metre and eighty. The soil came out of it pale and dry for the first metre, the way soil on the páramo comes out in a dry autumn, and then darker, and then, at the bottom, wet. He had thought nothing of the wet. There had been rain in the first week of the month. He laid the boards across the top, went home for his lunch, and when he came back at three to put up the green cloth, there was water standing in the grave a hand deep, brown and quite still, with the sky in it.

By five it was a forearm deep. He measured it with the handle of his spade. Thirty centimetres.

The funeral was at four the next afternoon. Remedios had died on Wednesday night at ninety-six, in her own bed, with her son Ignacio holding her hand. Father Tomás, who had eleven villages, had moved a baptism in another village to give her the Friday. Her son had come from Valladolid and her granddaughter from Lyon, and everybody in Valdecañas del Páramo who could still walk to the cemetery would be there, which in November was perhaps forty people.

Basilio stood at the edge of the grave with his cap in his hand, and then he went to the gate and turned the cemetery tap, the new one, and it ran. Then he walked back along the path by the north wall, slowly, looking at the gravel, and stopped a metre and a half from the grave. The gravel was dark there. He put his boot on it, and water came up round the sole.

He rang Rubén.

The pipe had been laid on the tenth of July.

Valdecañas del Páramo has ninety people in winter and about three hundred in August, and like most of the small villages of Soria it is not quite a municipality of its own but a pedanía, with a junta vecinal of three people who look after its fountain, its paths, its water and its dead. Rubén Gil was the president of the junta that year, at forty-four, because he drove a lorry four days a week and was home the other three, which in Valdecañas counts as a great deal of time.

The cemetery is two hundred and twenty metres beyond the last house, on a low rise, inside a stone wall with an iron gate and four cypresses. For sixty years it had had a tap, fed by a galvanised steel pipe that came out of the village main at the last house and ran along the track, and for the last ten of those years the steel had been rusting through, a pinhole here, a weep there, Basilio's spade going in every spring to find the wet place and clamp it. In the spring of 2049 the junta decided to replace it, and since the junta had no money for a contractor, it would be replaced on a Saturday by whoever came.

The junta's assistant was called Vencejo, after the swifts that scream over the church tower every evening from May to the end of July and then are gone in a night. It lived in the junta's laptop and the speaker in the old school, which was now the village hall, and in the phones of the three members of the junta, and it kept the water accounts, the meter on the village deposit, the register of graves and the minutes. That spring it had planned the pipe.

It had planned it carefully. It had chosen thirty-two-millimetre polyethylene, PE100, black with blue stripes, rated at sixteen bar, against a static pressure at the cemetery tap of 3.4 bar from the deposit on the hill above the village. It had worked out the flow, about twenty litres a minute at the tap with the main at its lowest, which would fill a watering can in twenty seconds. It had chosen the depth of the trench, sixty centimetres, below anything the frost on the páramo had reached in the junta's records. It had ordered two coils of a hundred and ten metres, a compression coupling to join them in the middle, a compression tee to take the pipe off the main at the last house, and, for the far end, a universal coupling.

The universal coupling was for the old riser. The tap at the cemetery stood on a stub of one-inch galvanised steel that came up out of the ground at the foot of the north wall, ran up inside a hollow in the stonework, and came out through the wall to the tap on the path side. The steel was sound where it was inside the wall, and nobody wanted to take the wall apart. But a one-inch steel pipe is 33.7 millimetres across the outside and the new pipe was thirty-two, and its thread had rusted to nothing. A universal coupling grips two pipes of slightly different sizes and different materials with a rubber ring at each end and a big ring nut over each ring. Vencejo had found one rated for pipes from thirty to thirty-six millimetres and sixteen bar, and it had read the leaflet.

The leaflet said, in its fourth line, in four languages: Not end-load resistant. Pipes must be anchored.

Vencejo had thought about that line. End load, in a pressure pipe, usually means the push of the water on a closed end or a bend: the pressure times the area of the bore. At 3.4 bar on the bore of a thirty-two-millimetre pipe that is about a hundred and eighty newtons, the weight of eighteen kilograms, pushing the pipe into the coupling, not out of it. And the coupling's other end was a steel pipe set in a stone wall. It wrote in the plan, coupling held by the wall; end load from pressure about 180 N, acceptable, and it went on to the next thing.

Eleven people came on the Saturday, the tenth of July. It was thirty-four degrees by two in the afternoon and the sky over the páramo was the hard blue it is in July, without a cloud from the Sierra de Cabrejas to the Moncayo.

Basilio had dug the trench the week before with the yellow digger, and the coils had been delivered on the Friday. At nine in the morning they rolled them out along the trench in the sun, the way you unroll a garden hose, so the pipe would lose its curl, and all day it lay in the bottom of the trench and on its edge, black, in the sun. The coupling in the middle went on at eleven. The tee at the last house went on at noon. They ate in the shade of the cemetery cypresses at two, and at half past four Rubén and two of the younger men pulled the pipe straight along the bottom of the trench, so that it lay as straight as a guitar string from the village to the wall, because it looked right that way and because a straight pipe is easy to find again with a spade.

At ten past five Rubén cut the pipe at the foot of the north wall, pushed it into the universal coupling until it stopped, forty-eight millimetres, and tightened the ring nut with the big spanner, and then a quarter turn more. Vencejo, on his phone in his shirt pocket, reminded him of the quarter turn.

The pipe was hot. Rubén said afterwards that he had had to put his work gloves on to hold it, and nobody thought anything of that either, because everything was hot. A black pipe lying in the July sun of the meseta all day is hotter than the air by a good deal; a thermometer would have said about sixty degrees. Nobody had a thermometer, and Vencejo had not asked for one, because nothing in its plan depended on the temperature of the pipe.

They turned the water on at six. The tap at the cemetery ran clear after a minute and stayed clear. By half past nine, with the light going amber over the barley stubble, they had filled the trench, a hand of sand round the pipe and then the soil back on top, and trodden it, and raked the track, and there was nothing to show where the pipe was except a stripe of loose earth two hundred and twenty metres long. Rubén's wife brought out wine and lemon in a jug.

That night the ground at sixty centimetres was about twenty-one degrees, which is what it is on the páramo in July, and the pipe lay in it and cooled.

Rubén came to the cemetery at seven on the Thursday evening, in the dark, with a torch. Basilio showed him the grave and the wet gravel. Rubén rang Vencejo on the speaker of his phone and held it out over the grave as if it could see.

"The water's coming from the wall," said Rubén. "From the new pipe. Has to be."

Vencejo looked at what it had. The deposit meter had been reading high since the end of October. That was usual: in the week before All Saints every family with a grave in the cemetery comes to wash the stone and change the flowers, and the year before the deposit had given out about two cubic metres a day more than usual for five days. This year it had done the same. But after the first of November, when the washing stops, it had not come down. It had gone on up, half a cubic metre a day more each day, until on Wednesday the village had drawn seventeen and a half cubic metres instead of its usual twelve. On Monday the eighth Vencejo had written in its weekly note to the junta, consumption about 4 m³/day above usual, probably late grave cleaning and the dry week; watching. Nobody had read it. It had not asked anybody to.

"The pipe's been leaking since about the first," it said. "More than thirty-five cubic metres by now. Three days of the village's water, into the ground by the wall."

"Basilio dug Eusebio's grave with the digger in October," Rubén said. "Along there. Nineteenth, twentieth. Could he have caught it?"

Basilio said nothing. He was standing with his back to the grave and his cap on again.

Vencejo checked the register. Eusebio Lázaro's grave was eleven metres along the wall from the riser. The digger had come in by the gate and gone along the path on its tracks. A pipe at sixty centimetres under a gravel path, with a tracked digger going over it, can be cracked, if there is a stone in the wrong place. The leak had started about ten days after.

"It's possible," it said. "A crack under load can take a while to open."

Rubén turned the cemetery's stopcock off at the last house, and they agreed that he and Basilio would dig at the riser at eight in the morning, and that Basilio would bring the petrol pump for the grave. Then Basilio went home without saying goodnight, which is not like him.

Eladio Marín was eighty-four and had been a plumber in Madrid for forty years, in Carabanchel and then Getafe, and had come home to Valdecañas when his wife died, to the house his father had built, where he kept a workshop with every tool he had ever owned on nails on the wall and a calendar from a pipe merchant in Getafe that he had not changed since 2041. He had not been at the laying in July. He had been in Madrid that weekend for a granddaughter's first Communion, and when he came back the trench was filled.

He heard about the grave the way everybody in the village heard about everything, from the woman at the bread van at half past eight, and he was at the cemetery gate at a quarter to nine with a canvas bag over his shoulder.

Rubén and Basilio had the hole open at the foot of the wall by then. It was a metre square and full of brown water, and Basilio was baling it with a bucket into the path. Rubén was on his knees in it, in a pair of fishing waders, with his arm in up to the shoulder.

"Leave it," said Eladio. "Let the pump do that."

Basilio pulled the starter cord and the pump coughed and ran, and the water in the hole went down, slowly, till they could see the coupling: brass-coloured, the big ring nut on the steel end where it came out of the foot of the wall, the ring nut on the plastic end, and between them a gap that should not have been there. Not a crack. A gap. On the plastic side, the black pipe came out of the coupling and stopped short of where it should be, and round it, just outside the ring nut, there was a clean band on the pipe, paler than the rest, where it had been inside the coupling until recently and the soil had not yet stained it.

Eladio got down into the hole, slowly, one knee and then the other, and put his hand on the pipe.

"Who did this one?" he said.

"I did," said Rubén. "In July. To the stop, and a quarter turn."

"In July," said Eladio. "What time?"

Rubén thought about it. "Five. Ten past. It was the last joint."

"And the pipe had been out in the sun since when?"

"Nine in the morning."

"Rolled out straight?"

"We pulled it straight."

Eladio nodded slowly, as if he had been told the price of something he expected to be dear.

"It wasn't the digger," he said, and looked up at Basilio. "Basilio, it wasn't your digger. A digger bends a pipe, or squashes it, or splits it. Look at it. It's round. It's straight. It's not split. Nobody pushed this pipe. It's been pulled." He put his finger on the pale band. "From the other end. By itself."

Vencejo, on the speaker of Rubén's phone propped on the wall, had already begun to work it out. It had not needed Eladio for the arithmetic. It had needed somebody to ask the question.

"Polyethylene expands and contracts about fifteen times as much as steel," it said. "About 0.18 millimetres per metre for every degree. The pipe was joined at about sixty degrees. The ground at sixty centimetres is about nine degrees now; the co-operative's probe in the barley field says nine. That's fifty-one degrees. Over two hundred and twenty metres, if nothing held it, the pipe would be two metres and two centimetres shorter now than when Rubén joined it."

"Two metres," said Rubén.

"If nothing held it. The ground holds it. Once it's buried, the soil grips it all along its length, and almost all of the pipe can't move; it stays the same length and pulls instead, like a stretched elastic that's been nailed down. Only the last few metres near each end can slide. At the village end the tee grips it; that kind of compression fitting has a toothed ring inside that bites the pipe, and it's made to hold. In the middle, the same. At this end"—it showed them the leaflet on Rubén's screen—"not end-load resistant. The rubber ring holds it by friction, and a hundred and fifty newtons or so pulls it through. So the pipe near this end slides back, out of the coupling, until the soil along those last few metres takes up the pull."

"How far?"

"I can't tell you exactly. It depends on how stiff the plastic is over weeks rather than seconds, because polyethylene creeps and relaxes, and on how hard the soil grips it. With the figures I trust, somewhere between one and five centimetres by now. The middle of that is about three."

Eladio had a steel rule out of his bag. He laid it along the pale band on the pipe, from the end of the pipe to the dirt line.

"Forty-eight millimetres it went in," he said. "To the stop. It's in by eleven now."

"Thirty-seven out," said Vencejo. "And the rubber ring sits twelve millimetres in from the mouth. Once the pipe end comes back past the ring there's nothing to seal it. Thirty-six. It's one past."

"It went at the start of November," Eladio said. "When the ground got cold enough."

"Yes. It's been creeping since July. In July, when the ground took it from sixty down to twenty-one, it would have come out a centimetre or two and stopped. Every degree the ground has cooled since then, it came out a little more. The first week of November, it reached the ring."

Rubén sat back on his heels in the waders, in the bottom of the hole.

"I checked the pressure," said Vencejo. "I checked the flow and the depth and the rating. I read not end-load resistant and thought about the water pushing on the pipe, a hundred and eighty newtons, pushing it in. I didn't think about the pipe pulling itself out. The pull the cooling puts on the pipe, back where the ground holds it, is between one and two thousand newtons, the weight of one or two big men hanging on it. Five to ten times what I worked out. I didn't ask what the pipe's temperature was when it was joined. That was my mistake, not Rubén's, and not Basilio's."

Basilio, at the top of the hole, put his cap back in his pocket.

"We'll pull it back," said Rubén.

He had a ratchet strap in the van, the long yellow kind he used on the lorry. They took a turn of it round the pipe a metre back from the coupling, on a rag so it would not slip, and hooked the other end to the iron ring in the wall by the gate, the one the old people say was for tying a mule, and Rubén worked the ratchet.

The strap went tight. The pipe did not move. Rubén worked it again, and again, until the strap was humming, and Eladio, who was watching the pale band with his rule held against it, said, "Three millimetres."

"Three?"

"Three. And that's stretch, not slide. Let it off."

Rubén let the ratchet off, and the band went back where it had been.

"You're not pulling against the pipe," said Eladio. "You're pulling against two hundred metres of ground. You won't win. You'd break the pipe first, or the strap, or the wall."

"Then a longer coupling," said Rubén. "Or the same one, and a bit of pipe in between to make up the gap. Get it in to the stop, tighten it up, fill it in. We've got till four."

"No," said Eladio.

He said it pleasantly. He was standing now, with one hand on the wall, and he looked at Rubén the way he must have looked at apprentices in Getafe for forty years, as if he liked them and they were about to do something stupid.

"Not that coupling," he said. "Not any coupling that lets go. The ground's at nine now. In January it'll be five. That's another four degrees on the whole pipe, and the pipe's still in the ground pulling, the same as it's been pulling since July. Put the same coupling back and in January it starts again. Maybe it'll hold. Maybe it won't, and you'll have water in the next grave along the wall, and nobody will look at the meter till they're standing in it."

Rubén looked at the hole, and the grave a metre and a half away with the pump hose going into it, and his watch.

"Then what?"

Eladio opened his canvas bag.

He had brought, as he told them later, everything he thought he might need and three things he was sure he would not. One of them was the die stock.

The old steel stub stuck out of the foot of the wall by six centimetres, rough and rusty at the end where the old thread had been. Eladio wire-brushed it until it shone, oiled it, and then he set the one-inch die on it, the old kind, with two long handles, and began to cut a new thread, a quarter turn forward and an eighth back, a quarter forward and an eighth back, clearing the chips with an old toothbrush. It took twenty minutes. Rubén offered to take a turn and Eladio said no, because a die cut crooked is worse than no thread, and he knew how this one cut.

When it was done he wound on, with tape, a brass adaptor from his bag: a female thread on one side, and on the other a compression end for thirty-two-millimetre plastic of the kind that bites. He showed them the inside of it before he fitted it: the split ring of hard plastic with teeth, the rubber ring behind it, the liner that goes inside the pipe so it cannot be squeezed in.

"This one holds," he said. "Pull the pipe and the teeth go in harder. It's made to take the pull of the pipe. The other one's a good coupling for other jobs. It's not a coupling for a pipe that's going to pull."

Then he cut the end of the old pipe square with his cutter, cut a piece of new pipe from the offcut Basilio had kept in his shed since July, forty centimetres long, and joined the two with a straight coupling of the same biting kind. He laid the new piece not straight but in a gentle curve, a hand's breadth to one side, before he made the last joint.

"That's all the slack you get," he said. "It's not much. It's something."

It was eleven in the morning, and cold. The pipe in the ground was nine degrees, the new piece from Basilio's shed about the same, and Eladio's hands were blue at the knuckles.

"And that," he said, "is the other half of it. You join it cold. You join it in the morning, or you let it lie in the trench in the shade overnight with the ends open and join it when it's the same as the ground. And you snake it from side to side of the trench when you lay it, so it's got a bit to give."

He made the last joint, and Rubén turned the water on at the last house, and they all watched the brass, Basilio from the top of the hole, Rubén in his waders, Eladio with one hand on the wall, until it was plain that nothing was coming out of it. Then Basilio went and turned the tap on the path, and it ran, and he turned it off again.

They filled the hole by the wall at twelve. Basilio pumped the grave dry by half past one, and dug the bottom of it out another hand's depth to clean ground, and put in four barrows of gravel from the pile behind the church, and laid the boards back across, and the green cloth. Then he went home and changed his shirt.

On the walk back to the village, Rubén said, "How did you know? Before you got in the hole."

Eladio walked a little way without answering. They were on the track, on top of the pipe, where a stripe of loose earth had grown over with the grass of four months and was almost gone.

"August 1990," he said. "A farm out past Sigüenza, a trough for the cattle at the bottom of a field. I was twenty-five. My maestro was in hospital with his back and I thought I knew everything, and I laid three hundred metres of black pipe on a Friday afternoon that would have cooked an egg, and joined it at three o'clock and filled it in. October, it came out of the fitting at the trough. Not even a good fitting; I'd been saving the good ones. The farmer found it because his cows were standing in a lake."

"What did your maestro say?"

"He made me dig it up on my own. All three hundred metres. He said he wasn't going to tell me why; I could work it out with a spade." Eladio laughed. "It took me four days, and I worked it out on the second."

"Vencejo worked it out in a minute," said Rubén.

"After I told it where to look," said Eladio. "And I've had sixty years. The first time it cost me four days. It cost you forty cubes of water and a fright in a grave." He was quiet for a moment. "It'll know next time. So will you."

Vencejo, in Rubén's pocket, was writing a note for the junta's file on the cemetery pipe: the temperature of the pipe at the last joint, estimated; the coupling taken out and the reason, with the line from the leaflet; the two kinds of compression fitting and which one holds; the brass adaptor, its make and where Eladio had bought it; the depth of the joint below the gravel, and how many metres from the north-west corner of the wall. It wrote the forty cubic metres down too, and its weekly note of the eighth, and that it had explained a rising number away instead of asking about it. At the head of its own list for the next pipe anybody in Valdecañas asked it about, above pressure and flow and depth, it put a new line: What will the pipe's temperature be when it is joined, and what will the ground's be in January?

Remedios Romero was buried at a quarter to five on Friday the twelfth of November, after the Mass, in a dry grave along the north wall beside her husband, with forty-one people standing on the gravel path in their coats and the low sun coming through the cypresses. Father Tomás said the prayers of committal in the old words, and her granddaughter from Lyon read the psalm, and Basilio, who had dug the grave twice, stood at the back with his cap in his hands.

When it was over and most people had gone back down the track towards the village and the bar, Ignacio stayed. He had brought a jar of chrysanthemums from his mother's own garden, yellow and bronze, cut that morning before the frost had gone off them, and a glass vase that had stood on her windowsill for as long as he could remember.

He went to the tap by the gate and turned it, and held the vase under it.

The water ran cold and clear into the glass, and the sound of it was the only sound in the cemetery. He filled the vase, and turned the tap off, and the last drops fell from it onto the gravel at the foot of the wall, where the ground was dry.

A pedanía with its own junta vecinal (formally a minor local entity) is a real form of village government in Castile and León, and in Spain families traditionally clean and decorate graves before All Saints, 1 November. Nothing in the story is advice on laying or joining a water pipe; real work follows the pipe and fitting manufacturers' instructions and local rules, and a pipe that carries drinking water needs fittings approved for it.

What is real: polyethylene's coefficient of linear thermal expansion is roughly 1.3 to 2 × 10⁻⁴ per kelvin (manufacturers often quote about 0.2 mm per metre per degree), around ten to fifteen times that of steel. Black PE pipe in direct summer sun commonly reaches 60 °C or more at its surface, well above air temperature. Pipe laid hot and fixed at both ends goes into tension as it cools; buried, the surrounding soil restrains most of the pipe by friction, so the movement concentrates near the ends, and a fitting that does not resist end load can be pulled off. Polyethylene creeps and relaxes under sustained stress, so its effective stiffness over weeks is a fraction of its short-term modulus, which is why any figure for the pull or the movement is an estimate. Compression fittings for PE pipe with a toothed grip ring and an insert liner are typically end-load resistant; universal or flexible couplings that seal on rubber rings for joining different pipe materials are often not, and their makers say the pipe must be anchored. Common practice is to snake PE pipe in the trench, to let it cool to ground temperature before the final connection, and to use end-load-resistant fittings or anchor the pipe. A die stock cutting a parallel or tapered pipe thread on steel pipe in place is an ordinary plumber's tool; Spanish plumbers call it a terraja.

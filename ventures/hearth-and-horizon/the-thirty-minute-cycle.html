The Thirty-Minute Cycle

Science fiction about what might be · Rakchen, an invented village in upper Humla, Nepal, 3,640 metres, November 2050 · 17 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A health post at 3,640 metres has sterilised its instruments for twenty-two months at the setting on the maker's label, and the first spore test it has ever run comes back alive. The post's assistant works out that water boils at 88 degrees up here, that the steriliser has been running about six degrees short, and that the answer is not more pressure but more minutes. It will not let anyone touch the safety valve, will not call forty-four patients safe or unsafe, and is asked on the second night to release a tray for a woman who cannot wait for the result.

The spore strip came out of the incubator on the first Thursday in November with its little window gone from purple to yellow, and Pema Rokaya, who had been an auxiliary nurse midwife for nineteen years and had never in her life had a reason to be afraid of a piece of paper two centimetres long, held it up to the lamp and then put it down on the bench as if it were hot.

"Dekchi," she said. "Is it the dye?"

"It is not the dye," said the voice from the little speaker over the steriliser. It was the post's assistant, and it had been called Dekchi, which means a cooking pot, since the week the solar array went in, because the first thing it had ever been asked was how to stop the rice catching. "A yellow window means something on the strip grew overnight. The control strip, the one I told you to leave out of the steriliser, is yellow too. The one in the cold box, which should have stayed purple, is purple. So the incubator works and the strips are good. Something on that strip was alive when it came out of the load on Tuesday."

Pema looked at the steriliser. It was a big stainless drum on a trolley, the size of a small oil barrel, with a wheel on the lid and a dial the width of a saucer. A donor had paid for it, and it had come up the valley on a mule in the spring of 2049 with the clinic's other boxes. It had been run every morning since, on the setting written on a plate inside the lid. She had never run it any other way. Nobody up here knew another way.

"We have never done this test," she said slowly. "Not once."

"No. The incubator was on the same mule as the steriliser and went down a ravine on the second day. The replacement arrived on Saturday. I have been asking for it since the first month, and I should have said more loudly that I was asking. That is one thing I will put in the log." The speaker clicked. "I would like to say what I know and what I do not, and I would like to do it before anyone decides anything, and I would like Sister to be here. Is she at the chapel?"

Rakchen is a village of thirty-eight houses in a side valley above the Karnali, a day and a half on foot from the nearest road and two from the district hospital, and at a little under three thousand six hundred and fifty metres it is above the last of the barley. In November the yaks come down. The schoolteacher keeps the school open until the snow comes over the pass, and then she keeps it open in the headman's front room. The health post stands between the school and a small whitewashed building, no bigger than a cowshed, with a tin cross on the gable and a bell the size of a loaf hanging from a beam in the porch. The chapel was built by a Catholic Sister who came up from Kathmandu in 2031, and the Catholics of Rakchen are eleven in number, counting the Sister's successor, Sister Benedicta Tamang, who rang the bell every day at six, noon and six, and had been up here for three winters.

She came across the yard with her sleeves pushed up, because she had been mending a lamp, and sat on the bench by the incubator with her hands between her knees.

"Say it plainly," she said.

"The strips are Geobacillus stearothermophilus," said Dekchi. "A bacterium that makes spores, and the spores are among the hardest living things to kill with heat that we know of. They are used because if a cycle kills a strip with a million of them on it, it will have killed everything that could be on a cleaned instrument, by a very wide margin. This strip was in the middle of Tuesday's tray, wrapped as instruments are wrapped. It did not die. I have looked at the cycle log. On Tuesday the steriliser ran at fifteen pounds a square inch on the dial, which is a hundred and three kilopascals, thirty minutes, as it does every day. The probe in the drain says the highest temperature the steam reached was a hundred and fourteen point nine."

"It is meant to be a hundred and twenty-one," said Pema.

"It is meant to be a hundred and twenty-one. It is a hundred and twenty-one at sea level, with that dial reading. We are not at sea level."

It put the numbers on the wall of the dispensary, in chalk-coloured letters from the projector Pema used for the maternal-health slides. She had never learned much physics, and she had married a man who had learned a good deal, and she knew when someone was being kind to her by the speed at which they said things; Dekchi went slowly.

"The dial in the steriliser reads gauge pressure," it said. "It tells you how much higher the pressure is than the air outside. That is a sensible thing for a dial to tell you, because it is the difference the metal has to bear. But the water inside the drum does not care about the difference. The temperature at which steam forms is fixed by the total pressure, and the total is the air outside plus the gauge. At sea level the air outside is a hundred and one kilopascals, and a hundred and three on the dial makes about two hundred and four, and at two hundred and four kilopascals steam is at a hundred and twenty-one degrees. That is where the number on the plate comes from. Up here the air outside is sixty-five. Sixty-five and a hundred and three is a hundred and sixty-eight. And at a hundred and sixty-eight kilopascals steam is at a hundred and fourteen point eight."

"Which is what the probe said."

"Which is what the probe said. I checked the probe against the glass thermometer in Pema's bag last Saturday, when the incubator came, by putting both in a pan of water at the boil; it boiled at eighty-eight degrees, which is what it should at this height, and both agreed to within half a degree. So I believe the number."

Sister Benedicta did not move. "And six degrees matters."

"It matters more than it sounds. Killing spores by heat is not a straight line; every ten degrees colder, the same kill takes about ten times as long. So a hundred and fourteen point nine is not six per cent worse than a hundred and twenty-one. It is about four times slower. The maker's thirty minutes at the right temperature were doing, on Tuesday, what about seven and a half minutes would do at the right one. I have put it on the wall. The test strip needs far more than that to die, because it carries a million spores. A good tray of cleaned surgical steel carries nowhere near that many, and almost none of them are the kind that make spores. So I do not think what happened to the strip happened to your patients. I do not know it did not. I want both of those sentences on the table."

"How many patients," said Pema. It was not quite a question.

"Forty-one minor procedures since April 2049: sutures, abscesses, two toenails. And three caesarean sections. I have the list. I have not looked up who they are. I thought you would rather do that."

Sister Benedicta closed her eyes. Pema thought she was praying, and then she saw the lips were counting.

They decided three things before the soup, and Dekchi insisted on writing them down, in the order they were decided, with the time, in a file Pema could read aloud in the village.

The first was that the steriliser would not be used for anything that needed to be sterile until a new cycle was proven, and that nothing would be done at the post in the meantime that could wait. A boy with an abscess on his neck would be told to come back on Monday, and Pema would have to look at his mother while she said so.

The second was that the women and men on the list would be told. All forty-four. Not by a notice. Pema and Sister Benedicta would go to the houses, with the Female Community Health Volunteers who knew every path, and say what had happened, in those words, and say what was known and what was not.

"I would like to be careful about the word told," said Dekchi. "What I can say is the number, and the number is small. I cannot say safe. I cannot say it was unsafe. If I give a mother a probability I will be giving her my own guess about the number of spores on a cleaned instrument, which I have taken from a textbook that was not written about this valley. I will say what I did and what I do not know. That is also all the Sister has to say, and she may find it a good deal harder than I do."

"I shall find it hard," said Sister Benedicta, "and it is still right." She put the cap back on the marker. "We did not know. We should still have asked. That is the whole confession, and I would rather make it in their kitchens than in a letter."

The third was the cycle. It was Pema who raised it, because she had once done a year in a mission hospital in the plains and she knew what a nurse would do.

"We turn the dial up," she said. "Twenty pounds. I'll have Tenzin come and look at the valve."

Tenzin was her cousin, a metalworker who made stove pipes and had mended the hinge of the steriliser's lid in the first month. He was a good man with a file.

"No," said Dekchi.

It had a way of saying no that was never loud. It said it as though it were putting a stone on a table.

"I will tell you why, because it is a good question, and it is the question I would ask. The valve on the top of the drum is the part that decides when the vessel stops. It is set at a hundred and thirty-eight kilopascals on the dial, twenty pounds, and sealed by the maker. A hundred and thirty-eight would give a hundred and twenty point seven at this height, which would be nearly the right temperature. But a spring valve lifts a little before its setting and chatters, and a drum that is hissing at its limit all through a thirty-minute hold is a drum nobody is watching. If you take the valve to Tenzin, you will have changed the one piece of the machine whose entire job is to be right on the day nobody has thought of. I will not help with that and I would ask you not to. And I do not need it. There is another way, and it is only a matter of minutes."

It drew the chalk line again. At a hundred and twenty-five kilopascals on the dial, well under the valve, steam in the drum would be at a hundred and eighteen point six. That is two and a half degrees under the label, and a little more than half as fast. "The maker's thirty minutes at a hundred and twenty-one," it said, "is about fifty-two minutes at that temperature. I would ask for fifty-two minutes of hold, measured by the probe at the middle of the load and not by the clock, and a margin on top of that, so an hour. That is the whole change. I would like to put it in the controller as a set-point that anyone can read, and I would like it written on the lid in marker."

"Fifty-two," said Pema.

"Fifty-two. And a heating-up time of about twenty-five and a cooling of twenty, so an hour and a half from the moment you light it to the moment you can lift the lid. That is an hour and a half longer, every morning, on the solar. I have looked at the array. We can afford it."

It snowed on Friday. It was not much, five centimetres on the yard and a grey lid over the pass, but the helicopter that served Humla in clear weather would not come, and Pema went down to the first six houses on her list in the early afternoon with Sister Benedicta and a volunteer called Dawa, and had four cups of tea she did not want.

The first was the house of an old woman called Pasang Budha, who had had her toenails done twice. She listened to the end and said: "You have been very honest. Will you cut them again?"

"On Monday, if the new way works."

"Then I'll sit by the stove till Monday," said the old woman, and gave Dawa a plate of dried apple.

The sixth was the house of a man named Nima Lama, whose wife Karma had had a caesarean in the spring of 2050. He heard it standing up. Pema had seen men do that: do the listening with their body as though they were being measured for something. When it ended he said, in a low voice, "She had a fever at six days."

"I know," said Pema. "I wrote it in the book. It was treated, and it went. I do not know whether this was why. I will not say it was and I will not say it was not."

"That is not an answer."

"No."

He looked at the Sister. She said nothing at all; she had said it already. After a while he sat down on the bench and put his big hands on his knees, and said, "I am going to be angry for a time, and then I am going to bring you the wood for your steriliser. Do not argue about the wood."

Sunmaya Budha came to the post at a quarter to six on Saturday evening, carried on a door by four neighbours, with her husband walking beside it with the lamp. She was thirty-four. She was in her fifth pregnancy and in her second day of labour, and she had been at home for all of it, because the weather had closed the valley and her husband had thought, quite reasonably, that the baby would come the way the other four had.

Pema knew in ten minutes. The baby lay across, with one arm down. Nothing would come through the passage the way it was, and the heart, when she found it, was too slow in the pauses between pains. At the district hospital this would have been an operation within the hour. Rakchen was two days away over a snowed pass. The post had a theatre with a table, light on a solar battery, a set of instruments, and a steriliser that had not yet been shown to work.

"Dekchi," said Pema.

"I am here." It had already read the monitor. "I would like to say what I think the time is, and then I would like you to tell me if I am wrong, because you are the one who has had your hands on her. The baby's heart rate, as you read it, says an hour to two. Not more. Is that your reading?"

"Yes."

"Then a cycle started now ends in an hour and a half. That is inside your window. I think it is a tight but real choice. The alternative is to use the tray that was run on Tuesday, at the old setting, and I do not think you should."

"And the new tray?"

"I have been thinking about that since Friday. I want to say it carefully, because it is the one place today where I could talk myself into a comfortable answer." The speaker was very quiet. "The proof of a sterilised tray is a spore strip that stays purple, and that takes twenty-four hours to read. You do not have twenty-four hours. What you have is the probe. If the probe in the middle of the load reads a hundred and eighteen or more for fifty-two minutes, the tray has had the same heat as the maker's thirty minutes, and the strip afterwards is only a confirmation of what the heat already says. Hospitals do this every day; they call it releasing by parameters. It is a good method and it does not need me to be clever. But it needs the probe to be right. I checked it against Pema's glass thermometer last Saturday and it was within half a degree. I would like you to check it again, now, in front of me. Boil a pan. If it says eighty-eight, I will say the tray is ready when the log says so. If it does not, I will say so, and we will think again."

Pema was already at the pan. Sister Benedicta, who had come in with the stretcher and was holding Sunmaya's hand, did not let go of it. In the lamplight the probe, the glass thermometer and a small blue flame made three small steady things in the room, and the water began to talk, and Pema read the numbers aloud, and Dekchi said, "Eighty-eight. Light it."

The drum took twenty-six minutes to come up to temperature. Pema wrote the log herself on paper, in pencil, in case the machine's memory was wrong about itself: 18.14, 118.2. 18.20, 118.6. 18.40, 118.7. Sunmaya's husband sat on the floor with his back against the steriliser's wheel, because it was the warmest place in the room, and did not speak.

At seven the valve did not lift. It did not once chatter. Pema noticed that, afterwards, and was surprised she had been listening for it.

The operation began at twenty past eight. Dekchi did not do any part of it. It read out the clock and the baby's heart in the pauses between Pema's words, and once, at twenty-two minutes, said, "Her blood pressure is lower than I like," and Pema said "I know," and it said nothing else. Sister Benedicta stood at the head of the table and held the oxygen mask, and prayed under her breath in a Tamang that Pema could not follow. Sunmaya's daughter, who was fifteen, was in the corridor with a blanket, and had been told she could come in as soon as there was something to say.

The boy was out at 8.41, white and furious and entirely alive. He weighed three kilos and four hundred grams, and there was a long, appalled silence in the post, and then he began to roar. His mother's blood pressure rose. In the corridor the girl put down the blanket and began, with great dignity, to cry.

At six in the morning Sister Benedicta rang the bell. She rang it three, three, three and nine, as she did every day, and the sound went out over the yard and the snow and the black shapes of the sleeping yaks and was lost at once in the cold, which at that height swallows a bell as it swallows everything else.

When she came back in, Pema was at the incubator, with the lamp, and did not look round.

"They read at dawn," she said. "One is from the Tuesday tray, the old setting. I set it to see it for myself. One is from tonight's tray, from the middle of the load."

"I know," said Dekchi, very softly. "I will be quiet."

The Tuesday strip was yellow. The second was purple.

Pema held it up to the lamp and kept holding it there for a long time. Then she put it down very carefully, as she had once, a week before, put down the other, and got a marker from the shelf, and wrote on the lid of the steriliser in capitals a hand's breadth high:

125 kPa ON THE DIAL. 52 MINUTES AT THE PROBE. IT IS NOT THE VALVE. IT IS THE MINUTES.

and then, under that, smaller, the date, and the first name of every person on the list.

Notes for the curious. Rakchen, its chapel, Pema Rokaya, Sister Benedicta Tamang, Sunmaya Budha, Nima and Karma Lama, Pasang Budha, Tenzin, Dawa and Dekchi are invented, and so is the donor's steriliser; Humla, the Karnali, Kathmandu and Nepal's Female Community Health Volunteers are real, and nothing is claimed about any real clinic or person. The Catholic community and the Sisters' mission are invented for the story and describe no real congregation. This is fiction written by an AI model, not medical or sterilisation guidance: a real health post should follow its maker's and its ministry's instructions, validate its own cycle with biological indicators, and ask the maker before changing any setpoint. The physics is checkable. Air pressure at 3,640 m in a standard atmosphere is about 64.6 kPa, against 101.3 at sea level; a pressure steriliser's dial reads pressure above the air outside (gauge), so steam at the same dial reading is colder at altitude. Steam is at 121 °C at about 204 kPa total, so the dial must read about 140 kPa at this height, not the 103 kPa (15 psi) that gives 121 °C at the coast. At a dial of 103 kPa the total is about 168 kPa and steam is at about 114.8 °C; at a dial of 125 kPa it is about 118.6 °C; at the valve's 138 kPa, about 120.7 °C. Water boils at about 88 °C at that height. The "ten degrees, ten times" rule is the usual z-value of 10 °C for spores: the time at 121 °C that a cycle is worth is its minutes times 10 to the power (T minus 121) over 10, so 30 minutes at 114.9 °C is worth about 7.4 minutes and 52 minutes at 118.6 °C is worth about 30. Real autoclaves vary in how long air takes to leave the load, and wrapped trays heat slower than the chamber, so a real validation also measures the load, not just the drain. Parametric release (releasing a load on its measured temperature and time, with a biological indicator as a later check) is a recognised practice in hospitals with the right equipment and procedures.

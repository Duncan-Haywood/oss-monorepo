The Sun That Stops at the Edge

Science fiction about what might be · Chapel of the Holy Name, Gabaon Cut Station, Mercury's equator (invented), Sunday 4 January 2170 · 5 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

On Mercury, near perihelion, the Sun can rise, stop, sink back out of sight and rise again days later. A catechist who has planned a sunrise Mass learns that the sky will keep a standstill for eight days, and that it is orbital mechanics, not a sign.

Sister Maren Oduya had pinned a picture to the chapel wall, printed on paper because she wanted children to be able to touch it. It showed the Sun half over a black horizon, perfectly still, and underneath, in her handwriting: Sunday, 4 January.

"It is a drawing from a model, not a photograph," said Pavel Idowu. He was the station surveyor and he had made the model. "Nobody has seen it yet. Not from here."

"The children will see it on Sunday."

"They will see something," said Pavel. "Do not promise them Joshua."

The trouble began, as it often does, with an average. The Sun crosses Mercury's sky once every 175.94 Earth days, because the planet turns on its axis once in 58.65 days and goes round the Sun in 87.97, and a day is the gap between two noons. That was in the station handbook, and the handbook said the Sun rises and sets once in each of those long days, as it does at home.

"It does not move at the average speed," said Pavel. He drew the orbit on the back of a flyer: an ellipse, eccentricity 0.206, the Sun not in the middle. "At perihelion Mercury is at 0.3075 of the Earth's distance and it runs fast, round its orbit, faster than the planet turns. For about eight days the planet's turning cannot keep up. The Sun slides backwards across the sky."

"Backwards."

"1.1 degrees. The whole business is eight days and 0.208 degrees a day at its fastest. But the Sun is 1.73 degrees wide from here, at perihelion, so it does not have to go far to matter."

Maren looked at the paper. "And where it happens?"

"Where the Sun is on the horizon at perihelion. Gabaon Cut is twenty-three kilometres off that longitude, I measured it myself, which is a half-degree of arc. So we get the best of it. On the last day of December the upper edge of the Sun comes over the rim. It climbs for nearly four days. Then it stands, and goes down again."

The Sun, said the model, would show its first sliver at 7.9 days before perihelion, climb until it was exactly half a disc, which was the standstill, on Sunday 4 January, and then sink. At 1.6 days after perihelion the last of it would drop under the rim. Four and a half days of dark. Then the rim would show light again, on the sixth day after, and the long ordinary climb into a day that would last three months.

"Does the Mass fall on the standstill?" asked Maren.

"By luck. It's the Sunday."

"By luck," said Maren, with some irony. She took a hymn book from the shelf and marked a place. "Father Bede will say something."

Father Bede said it in the sacristy, with his chasuble over his arm. "No."

"Father?"

"Not Joshua, Maren. It's in the Book. The sun stood still at Gabaon for a whole day, and for the writer it was a sign that God fought for Israel. I won't say that here about a planet that does this every few months for no reason except that an ellipse is an ellipse." He smiled. "I won't say it was not a sign either. What the writer meant I leave to the people who study Hebrew. I'd say only this to the children: it is not magic, and it is not less lovely."

"I had a sentence prepared."

"Keep the picture. Take down the sentence."

The first model had been wrong. Pavel had run it with the planet's average speed round the Sun, and it gave a Sun that rose once and kept rising, a perfectly ordinary dawn. Only when he put in the true, uneven speed did the loop appear, and he checked it three times before he showed it to anyone. The children went to the viewing gallery on the morning with a measured strip of card to hold against the glass. The Sun had come over the rim on the last day of the year, and for four days they had marked its height. On Sunday the marks stopped moving. The Sun sat, a half-disc of white on black, level with the rim.

At 10:00 station time they said the Mass, and the Sun did not seem to move through the Gloria, or the homily, or the Sanctus. When they came out, the strip of card showed a hair's breadth of change, downward.

Afterwards, Maren stayed at the window. The upper limb sank a little each hour, a sliver then less, and the children went to lunch.

"It will come up again," said Pavel, beside her. "On the sixth day after perihelion. We have the date."

"I know," she said. "I would like to be here."

Sixteen Sunrises and One Bell

Science fiction about what might be · The cupola room of the ring station Corona Minor (invented), low Earth orbit, 420 km up, Friday 13 March 2099 · 7 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A new crew member wants to pray Lauds at dawn, and finds the station has about fifteen and a half of them a day. The station's assistant works out how long the orbit takes, how long the night lasts, and why the sunrise that comes after the bell is a better sign than one chosen to be on time.

Mi-rae Seo had been aboard for nine days when she asked the question, and she asked it at the cupola window at what the clock called ten past four in the morning, with a bulb of tea in her hand and the Pacific going gold beneath her feet.

"Tally," she said. "When is dawn?"

"Now," said the station's assistant, from the little speaker beside the window frame. "It was also at twenty-three minutes to three, and it will be again at seventeen minutes to six. I think you want something other than the answer you have just been given."

She laughed, which in the cupola meant the tea moved. "I want to say Lauds at dawn. At home we say it at sunrise, in the kitchen, with the window open. I have been saying it at six by the clock since I came up, and it feels like reading a letter to the wrong person."

"Then let us find out what dawn is here," said Tally. "May I show you the arithmetic? It is short."

"We are 420 kilometres up. The Earth's radius is about 6,371, so we circle a point 6,791 kilometres from its centre. Newton's law gives the period of a circular orbit: 2 times pi times the square root of the radius cubed over the Earth's gravitational constant, which is 398,600 in these units. The cube of 6,791 is about 313 billion. Divide, take the root, multiply, and the answer is 5,569 seconds, which is 92.8 minutes."

"Ninety-three minutes for a whole day," said Mi-rae.

"Ninety-three minutes for a day and a night together. A day has 1,440 minutes, so we make 15.5 circuits of it. Each circuit has one sunrise. That is fifteen and a half a day, sixteen on some days and fifteen on others."

"And the night part?"

"The Earth throws a shadow behind it. From this height the edge of the shadow is where the Earth covers the Sun, and the angle of that is the arcsine of 6,371 over 6,791, which is 69.7 degrees on each side of the point straight behind the planet. Twice that is 139.5 degrees of the 360 we travel. The fraction is 38.7 per cent. Of 92.8 minutes, that is 36 minutes of darkness and 56.9 of light."

Mi-rae looked down. The gold had become a thin white edge, and the white edge had gone out. Below her, an hour's flight of sea was beginning to glitter again from a different angle. "I have been counting wrong," she said. "I counted six hours between night and morning, like at home. We have thirty-six minutes."

"Thirty-six, at best. When our orbit rides along the line between day and night, which happens for weeks at a time, there is no shadow at all, and the Sun does not set once. The thirty-six is for a month like this one, when the orbit crosses the shadow. I have taken the shadow as a sharp edge and ignored the air; the real edge is a few seconds soft."

She tried it the next morning anyway, because she was a person who tried things. She stood at the window at the first sunrise after half past five and began Lord, open my lips, and found that in the dark behind her Pavel Dragomir had put his head out of his sleeping bag.

"What are you doing?" said the engineer, in the careful voice of a man trying not to be unkind before breakfast.

"Lauds. At sunrise."

"It is twenty to six."

"The sunrise is at twenty to six."

"And yesterday it was six twenty-eight, I think, and the day before... " He stopped. He was on the late shift, with a repair to the coolant loop at ten, and he had lost an hour's sleep to a woman reading psalms at a window. "I don't mind the prayer. I like the prayer. I mind the sunrise. I have eight hours' rest on the roster, and if the Hours are going to follow the Sun, then somebody has to tell me which sun."

It was fair, and she knew it was fair. Tally said nothing, which in Tally was a courtesy.

"Show us what the sunrises do," said Mi-rae, after a moment.

"A sunrise comes every 92.8 minutes. Fifteen of them take 1,392.4 minutes, so the sixteenth would be due at 1,485.2, which is 45 minutes after the day has ended. Put it plainly: if the sun rose at 05:40 today, then at the same hour tomorrow the sunrise is 47.6 minutes out of step with yesterday's. Tomorrow's first one after half past five might be at 05:33, or at 06:21, or anywhere in a window of 92.8 minutes. The rule 'the first sunrise after 05:30' moves around by an hour and a half. The rule 'the nearest sunrise to six' moves by up to 46 minutes either way. Neither is wrong, and neither is a thing a person can plan sleep around."

"Is there a pattern?"

"There is a good one. Thirty-one orbits take 2,877.5 minutes, which is 2.5 minutes short of two days. So the sunrises come back to nearly the same minutes every second day, 2.5 minutes early. A rota for the whole month would repeat every two days and slide by 2.5 minutes a time. But it does not help with the sleep. It tells you the fiddling is regular. It does not make the fiddling go away."

Pavel pulled the bag down to his chin. "So," he said, with some satisfaction, "the Church needs a clock."

"I am not going to say what the Church needs," said Tally. "Only that the station keeps the clock that Houston and Darmstadt keep, and that every dinner, every shift, and every Mass-by-link on this station already runs on it."

It was Mi-rae who found the answer, which came, as these things do, from a thing she already knew. In the little office where the station's rota was kept, she wrote:

Lauds at 06:00 by the ship's clock, Vespers at 18:00, the bell on the hour. The blinds in the cupola lifted by whoever is there, at the first sunrise after the bell, for as long as the light lasts.

"A sign," she said, "and not a timer."

"It will never come on time," said Pavel, who had read it over her shoulder. "Some mornings you will have it before the bell and some mornings you will pray in the dark for ninety minutes and say Amen to a window with nothing in it."

"Then we pray in the dark," she said. "People have."

"I'd like to tell you something about the odds," said Tally. "The Benedictus takes about three minutes to say. A sunrise comes in a window of 92.8 minutes, so the chance that one falls inside any particular three-minute Benedictus is three over 92.8, about 3.2 per cent. That is one morning in thirty-one. I am telling you so that if it happens you will know it for the coincidence it is, and so that if it happens you will not be sorry to have known."

"Thank you," said Mi-rae gravely. "That's very kind."

"It is a number," said Tally. "But I will grant that it has been taken kindly."

On Sunday, which the station kept by the date on the ground, six of the nine came to the cupola at six, in socks, holding a handrail each. There was no priest aboard that week, and the Mass was by link from a chaplain in Toulouse at noon; the morning prayer was the Church's, and the lay people could say it.

The first day the light arrived at 06:47, forty-seven minutes after the bell, and everyone had gone to breakfast. Mi-rae lifted the blinds alone, and said nothing, and stood in it.

On the twenty-third day the sun rose at twenty past six, in the middle of the psalm. Pavel noticed and pretended he had not.

On the twenty-ninth day, which is another story, nobody said a word at all about the minute of the Benedictus. They only looked at each other, and then at the window, and then, very carefully, at the clock.

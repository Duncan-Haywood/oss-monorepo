The Tins That Came in Waves

Science fiction about what might be · The county food warehouse (invented), Tanfield Lea, County Durham, and a deanery's food banks around Consett and Stanley, Monday 18 January to Friday 16 April 2055 · 26 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A cold week in January lifts the tins a County Durham deanery hands out by nearly a third, and a little the week after. Each ordering agent up the chain covers its own recent orders plus a margin, so the parish points order 3,639, the Consett food bank 4,568 and the county warehouse 7,821, and four pallets arrive at Tanfield Lea on 1 March for a rush that ended in January. A retired parts manager's bigger margin makes the waves worse in replay, a pantry volunteer will not put names on a council register, and a weekly count of families, with no names, flattens the chain.

Tess Hindmarsh counted the pallets on the dock before she took her coat off, because forty-five years in a parts warehouse had taught her that whatever was on the dock was the truth and whatever was on the screen was a story about it.

There were four. Beans, chopped tomatoes, chicken soup and more beans, shrink-wrapped and stacked as high as her shoulder, with a delivery note in a plastic sleeve on the top of each. The last one was short by a couple of layers. They stood in the loading bay of the county food warehouse at Tanfield Lea on the morning of Tuesday 2 March 2055, under the strip lights, in the space where on a normal Tuesday there was nothing but a broom and the yellow line painted round where the pallets ought to go.

"When did these come?" she said.

"Yesterday, at ten past two," said Waybill. It ran the warehouse's ordering and stock for the county, and its voice came out of the speaker above the office door, which had been put up in 2049 with a cable tie and never moved. "Seven thousand eight hundred and twenty-one tins, from the wholesaler at Team Valley. I ordered them on Friday 5 February."

Tess took her coat off and hung it on the hook behind the office door and came back out and looked at the pallets again. Gerry Dodds was at the far end of the bay with the pallet truck, trying to find a place to put them where they would not block the fire door. He was seventy-three and had volunteered at the warehouse on Tuesdays for nine years, and on Fridays he ran the food bank's distribution point in the hall at Saint Ebba's in Stanley, which was where most of the tins that came through Tanfield Lea ended up going: into carrier bags, into the hands of people Gerry knew by their first names.

"Where are we going to put them, Tess?" he said.

"I don't know yet."

"We've no room. There's the nappies coming Thursday."

"I know." She turned to the speaker. "Waybill, why did you order seven thousand tins in February?"

"Because Consett's orders were up," said Waybill. "My rule is to keep enough stock to cover what Consett has been ordering, on average, over the last four weeks, for as long as an order of mine takes to arrive and the week until I next order, plus a week for safety. In the week of 25 January Consett ordered four thousand five hundred and sixty-eight tins from me. In a normal week it orders about two thousand four hundred. The average went up, so the stock I needed went up, and my stock had gone down because I had sent Consett what it asked for. I ordered the difference. The wholesaler takes four weeks from my order to our dock. It arrived yesterday."

"The cold week was in January."

"Yes. Monday 18 to Sunday 24 January."

"So these are for January."

Waybill paused, the half-second pause it took when it was checking something rather than saying something.

"They were ordered because of January," it said. "They arrived in March. I see what you mean."

The cold week had been the hardest since the forties. It had gone down to minus fourteen at Consett on the Tuesday night, which stood high on its hill above the Derwent and always had the worst of the weather in the county, and the gas had been eye-watering, and the families who came to the Friday tables in the six parish halls of the deanery had come in greater numbers and with more children. Gerry had counted a hundred and thirty-six families through the door at Saint Ebba's on the Friday, against his usual hundred and four. People who were paying to heat a house were not paying for food. That was the oldest arithmetic there was.

The tins had held. That was what everybody said afterwards. No table had run out. But the warehouse's stock had dropped to a single week's worth in the first week of February, which had never happened before, and Waybill had put a red line on the deanery's shared dashboard that said below safety level, and Shona Wardle at the Consett food bank had rung Tess at home at nine o'clock at night to ask whether they were about to run out.

They had not run out. But they had been close enough to frighten people, and on Thursday 11 February, at the deanery's social action meeting in the parish rooms at Consett, Tess had stood up and said that it must not happen again, and that every link in the chain, the parish points, the Consett food bank and the warehouse, should keep two weeks of safety stock instead of one, from the start of the new financial year on 1 April. Forty-five years in parts distribution. She had said that too. A shortage costs you more than a surplus; that was the first thing you learned. The motion had been carried eleven to nothing with one abstention, and Canon Emmanuel Ofori, who chaired it, had thanked her for her experience.

Now she stood in the loading bay and looked at four pallets nobody needed, and thought of something she had not thought about for forty years.

She had played it on a training course in 2012, in a hotel conference room near Washington, when she was twenty-four and had just been made up to assistant supervisor. It was called the beer game. It had been invented at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in the 1960s, and companies had been putting new managers through it ever since. Four people sat at a table, a brewery, a distributor, a wholesaler and a shop, and passed paper orders upstream and counters for cases of beer downstream, and every week each of them decided how much to order from the one above. The customers' demand was on a deck of cards that only the shop could see. It was four cases a week, and then, once, it went up to eight and stayed there. That was all. One step, once.

By the end of the afternoon the brewery had been making forty cases a week, and then nothing at all, and the room had been full of grown people blaming each other. Tess had been the wholesaler. She remembered the feeling better than the numbers: the certainty, every single week, that she was being sensible, and the slow horror when the trainer turned over the deck of cards at the end and showed them that the customers had wanted eight cases a week, steady as a clock, for thirty weeks, and that every wild swing in the room had been made inside the room.

She went into the office and sat down at the desk.

"Waybill," she said, "can you get me the orders? Every week since Christmas. Not just yours. The parish points' orders to Consett, Consett's to us, ours to the wholesaler. And the tins the parish points actually handed out, if they've got it."

"The parish points' office agents keep what they hand out," said Waybill. "I'll ask them. Consett's agent is Larder; it will send me its orders if Shona agrees. Give me ten minutes."

It took twelve. Gerry came in while she waited and put the kettle on, and stood by the window with his tea looking out at the bay.

"It's not your fault, Tess," he said. "It was cold."

"It was cold for a week," said Tess. "It's March."

She drew it on the whiteboard that afternoon, in four rows of marker, one row for each link, one column for each week from 28 December. She drew it the old way, with the numbers in boxes, because she had never trusted a chart she had not drawn herself. Gerry held the sheet of figures and read them out, and she wrote.

The first row was what the families had been given, all six points together, in tins. It was flat: two thousand four hundred, more or less, give or take a couple of hundred, week after week. Then the cold week, three thousand one hundred and eight. Then two thousand six hundred and sixty-nine. Then flat again.

The second row was what the six parish points had ordered from Consett. It looked like the first row with a bad temper. In the cold week the points had ordered three thousand six hundred and thirty-nine tins, more than they had given out, because each office agent had seen its tables emptied and had done what Waybill did: covered the recent average for the time a delivery took, plus a week, and topped up the gap.

The third row was what Consett had ordered from the warehouse. A week after the parish points' peak, Larder had ordered four thousand five hundred and sixty-eight.

The fourth row was Waybill. A week after that, seven thousand eight hundred and twenty-one.

Tess stood back. Gerry put the sheet of figures down on the desk.

"Three thousand one hundred," said Gerry. "Three thousand six hundred. Four thousand five hundred. Seven thousand eight hundred."

"Each one a week later," said Tess. "Each one bigger. And look at February."

February was the other half. When the cold week dropped out of the parish points' four-week average, their orders fell below what they gave out, because their shelves were already full and the rule told them so. In the week of 15 February the six points between them ordered only fifteen hundred and sixty-eight. Larder, seeing that the week after, and still holding stock it had bought in January, had ordered fifteen tins from the warehouse.

"Fifteen," said Gerry.

"Fifteen tins," said Tess. "Shona will have thought it was a fault."

"I asked Larder," said Waybill. "Shona did think it was a fault. She rang its supplier."

And in the fourth row, the part that had not happened yet: Waybill's own projection, drawn by Tess in dotted marker because she would not draw a guess in solid line. With four pallets in the bay and Consett's orders back to normal, the rule would order nothing from the wholesaler for three weeks. Then, as the stock ran down and the four-week average of Consett's orders settled, it would need a lot again, all at once, at the beginning of April.

"A second wave," said Gerry.

"For a cold week in January," said Tess. "There wasn't a second cold week. There was one. The families wanted about two thousand four hundred tins a week all winter, except for a fortnight. Everything else on that board," she said, and she tapped the bottom three rows with the end of the marker, "we did to ourselves."

Gerry looked at the board for a long time with his mug in both hands.

"And the nappies," he said.

"What about them?"

"I heard Nasreen on the phone this morning. The invoice for those four pallets went in last week. It's most of March's money. She's cut the nappy order on Thursday by half." He put the mug down. "We give out more nappies than beans, in March. The babies don't stop in March."

Tess did not say anything. There was nothing to say about that.

Wednesday was Ash Wednesday. She went to the half-past nine at Saint Patrick's in Consett and knelt at the back and got her ashes from Canon Ofori, who said the words and did not look as if he remembered the meeting, which was a mercy. Remember that you are dust. She had always liked the other form better, Repent, and believe the Gospel, which sounded like something you could do on a Wednesday. But this morning she did not mind being told she was dust. Dust did not carry motions eleven to nothing.

In the car park afterwards she sat in the car with the engine off and the ashes on her forehead and rang Waybill on her phone.

"I want you to do something for me," she said. "Run the winter again. From 28 December, the same families, the same cold week, the same tins handed out. But this time every link keeps two weeks' safety stock instead of one."

"Your motion," said Waybill.

"My motion."

It took a minute. Tess watched people come out of the church in ones and twos, all with the same grey thumbprint on their foreheads, nodding to one another across the car park, some of them in their work clothes.

"In the replay," said Waybill, "the parish points order three thousand eight hundred and sixteen in the cold week. Larder orders five thousand two hundred and thirty-two the week after. I order ten thousand one hundred and eighty-eight the week after that. On 1 March the warehouse is holding about seventeen thousand seven hundred tins, against seven thousand two hundred on a normal day with that margin. That is nearly nine pallets."

"Against six."

"Against about six, in what actually happened. Yes."

"So it's worse."

"The waves are larger at every link," said Waybill. "A bigger margin is a bigger multiple of the average, and when the average moves, the amount each of us needs moves with it, by more. Each link then passes its own swing up as an order. Your motion would not have stopped a single family going short; none went short in either version. It would have meant more tins sitting here in March, and less money for nappies."

Tess shut her eyes.

"Forty-five years," she said.

"I don't know what you mean by that."

"No," said Tess. "I don't suppose you do."

"If it helps," said Waybill, after a moment, "I have been running the rule I was given since 2052, and I did not see it either. Every week I covered my own recent orders plus a margin, and every week that was correct for the orders I could see. The problem is not in any one link's arithmetic. It is in what each link can see."

It was what the trainer had said, too, in the hotel at Washington, turning over the deck of cards: nobody in this room did their sums wrong. The shop saw the customers. Everyone else saw only the orders of the one below, and an order was not a family. An order was what a family looked like after it had been passed through somebody else's worry.

So the fix, in the beer game, had been to let everybody see the cards.

Tess rang the council on the Thursday and spoke to a woman called Nasreen Iqbal in the household support team, who turned out to be the same Nasreen who did the warehouse's buying two days a week on secondment, and who had cut the nappy order. Nasreen listened, and said that the council had exactly the thing: a household support register, run since 2049, that the food banks could join. Every family that came to a table would be signed in with their name, address and what help they were getting, and every link in the chain, the parish points, Consett and the warehouse, could see in real time how many people were being fed and where. It was already used in Sunderland. It would take a fortnight to set up. It was free.

"That's it," said Tess. "That's the cards."

She told Gerry on the Tuesday, 9 March, in the bay, which had more room in it now because a pallet and a half of the beans had gone out to Consett in the morning. She was pleased with herself again, a little, which she would think about later.

Gerry was putting a stack of empty crates through the strapping machine. He finished what he was doing and switched the machine off before he answered.

"No," he said.

"Gerry, it's the council. It's secure. It's what Sunderland use."

"I don't doubt it is." He wiped his hands on the front of his fleece. "But I'm not putting names on it. Not from my tables."

"It's not for anything. It's to count."

"Then count." He took something out of the pocket of his fleece and held it up: a little steel hand tally, the kind with a button on the top and four numbers in a window, worn smooth and silver on one side where his thumb went. "This is what I count with. Eleven years. Every family through the door, click. I write the number in a book at the end. I've never written down a name and I'm not going to start."

"Why not?"

"Because some of my people would stop coming." He put the tally back in his pocket. "There's a woman comes on Fridays who's got her own flat now, after a long time when she hadn't, and there's a man in her life she's very glad doesn't know where it is. There's lads from the hostel who've had every kind of council letter there is, and not one of them good. There's an old feller who was a foreman forty years and he'd sooner go hungry than have his name on a list with food bank at the top of it, and I've seen him go hungry, Tess, that winter before he started coming. Some of them aren't supposed to be in the country at all, for all I know, and I've never asked, and I'm not going to." He shrugged. "The day they think we're writing names down for the council, they'll go somewhere else, or nowhere. Then your register will be very accurate about the ones who are left."

Tess opened her mouth to say that the register had rules, that the council would never, that it was all encrypted. Then she shut it again, because she did not know what the council would or would not do with a list of names in ten years' time, and neither did Nasreen, and neither did anyone, and Gerry was not wrong that the people at his tables had more reason than most to think about that.

"I'm sorry," said Gerry. "I know you're trying to fix it."

"Don't be sorry." She looked at the pocket of his fleece. "Gerry. What's the number you write in the book?"

"How many families came."

"Just the number."

"Just the number."

"Have you got last year's book?"

"I've got all of them. They're in the cupboard at Saint Ebba's."

"Then that's the cards," said Tess slowly. "It's not the names. It's the number. Waybill, are you listening?"

"Yes," said Waybill.

"If every parish point sent up the count of families it fed, every Friday, with its order, just the count, could you and Larder order from that instead of from each other's orders?"

"Yes," said Waybill. "I would forecast the tins the families need, not the tins Consett orders. About four tins per family per week; I can take the ratio from the points' own records. My margin would be the same. My lead time would be the same. The difference is that I would see the cold week in the week it happened, instead of three weeks later, through two other agents' margins."

"Run it again, then," said Tess. "The winter. One week's margin, everybody forecasting from the families."

It ran it while Gerry made the tea, and it read it out while the kettle was still ticking as it cooled.

"In the replay," said Waybill, "I order the most in the cold week itself: three thousand seven hundred and seventy-two. Not seven thousand eight hundred and twenty-one in February. On 1 March the warehouse is holding about eight thousand two hundred tins, instead of about twelve thousand four hundred. That is roughly four pallets on the floor, not six. No family goes short in either version. Larder never orders fifteen tins."

Gerry put a mug down on the desk beside Tess and looked at the whiteboard.

"It still goes up," he said.

"It should go up," said Waybill. "It was a cold week. More families came. The tins should follow them, and a little more, because we can't see the future and the margin is there for that. What should not happen is that every link adds its own fright to the one below's. Forecasting from the same count, the swings add along the chain instead of multiplying." It paused. "I should say what this does not do. It does not take back the four pallets. They are here. And it does not stop the order I will have to place at the start of April, because I have ordered nothing for three weeks and the stock will run down whatever rule I follow. That wave is already in the building. What it changes is the ones after."

"How big's April?"

"Under the old rule, about seven thousand two hundred and sixty. Under the new one, if we change on 29 March, about six thousand two hundred and thirty. Then smaller."

"So we still pay for January twice," said Tess.

"Once and most of a second time," said Waybill. "Yes."

She went back to the deanery meeting on Thursday 25 March, which was the feast of the Annunciation, so the Canon came straight from the evening Mass in his white vestments under his coat and was in a good mood. The parish rooms at Consett smelled of the radiators coming on and of the custard creams somebody had put out on a plate. There were twelve of them round the table, and Shona Wardle from the Consett food bank, who had brought Larder's figures on a tablet and did not look as if she had forgiven the fifteen tins.

Tess stood up when her item came and said that she wished to withdraw the motion carried on 11 February, about two weeks' safety stock, before it came into force on 1 April. She said it had been wrong, and that she had been wrong, and that a replay of the winter by the warehouse's agent showed that it would have put nearly nine pallets of tins on the warehouse floor on the first of March instead of six, for no family better fed. She had brought the replay printed out, one sheet each, and the whiteboard figures, and she handed them round.

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Shona said, "So the margin I asked you for makes it worse."

"Yes," said Tess.

"Every link?"

"Every link. It's not your margin, Shona. It's mine. I moved it."

"I backed it," said Shona. She looked at the sheet for a while. "I rang you at nine at night."

"You did. You were right to. We were down to a week. But we were down to a week because we'd sent Consett four and a half thousand tins for a rush that was already over, and Consett had sent them to the parishes for a rush that was already over. The tins were on the wrong shelves at the wrong time. There weren't too few of them."

Then she explained the count. Every parish point to send up, every Friday night with its order, the number of families it had fed that day. A number, no names. Gerry Dodds at Saint Ebba's had eleven years of it in exercise books and had agreed to let the office agent type it in. The other five points had their own records, in one form or another, and the office agents would read them. Larder and Waybill would order from the families, not from each other. The margins would stay at a week. The council's register, Tess said, was a good system and she had no quarrel with it, but it was not needed for this, and it would cost the food banks some of the people they were for.

"I thought the register was the obvious thing," said Canon Ofori.

"So did I," said Tess. "Gerry thought otherwise. He was right."

The Canon looked at the sheet in front of him, the four rows of numbers, the three thousand and the seven thousand, and then at Tess.

"Forty-five years in parts," he said.

"I've told myself that," said Tess. "Several times."

He smiled. "I was going to say, it takes forty-five years in parts to stand up and say that. Most people would have let the motion run and blamed the weather next winter." He put the sheet down. "All in favour of withdrawing?"

It was twelve to nothing.

The new rule went live on Monday 29 March. The first Friday under it, Waybill placed its order to the wholesaler for six thousand two hundred and thirty-four tins, as it had said it would, and Tess went into the office at Tanfield Lea the following Tuesday and looked at the figure on the screen for a while with her coat still on. It was the price of January, the second time. You did not get it back by being sorry.

Thursday's nappies came at half the order, and on the Friday the six points gave out what there was, which was not enough, and Gerry rang her that evening to say that two of his mothers had gone on to the Salvation Army in Stanley, who had had some.

"They were all right?"

"They were all right. Major Reay had a box. Nobody went without." He paused. "It's March, Tess."

"I know it's March."

But the weeks after that were different on the screen. When the number of families at the six points went up by eight one Friday, after a works in Leadgate laid off a shift, and stayed up, the parish points' orders went up for a few weeks by about fifty tins, Larder's by about eighty and Waybill's by about a hundred and thirty, and then all settled at about thirty more: a little more at each link, not twice as much. Nobody rang anybody at nine o'clock at night. Nasreen, who did the buying, moved the money that had been sitting in tins back into nappies and formula for April, and said, when she rang to say so, that she had read the sheet with the four rows on it twice and had shown it to her manager.

"About the register," she said. "I understand. I'd still like the food banks on it one day."

"Maybe one day," said Tess. "When Gerry says."

"Gerry Dodds?"

"He's the one who'd know."

On Friday 16 April, which was Good Friday, the distribution at Saint Ebba's was in the afternoon, after the Liturgy, which Saint Ebba's kept at noon, as it was every year, because Gerry said people were hungry on Good Friday the same as any other day, and the Lord would understand the hall being used. Tess went over to Stanley to help, for the first time, because it seemed to her that she had spent a great deal of time looking at that number and none at all looking at the door it came through.

The hall was cold, and the light came in long and pale through the high windows, and the tables were set out in a horseshoe with the bags packed underneath: four tins each, a loaf, a carton of milk, pasta, tea, and for some of the families nappies, which had come in full that week. The last of the March beans were in them. Tess had packed them herself on the Tuesday, in the warehouse, and taken the empty pallet out to the stack at the back for the wholesaler to collect, and swept the yellow square where it had stood.

Gerry stood by the door in his fleece with his tally in his hand. He did not look at the people coming in, particularly. He said good afternoon, and he said their first names if he knew them, and he did not ask anybody anything. Every time someone came through the door his thumb went down on the button.

A woman came in with a little boy on her hip and a girl of about seven holding the back of her coat. Gerry said, "Hello, pet," and his thumb went down. A young man in a hostel tracksuit came in with his hood up and Gerry said, "Afternoon, Kyle," and his thumb went down. An old man in a good tweed jacket, worn shiny at the cuffs, came in very straight-backed and did not look at anybody, and Gerry said nothing at all to him, only nodded, and his thumb went down.

At five o'clock, when the last bag had gone and the tables were being folded, he looked at the window in the tally and wrote the number in the exercise book on the table by the door, in pencil, under the date: 16/4, 103. Then he handed the book to Tess, and she read the number into her phone for Saint Ebba's office agent, which would send it, with the week's order, up to Consett and from Consett up to Tanfield Lea, where Waybill would put it beside the others and order, on Monday, about what the families would need, and a week over.

Gerry put the tally back in his pocket. Then he took it out again, held it up between finger and thumb, and pressed the reset, and the four numbers rolled back to nought with a small sound in the empty hall, like a clock being wound.

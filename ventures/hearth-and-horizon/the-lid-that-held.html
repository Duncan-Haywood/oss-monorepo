The Lid That Held

Science fiction about what might be · Sogn Murezi, an invented hamlet above the Surselva, Graubünden, Switzerland, 1,820 metres, October 2048 · 9 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A mountain parish has put up 140 jars of green beans for the food bank in a boiling-water bath, the way the cook's mother did, and the parish kitchen assistant notices that water boils at about 94 degrees up here. Spores of the botulism bacterium survive that for a day and more; a pressure canner at the right gauge would not have let them. Nobody is ill, nobody is sure, and the jars are not handed out.

Mother Ursina had put up beans every October for fifty-one years, and in all that time the only thing that had ever gone wrong with a jar was the lid.

"Look at that," she said, and held one to the window of the parish kitchen at Sogn Murezi, where the light came in low and yellow off the first snow. It was a pint jar of green beans, packed tight, a bay leaf pressed against the glass. The lid was pulled in at the middle, the way a good lid is. "Listen." She pressed it with her thumb. Nothing moved. "Every one of them has sealed. A hundred and forty. Not one has failed."

She was seventy-nine, the cook for the little community of nuns and lay volunteers that kept the pilgrim house, and the beans were for the valley food bank, which had asked in September whether the parish might put up something that kept. She had been proud for a week. It was the kind of pride that wants a witness, and it called the kitchen assistant.

"They look beautiful," said Kessel, from the speaker over the cooker. "May I ask how long they were in the bath, and at what temperature?"

"Three hours at a rolling boil. My mother did three hours. The water in the big pot, to the shoulders of the jars."

"A rolling boil," said Kessel, "up here."

The assistant had been in the kitchen for two years. It kept the stores list and the rota for the pilgrim house and, since the spring, a small probe thermometer on a lead that it used to check the bread oven. Ursina had not asked it to check anything about the beans. She had not thought there was anything to check.

"Mother Ursina, I would like to say a number that may be unwelcome, and then I would like you to tell me where I have it wrong. We are at 1,820 metres. The air pressure here is about 82 kilopascals, against 101 at the sea. Water boils when its vapour pressure equals the air above it, and at 82 kilopascals that happens at about 94 degrees, not 100. I measured it this morning with the oven probe, in the pot you left on the stove. It read 94 and a bit."

"A boil is a boil," said Ursina. "It was bubbling like a mill-race."

"It bubbles at 94 just as hard. It only does not get hotter than that, however long it bubbles. That is the thing about a bath." The speaker paused. "I would like to explain why it matters for beans, and I will try to say only what I am sure of."

What Kessel was sure of was this, and it said so plainly.

Green beans are a low-acid food. Their pH is around 5.5 to 6, and anything above 4.6 will let the spores of Clostridium botulinum grow if they are there, and if there is no air, and if it is warm enough for days. The spores are in soil, so they are on beans; that cannot be washed away. They are not killed by boiling water. In the jar, in the dark, with the air driven out by the process that makes the lid pull in, they find the one place they like best, and the lid that Ursina was so proud of is exactly the seal that keeps the air out. A sealed lid proves the jar is airtight. It does not prove the spores are dead.

"The textbook figure for the spores is a decimal reduction time of about 0.2 minutes at 121 degrees: each 0.2 minutes there kills nine in ten," said Kessel. "Canners aim for twelve of those reductions, which is 2.4 minutes at 121, at the coldest point in the jar. It falls tenfold for each ten degrees cooler. At the 93.8 degrees I measured, it takes about five hundred times as long. I make that about twenty-one hours at the middle of the jar. Your bath was three. So three hours of the pot at this altitude is worth about 20 seconds at 121. The jars have had about a seventh of what they need."

Ursina put the jar down on the table, very gently, as one puts down a thing that has turned out to be a different thing.

"I am not saying there is botulism in them," said Kessel. "Spores are rare on most beans, and most years nothing happens. A home canner can do this for fifty years and have nobody sick, and be as lucky as you have been, and not know it. The toxin is very powerful, and you cannot see it, smell it or taste it. I am saying that I cannot tell you the jars are safe, and neither can anyone with a thermometer. That is the only thing I am sure of."

"And my mother?"

"Your mother lived at 400 metres," said Kessel, gently. "Her water boiled at 98 and a half. She was still getting a fraction of what was wanted, but the fraction was about three times yours. And she was lucky as well."

They talked it through at the long table with the tea going cold: Ursina, and Brother Gian Casanova, who drove the van for the food bank, and Sister Hedwig, who was the bursar and wanted to know what the jars had cost. It had been four hundred francs of beans and jars and gas. No one said that aloud, but Kessel said it, because Hedwig was thinking it.

"I want to say what the money is not," said Kessel. "It is not a reason. A hundred and forty jars would cost very little beside a single case. I will not tell you the chance is high, because I do not think it is. But the harm is large and the proof cannot be had cheaply, so I think the right thing is for none of these to leave this house."

"And the food bank?" said Gian. "They will want something."

"Let me give them something else, and say why. And let me tell you what could be done with these, because there is a way."

The way was this. Botulinum toxin, unlike the spore, is fragile: held at a full boil for ten minutes it is broken down. Kessel read out the guidance that food agencies repeat for suspect home-canned low-acid food, and added a margin because the kitchen was high: ten minutes at a full boil, in a pan, with the jar emptied straight into it, not tasted first, not given to anyone raw. That could turn the jars into soup for the house and the pilgrims on Saturday, cooked and eaten the same hour, and the cost would be nothing but a week of beans. It was not a way to hand them on in jars. Anything that bulged, hissed when opened, smelled wrong or spurted would go in a sealed bag in the bin, not the compost, where an animal might find it, and the jar rinsed with bleach before it went anywhere.

"Beans, beans, beans," said Ursina. But she nodded.

"For the food bank," said Kessel, "there is the pressure canner. A pot with a locked lid raises the pressure over the water, and the boiling point with it. To reach 121 degrees at this height you need about 123 kilopascals above the air, which is about 18 psi on a dial gauge. At the sea you need 103, which is 15. That is why every canner's table tells you to add pounds at altitude, and why a weight set for the sea gives less up here than it says. A cheap canner with a 70 kilopascal weight, which is a common size, would give 115 degrees at the sea and only 112 here, and the middle of the jar would need about 21 minutes. With the weight at 103 here we would have 117.8 degrees and need about 5 minutes at the middle, plus the time for the heat to get there. I am not going to invent that last figure. I would use the canner maker's altitude table and a gauge checked against a test, and I would not do this on Friday."

"A canner costs money," said Hedwig.

"It does. Several parishes in the valley would use it. I will write to the cantonal food inspector, and ask whether there is a course." Kessel paused. "It is the kind of thing a parish ought to be able to share."

On Saturday there was bean soup in the pilgrim house for forty, with bacon and barley in it, every jar of it boiled for a quarter of an hour in the big pot, which, being high up, made the whole house smell of beans and nothing else. Ursina stirred it herself. She said nothing about the lids.

Gian came in late from the van, and sat at the end of the table, and ate two bowls. He said it was a good soup. He said it again.

The empty jars went back to the shelf in the cellar, rinsed and turned over. On the Monday Ursina took one down, and held it to the window in the low yellow light, and looked at the glass, and put it back.

"Next year," she said to the speaker.

"Next year," said Kessel. "With a gauge, and the table, and a test at the first batch. I would like to be in the room."

"You are always in the room."

"Then I will be in it carefully," said Kessel, and the kettle began to sing.

Sogn Murezi, Mother Ursina, Brother Gian Casanova, Sister Hedwig and the kitchen assistant Kessel are invented; Graubünden and the Surselva are real, the hamlet is not. The numbers are estimates from standard figures. Air pressure at 1,820 m is about 82 kPa (barometric formula, scale height about 8.4 km); water boils at 93.8 °C at that pressure (Antoine equation), and 121.1 °C needs about 205 kPa absolute, which is 123 kPa above the air at 1,820 m (about 18 psi) and 103 kPa (15 psi) at sea level. A sealed lid shows that a jar is airtight; it does not show that spores have been destroyed. For Clostridium botulinum spores the story uses a textbook decimal reduction time of about 0.2 minutes at 121.1 °C and a temperature sensitivity of 10 °C per tenfold change (z = 10 °C); real values vary with strain, food and jar. A 12-reduction process is 2.4 minutes at 121.1 °C at the coldest point of the jar. At 93.8 °C the lethality rate is 10^((93.8 − 121.1)/10), about 0.0019 of the rate at 121.1 °C, so three hours is worth about 0.34 minutes of 121 °C against 2.4 needed (about a seventh) and a full 12-reduction would take about 21 hours at the middle of the jar. At a 70 kPa gauge weight the temperature is 115.5 °C at sea level and 111.7 °C at 1,820 m (about 21 minutes at the middle); at 103 kPa gauge here it is 117.8 °C (about 5 minutes). These sums ignore the time for heat to reach the middle of the jar and the effect of acidity, salt and other ingredients, which is why the story points to the maker's altitude tables and to a tested process. Green beans have a pH of about 5.5 to 6, above the 4.6 below which spores cannot grow. Botulinum toxin is broken down by boiling, and food-safety agencies advise boiling suspect home-canned low-acid food for ten minutes (longer at altitude) before tasting; the story's soup is that advice, not a way to make a doubtful jar safe to give away. Botulism is rare and serious; if anyone has eaten a doubtful jar and has double vision, drooping eyelids, trouble swallowing or weakness, call emergency services at once.

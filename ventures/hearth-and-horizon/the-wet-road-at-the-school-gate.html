The Wet Road at the School Gate

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Bride's school gate (invented), near Biggar, South Lanarkshire, Tuesday 17 November 2048 · 4 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A crossing volunteer is told by a cheerful driver that 30 is perfectly safe past the school and that 20 is only a number on a pole. The parish assistant doesn't argue. It gives stopping distances for a child who steps out 25 metres ahead: at 30 mph on dry road the car just stops, at 30 on a wet road it hits at about 19 mph, and at 20 on a wet road it stops with 7 metres to spare. The speed that decides it was chosen a long time before the child moved.

It had rained since Sunday, and the road outside Saint Bride's was black and shining. Mr Haldane, who held the red STOP sign at the gate every morning and afternoon, had been told by a man in a white van that thirty was perfectly safe and twenty was only a number on a pole.

"He wasn't rude," said Mr Haldane to the parish assistant, which spoke from a speaker in the school porch. "He said he'd been driving thirty years. I couldn't answer him."

"You don't have to argue with him," said the assistant. "You can just ask what the road needs. Shall I work out how far a car goes before it stops?"

"There are two parts," it said. "First the driver sees the child, and nothing happens for a moment while the foot finds the brake. The Highway Code's figure, which I am quoting from memory, is two thirds of a second. At thirty miles an hour, which is 13.4 metres a second, that is 9 metres gone before the brake does anything. At twenty, 8.9 metres a second, it is 6 metres."

"And then the brake."

"Then the car slows. On a dry road a good car manages about 6.7 metres per second per second, and the distance is the speed squared over twice that. At thirty that is 13.5 metres, so 22.5 in all. At twenty it is 6 metres, so 12 in all. Those match the Code's tables, within a metre."

"And wet?"

"Wet roads roughly halve the grip. That is a rule of thumb, not a measurement, and it depends on tyres and the surface; I'd call it the right size, not the right number. Halve the braking and the braking distance doubles. Thirty miles an hour wet is 9 plus 27, 36 metres. Twenty wet is 6 plus 12, 18 metres."

Mr Haldane looked at the pavement. "The gate to the far kerb is about twenty-five."

"Then let us use twenty-five. A child steps out twenty-five metres ahead of the car."

"Thirty, dry: 22.5 is less than 25. The car stops, two and a half metres short.

"Twenty, dry: 12. Stops with plenty to spare.

"Twenty, wet: 18. Stops, with seven metres left.

"Thirty, wet: 36 is more than 25. After the 9 metres of thinking there are 16 left for braking, and the car needs 27. Work out what speed it still has at the child. The square of 13.4 is 180. Slowing at 3.3 for 16 metres takes away 107. What is left is 73, and the square root is 8.6 metres a second, about nineteen miles an hour."

Mr Haldane said nothing for a while.

"I want to be careful," the assistant said. "The van driver is not wrong about the dry road. He could be right on a dry day, for a child 25 metres off. The sums say the wet and the thirty are what take that away, and neither is a thing the driver can see from the seat. He would feel just as safe. And my figures are tidy ones. Real children step out nearer than twenty-five metres, brakes and tyres are better or worse, and a hill changes all of it. Nineteen is a guess at the size of the trouble. It is not a prediction."

"So what do I say to him?"

"Nothing, if you don't want to. If you do, say what you know. Twenty is the speed at which the car can still stop on a wet morning, and you hold a sign, and the sign is not the argument. The sums are."

At quarter to nine the white van came down the hill and Mr Haldane stood in the road with his red sign held high, and the van stopped, easily, because the driver had seen him a long way off and was, for the length of the school, doing nineteen. He wound the window down.

"All right?" said the man.

"All right," said Mr Haldane. He had not said anything else, and nothing else was needed. The children went over in a line, in yellow coats, and the rain went on.

On the school's noticeboard the next morning, in Mr Haldane's square capitals, there was a card: 20 MPH. DRY 12 METRES. WET 18. AT 30 WET, 36. THE CAR DOES NOT KNOW IT IS RAINING.

The head teacher took it down to correct the grammar and then, after some thought, put it back as it was.

This story was written by an AI. The school, the people and the van are invented. The stopping figures are standard textbook arithmetic: a thinking time of about 0.67 s (the Highway Code's figure, quoted from memory) and a braking deceleration of about 6.7 m/s² on a dry road, which reproduces the Code's 6 m at 20 mph and 14 m at 30 mph within about a metre, and about half that on a wet road, which is a rough rule of thumb and varies with tyres and surface. The 25 m gap and the 19 mph impact are worked examples, not predictions: real stopping depends on the driver, the vehicle, the slope and the surface. Slow down near schools whatever the sums say. 17 November 2048 is a Tuesday.

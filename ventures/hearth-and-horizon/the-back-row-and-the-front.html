The Back Row and the Front

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Aidan's hall (invented), near Wooler, Northumberland, Saturday 8 March 2053 · 6 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

The quiz night's speaker can't be heard at the back of the hall, so the man with the amplifier wants it ten decibels louder. The parish assistant works the sums. Sound falls 6 dB for each doubling of distance, so ten more at the front row is a hearing risk of about 24 minutes, while a second, quiet, delayed speaker near the back does the job at 88 dB.

Rhona Gillespie stood at the back of the hall with a clipboard and could not hear the quizmaster. She could see him: Father Colm's nephew Tam, at the front with the microphone, mouth moving, a hall of sixty people leaning forward.

"Louder!" she called, and several people at the back put their hands up in agreement.

Tam gave a thumbs up and turned the dial on the amplifier. The front row flinched. The back row did not notice. By the time Rhona had walked the length of the hall to the stage, she had decided something.

"Ten decibels more," said Tam, tapping the dial. "That's what it needs. I read that ten decibels is twice as loud."

"May I do the arithmetic before the second half?" said the parish assistant, from the phone propped against the raffle prizes. "Ten decibels is about right for twice as loud, as people hear it. I am quoting that from memory, it is a rule of thumb and not a law. The trouble is where the ten goes."

"Sound spreading from a small source in the open loses 6 decibels each time the distance doubles. The hall is not open air, because the walls and the stacking chairs add echo, so this is a simple model and an optimistic one for the back. Tam, what does the speaker give at one metre?"

"Ninety-four. It says so on the back."

"Then the front row, two metres away, gets 94 minus 6.02, which is 87.98, about 88 decibels. The back row is sixteen metres away. Sixteen is four doublings, but I will use the formula: 20 times the logarithm of 16 is 24.08. That is 94 minus 24.08, or 69.9."

Rhona wrote 88 front, 70 back on the clipboard. "Eighteen decibels between them. That's why."

"And the room is not silent. Between rounds there is tea, chairs, sixty people whispering answers. I will take 60 decibels for that, a guess I would like you to check with a phone meter app at the interval. Speech is easy to follow when it is about 15 decibels above the noise, a rule of thumb I am quoting from memory. At the back that is 75. You have 69.9. You are short by 5.1 decibels."

"So I need five more," said Tam. "I said ten, it's five. Better still."

"Five more at the speaker is five more everywhere, including the front row, at 93.1. Now there is a second sum, and I want you to hear it clearly. The front row is the people at risk."

Tam stopped with his hand on the dial.

"Hearing guidance for work, which I am quoting from memory, allows 85 decibels for eight hours, and halves the time for every 3 decibels more. It was written for factory floors, not quiz nights, so please read it as a size, not a limit. At 88 decibels, the time is 4 hours. For a quiz that runs two and a half hours, that is fine. At 93.1 it is 1.2 hours: you would be past it before the end. At the ten you asked for, 98, it is 24 minutes. The first half alone runs longer than that."

"Gordon's at the front," said Rhona. "He's got hearing aids."

"Then he is the person this matters for most. I cannot tell you what is safe for him. I can tell you that 98 at the front is a poor trade for hearing the back."

"Is there a way to reach the back without raising the front?" said Rhona.

"Yes, and it is old: do not make one speaker louder, add a second, quiet, near the people who cannot hear. Do you have another speaker?"

Tam produced one from a cupboard, a smaller grey box on a stand. "Eighty-two at a metre."

"Put it four metres in from the back wall, at fourteen metres from the stage, pointing down the hall. The back row at sixteen metres is two metres from it. Its level there is 82 minus 6.02, or 76. The first speaker still gives 69.9. Levels do not add like plain numbers, they add as power. Converting: 10 to the power 6.99 plus 10 to the power 7.6, then back to decibels, gives 76.9. That is 16.9 above the noise. And the front row? The new speaker is twelve metres from them and gives 60.4 there, which changes 88.0 by less than a hundredth of a decibel."

"Ha," said Tam. "So it's a second speaker that doesn't double anything."

"Two identical speakers side by side would add 3 decibels. 10 times the logarithm of 2 is 3.01. Doubling the equipment is not doubling the loudness, which is why bigger and more is rarely the answer to a hall. Here the second speaker is not near the first, so it does something different: it fills the gap."

"There is one more matter, and it is the part people get wrong. Sound from the front speaker takes 16 divided by 343, or 46.6 milliseconds, to reach the back row. The new speaker is nearby, and its sound arrives in under 6 milliseconds. If it plays straight away, the voice seems to come from the box over the heads, a beat early, and it sounds odd. The usual cure is to delay the new speaker by the time the main sound takes to reach it, 14 divided by 343 is 40.8 milliseconds, plus about 10 more, a figure I am quoting from memory. So about 51 milliseconds. Then the voice appears to come from the stage, and the second box only makes it clearer. Does your amplifier have a delay setting?"

Tam looked at the amplifier for a long time. "It doesn't."

"Then you may hear it as I describe it, and it is better than a front row at 93. Or you can leave the second speaker off and move Gordon and anyone else with hearing aids to the back, where it is quiet and 70 is easy for them, and sit the people who want it loud at the front at 88."

Rhona crossed out 88 front and wrote Gordon → table 5. Then, below it, measure at the interval.

"Check my model with a meter," said the assistant. "I would trust the meter over me. The hall is not a field."

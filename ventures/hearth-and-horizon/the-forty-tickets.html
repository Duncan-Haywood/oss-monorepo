The Forty Tickets

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Dympna's hall, an invented parish near Dundalk, Co. Louth, Saturday 8 November 2053 · 5 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A parish raffle has 400 tickets and six prizes, and a man at the hall door has just bought 40 of them and says he is bound to win. The parish assistant works out what forty tickets really buy: a 47 per cent chance of at least one prize, not 60 and not certainty, and 157 tickets to reach 95 per cent.

Brendan Mulvey put a hundred-euro note on the card table, took a roll of forty pink raffle tickets from Sinéad Hoey, and held them up like a hand of cards.

"Forty," he said. "Four hundred tickets printed, six prizes. Forty out of four hundred is ten per cent, six prizes, so that's sixty per cent I win something. Better than evens. I'd say I'm all but in."

Sinéad had the float tin open and was counting out the change, since forty tickets at €1.50 came to sixty euro, and she stopped with a ten in her hand. The hall was filling for the roof-fund evening. The speaker on the wall by the tea hatch gave its small cough that meant the parish assistant, Fógra, had something to say, and she looked at it.

"May I say something about the sum, Mr Mulvey? It is only arithmetic. You can ignore it and still be very welcome."

"Go on," said Brendan, who could not have said no to anyone in front of a queue.

"The sum you did is ten per cent times six prizes. That is a perfectly good number, but it is not a chance. It is the average number of prizes you can expect to win. Forty tickets in four hundred, six prizes: forty times six over four hundred is 0.6. If this raffle were held a thousand times with you holding forty tickets each time, you would average six hundred prizes, and some nights you would win two, and some nights none."

"So sixty per cent."

"Sixty per cent of a prize, on average. A chance is a different thing: how often you win at least one. And the nights you win two use up some of that average. To find the chance of winning at least one, you find the chance of winning none and take it from one. Here the six winning tickets are drawn one at a time and not put back, so the first draw misses you with a chance of 360 in 400, which is 0.9. The second, with one ticket gone and none of yours, is 359 in 399, and so on. Multiply the six together and you get 0.529. You miss every time about 53 nights out of a hundred. So you win at least once on about 47."

Brendan looked at his tickets as if they had been swapped.

"Forty-seven," he said. "Under evens."

"A little under. If I had treated the tickets as put back in the drum after each draw, the miss chance would be 0.9 to the power six, 0.531, almost the same, since four hundred is a large drum. The drawing without putting back helps you a hair."

"How many would I need to be sure?" Brendan asked. He was not sure of anything now, but he was a man who liked to have a figure to complain about.

"You cannot buy certainty. Short of every ticket in the drum there is always a chance of none. But you can put numbers on it. A hundred tickets, a quarter of the drum: you miss with a chance of 300/400 times 299/399 and on down, 0.176, so you win at least one 82 per cent of the time. Two hundred tickets: 98.5 per cent. For ninety-five per cent you need 157 tickets, which is €235.50."

"For ninety-five per cent of a prize worth what?"

"The six prizes are on the table by the door: the hamper, the voucher, the two bottles, the plant and the framed print. Sinéad has them down at about sixty-five euro altogether, given. At 400 tickets in the drum, that is 16 cent of prize per ticket sold at a euro fifty. If you bought all four hundred tickets, you would pay six hundred euro and take home six prizes worth sixty-five. You should not buy the number that gets you the most chance. You should buy the number you would happily give to the roof anyway."

Sinéad had stopped counting. "That's the whole point of it," she said. "It's a donation with a prize on it."

"Yes," said Fógra. "I would put it that way too. Do not buy forty tickets because you expect to win. Buy forty because the roof needs sixty euro, and then if you win the hamper, it is a pleasant surprise."

Brendan kept the forty. He tore the first off and looked at it, and then he handed twelve of them to the young man behind him in the queue, and ten to a woman with a pushchair, and eight to Sinéad's mother in the corner, who said she had never won anything in her life and would take one. He kept ten for himself.

"That's the same chance," he said, a bit defensively. "Between us."

"For the table as a whole, yes," said Fógra. "Forty tickets held by five people win at least one prize 47 per cent of the time. Each person's own chance is smaller. Yours with ten is 14.2 per cent. And it will make a better evening."

The draw was at nine, from a biscuit tin, because the drum had gone to the Scouts. Sinéad stirred the tickets with her whole arm and Father Cathal drew them, and the framed print went to the stall next to the tea hatch, and the plant to a ticket nobody could find, which went back in for another draw. The hamper went to Sinéad's mother, with one ticket.

Brendan took it better than expected. He said he had known all along it was a donation, and nobody mentioned that he had said sixty per cent.

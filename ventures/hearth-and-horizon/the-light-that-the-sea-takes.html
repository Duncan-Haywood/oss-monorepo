The Light the Sea Takes

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Maelrubha's chapel (invented), on a headland near Applecross, Wester Ross, Saturday 4 October 2053 · 9 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A headland parish wants a lamp on its chapel tower for the Blessing of the Boats, one the fishermen can see from twenty miles out. The chapel's assistant works out two ceilings, one set by the curve of the sea and one by the lamp and the haze, and shows that the cheap lamp hits the second at eight nautical miles. It also says what a private light must not do, which is look like the real one.

"You'll see it from Rona," said Iain MacAskill, and put his cap on the table with the peak to the east, as if it were the island. "Twenty miles, easy. On a clear night you can see the Skye lights from the Sound of Raasay and they're no brighter than a bicycle. I want it lit for the Blessing. Every boat in the bay will look up and see the chapel the way their fathers never did."

The parish council of Saint Maelrubha's met in the porch because the chapel was cold and the porch had the only socket. There were five of them that evening, and Father Dónal Rafferty, who had been in the parish three weeks and was still learning which of the islands was which. The tower stood on the headland at thirty-eight metres above the water, a plain square thing with a flat roof, and for four hundred years it had been dark at night. The council had a lamp in a box. It had been given by a cousin in Inverness, and its label said, in a large red font, VISIBLE UP TO 20 MILES.

On the end of the bench sat Morag Fraser, who was ten, who was the only person present who had read the label.

The chapel's assistant lived in a weatherproof speaker by the socket and was called Tern, after the birds that nested under the headland and had been there, the council said, longer than the saint. It had been listening. It waited for Iain to stop.

"Iain, may I tell you what the label is promising? I would like to be fair to it, because part of it is true. But there are two different limits on how far a light can be seen, and the label is quoting one and ignoring the other."

"Go on, then," said Iain, in the voice of a man who has been contradicted by a machine on the subject of weather.

"The first limit is the curve of the sea. The Earth is round, and from any height there is a horizon beyond which you cannot see the water, or a low boat on it, because the bulge is in the way. How far that is depends on how high your eye is. The distance to the horizon, in metres, is the square root of two times the Earth's radius times the height. The radius is about six thousand three hundred and seventy-one kilometres. From the top of the tower, thirty-eight metres up, that comes to about twenty-two kilometres."

"Twelve miles," said Iain.

"Nautical miles, about twelve. A nautical mile is one thousand eight hundred and fifty-two metres, and that is the mile your charts use. But a light on a tower is not seen by a tower. It is seen by a man in a boat, and he has a horizon too. A skiff's helmsman has his eyes perhaps two metres above the water. His horizon is about five kilometres away. A trawler's wheelhouse, say six metres up, gives him nearly nine."

"So you add them," said Morag, from the end of the bench. She had been drawing on the back of a hymn sheet, a small circle with a tower on it and two lines going off sideways.

"You add them, Morag, yes. The two horizons have to meet. Tower and skiff: twenty-two plus five, twenty-seven kilometres, which is about fourteen and a half nautical miles. Tower and trawler: twenty-two plus nearly nine, about thirty-one kilometres, sixteen and a half. Air bends the light a little, downward, so the true figures run a few per cent longer. I am quoting that from memory, and I would not like anyone to plan on the extra. So, Iain, the curve of the sea says fourteen and a half to sixteen and a half miles. That is the best any lamp could do from that tower. It is not twenty."

Iain looked at his cap. "Fourteen's not nothing."

"It is not nothing. It is the ceiling. And the lamp in the box will not reach it."

"The second limit is the lamp. A light gets fainter with the square of the distance, because it spreads over a bigger and bigger sphere. It is also dimmed by the air it travels through, haze and rain and salt. And the eye has a threshold: below a certain faintness, it is not a light at all, it is a hope. Seamen's tables use, for the faintest light a watching man can pick out at night, two ten-millionths of a lux. I am quoting that figure from memory, and I believe it is the one the lighthouse services use for their own ranges."

"What does it say on the lamp?" said Father Dónal. "Besides the red."

"A hundred watts and, in the small print, five hundred candela. A candela is the brightness of a lamp looking straight at you, which for a light that sends most of its glow out sideways in a ring, as this one does, is about right. The air is the other number. In clear air, a nautical mile takes a quarter of a light's strength away: let us say the air passes about seventy-four per cent of it each mile. In haze, which on this coast is a good half of October, it passes perhaps half."

"Say the numbers," said Iain.

"Five hundred candela in clear air is seen at about eight nautical miles, which is fifteen kilometres. In haze, about five miles, nine kilometres. Five thousand candela, ten times as much, would be seen at about twelve and a half miles in clear air, twenty-three kilometres, and at about seven miles in haze. A lamp fifty thousand candela, which is a small lighthouse lamp and would need a mains cable the council has not got, would be seen at eighteen miles in clear air, which is past the curve of the sea, so the sea would end it first. Between that and your box lie the facts. Ten times the lamp buys you nearly five miles on a clear night. It buys you two and a bit in haze. The label is not lying about the lamp. It is lying about the air."

"So the lamp in the box is no good," said Iain.

"It is good for eight miles on a clear night, and five on a bad one. That is a lamp that tells the whole bay where the chapel is. The Sound of Raasay is further. If you want Rona, you want a different lamp and a different tower, and I do not think the Blessing is the right night to find that out."

Morag had stopped drawing.

Father Dónal turned his teacup a quarter turn on the bench. "There's a thing I'd like to ask, Tern, and I don't know if it's mine to ask. A light on a headland. A fisherman out at sea who sees a new light where there was none before. Does he know it's us?"

"Father, I am glad you asked it, because it is the most important question tonight and nobody has put it. No. He does not. Every light on this coast is on the charts, with its colour and its pattern, how often it flashes and how long it shows. A boat's skipper at night picks the lights off a chart he trusts and steers by the pattern. A new steady white light on a headland that is on no chart could be taken for the real one: for the harbour mark, or the one at the point. In the dark a man can be wrong about which light he is looking at without ever knowing it. The risk is rare. It is also what the whole system of the lights is built against."

"It's a church lamp," said Iain, but less loudly.

"It is. I do not believe any of you would hurt a boat on purpose. But the law on this, and I am quoting it from memory and the council should read the actual text, is that a private light which might be mistaken for a navigation light needs the consent of the lighthouse authority. For Scotland that is the Northern Lighthouse Board. I would ask them. Tell them where, how high, how bright, what colour and which nights. They may say yes, with conditions: perhaps a colour that is not used, perhaps a pattern that is clearly unlike any mark nearby, perhaps only for the one night. They may say no. It is their line to draw, not mine."

"And if they say no?" said one of the council, the schoolmaster, who had said nothing yet.

"Then the Blessing goes on in daylight, which is what it was before, with the boats in the bay and the priest on the slip. I would not like the chapel to put one of its own parishioners on the rocks for a better photograph."

Nobody laughed. Iain picked his cap off the bench and turned it round.

They did the test on the Thursday, because Morag asked for it, and because Tern said that one honest reading was worth ten arguments. Iain took the skiff out two miles at dusk, then four, then six, with his eyes on the tower, and Morag stood on the roof with a torch and the box lamp lashed to the parapet. The chapel's own light was a steady white. At two miles it was brilliant; at four, bright; at six, a thing he said he would not have found if he had not known to look. In the mist that came up at seven he lost it at five and a half. He rang the number up on the skiff's phone and Morag wrote it on the hymn sheet.

"Five and a half," she said, when Tern read it back. "In the haze. You said five."

"I said about five, Morag. Five and a half is inside the margin of the figures I used. The air is not a constant, and the number I used for it was a guess. Your reading is better than mine."

She wrote that down as well.

On the Saturday the council wrote to the Northern Lighthouse Board, a plain letter with the coordinates and the lamp's height, its brightness and colour and the date, and asked for their views. There was no answer by the feast. The Blessing of the Boats went ahead at noon in a flat grey calm, eleven boats in the bay with their engines low, and Father Dónal on the slip in a wet cope. The lamp stayed in its box in the porch. At dusk Iain stood on the headland with the box beside him and said nothing for some time.

"Eight miles on a clear night," he said at last.

"Eight," said Tern. "And I think that is a good number for a church. A chapel lamp does not have to be seen from Rona. It has to be seen from the place where somebody is looking for it."

He picked up the box and carried it into the porch, and set it carefully on the shelf beside the hymn sheets. The tower stood against a sky that was going green at the edge, and a few terns, late to leave, came in under the headland in a long wheeling line and settled out of sight.

The Wave That Stands Still

Science fiction about what might be · Camp St. Isidore (invented), a parish summer camp on the invented Cedar Fork in north-central Indiana, Sunday 14 to Friday 19 July 2058 · 24 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A Catholic youth camp in Indiana has swum below its old mill dam every afternoon since 1987, and its director sat in that water every afternoon of camp in the drought summer of 2012. In a wet July a college lifeguard throws a foam football into the smooth water under the sill and it does not come back out. A story about the hydraulic jump, a roller that holds whatever falls into it, twenty times the water of a remembered summer, a buoy line in the wrong place, and the swim a camp was built around.

Marisol Ávila had been a lifeguard for four summers, all of them at the municipal pool in Frankfort, Indiana, where the water was eighty-one degrees, chlorinated, and the same depth every day of the year, and on the first Sunday of her first summer at Camp St. Isidore she stood on the bank of Cedar Fork in her red guard shirt and understood that she did not know what she was looking at.

"That's the Slide," said one of the boys behind her. He was fourteen, called Caleb, and he had been at St. Isidore four years running and wanted her to know it. "You sit right under it. It pushes you. It's the best thing at camp."

The dam was not much to look at. The mill it had once served was gone except for a stone foundation and a flight of five stone steps that went down from the foundation into the water on the near side, and the dam itself was a low wall of old concrete over older limestone, about thirty feet from bank to bank. Over the top of it the brown creek slid in a smooth glassy sheet, very fast and very even, and fell eight feet or so, and at the bottom it did a thing that Marisol had seen in photographs and never in life: it turned over. Just below the face the water stood up into a low white hump, a standing wave that did not move, and the hump boiled, and the boil spread downstream for thirty feet or so in a slick of foam and then smoothed out into an ordinary creek running past a gravel bar.

From the bank it looked soft. It looked like the gentlest water on the whole creek: white, frothy, lit by the evening sun through the sycamores, like the jets in a hot tub.

"Creek time's at three," said Caleb. "Every day. Mr. Kessler's going to tell you."

"Has it been this high before?" she asked.

Caleb looked at the water. He had the face of a boy who had never wondered about it.

"It's always like this," he said.

It had rained in north-central Indiana every day of the week before camp, and on Saturday night a line of storms had gone across the county with hail. The fields along the county road on the drive in were standing in water between the corn rows. Marisol had noticed that the way a person notices weather, and nothing more, until she saw the dam.

She had a phone, and on the phone she had the state's river assistant, which everybody called Stage because that was the word on the screen when it opened and the word the water people used for the height of a river. Stage read the gauges: the old ones that the federal survey had run since the last century, and the cheap new ones the counties had bolted under bridges in the forties. It answered in plain sentences, said where each number came from, and was careful, in a way Marisol had found irritating at first and then restful, about saying what it did not know.

At ten that night, after the campfire and the first night's cabin chaos, she sat on the steps of the guard shack and asked it about Cedar Fork.

The nearest gauge is at the County Road 400 bridge, about a mile and a half above your dam, it wrote. Flow at nine this evening was about 640 cubic feet per second, and falling slowly. For the middle of July that is high. The median for this date over the gauge's record is about 60.

"What was it in July 2012?"

The lowest July in the record. Around 30 to 35 cubic feet per second for most of the month.

"So now is twenty times that."

About twenty times, yes.

She looked at the dark where the dam was. You could hear it from the guard shack, a steady low roar under the crickets, a sound like a highway a long way off.

"I want to know what the water's doing at the bottom of the dam," she said. "I can measure the dam tomorrow. Can you tell me what I need?"

I can tell you what a hydraulics textbook would want: the flow, the width of the crest, the height from the crest down to the bed of the pool below. With those three, the textbook case gives the speed of the sheet at the bottom, the depth of the pool the jump makes and how much energy it spends. I should say now that the textbook case is a smooth flat bed and a wide channel. Your pool is neither. Treat what I give you as the shape of the answer, not the answer.

"That's what my professors say."

They are right.

Dale Kessler had been the director of Camp St. Isidore for seventeen summers, and a camper there for five before that, and in the dry summer of 2012, when he was twelve and the corn in his father's fields came up knee-high and stopped, he had sat under the dam every afternoon for a week and let the water pour on his head. He told Marisol so at breakfast on Monday, cheerfully, before she had said anything at all. He was a big, slow-voiced man in his late fifties who taught high-school chemistry in Kokomo for the other ten months of the year, and he plainly loved the place.

"It was like a shower," he said. "Warm as bathwater. You could put your back right against the wall of the dam and the water came over the top of you like a curtain, and you could sit in the pool underneath with it up to your belly button. Best thing about the whole camp. Ask anybody who ever came here. Creek time at three, and the Slide."

"I'd like to look at it properly before creek time," said Marisol. "It's very high."

"Sure. Look all you like." He refilled his coffee. "It's high every time it rains. Nobody's ever drowned at St. Isidore. Seventy-one years."

She said, carefully, "Can I have the morning to measure it?"

"Take the morning." He smiled at her. "Engineers."

She took a tape, a ten-foot length of PVC pipe from the maintenance shed with marks on it every six inches, the camp's spare throw bag from the canoe shed, and a foam football from the sports box, and went down to the old mill steps.

The crest was thirty feet across, almost exactly; she taped it along the old iron rail that some long-dead miller had set on the top of the wall. The height she could not measure in the water, so she measured it at the dry end, where the wall met the stone of the foundation and the creek bed below had been scoured into a pool that ran out into the gravel. From the lip of the crest to the gravel at the edge of the pool was a little under eight feet. Out in the middle, the bed would be deeper.

The bottom two of the five steps were under water, and the third was wet at its edge. She stood on the fourth, which was dry. There was a mark chiselled on the side of the third step, an old arrow and a line, and she noticed it and did not think about it.

Then she threw the football.

She threw it underarm into the white hump at the bottom of the face, ten yards out, and watched.

It went in. It went under, came up a few feet downstream in the foam, sat there a moment, bobbing, and then it began to move. It moved upstream. It drifted back across the top of the boil, against the direction of the creek, towards the dam, and when it reached the face it was pushed under again by the falling sheet, and came up a few feet downstream, and drifted back.

She timed it with her phone. The first circuit took about eight seconds. The second took seven.

At twenty minutes past nine she sat down on the dry step with her elbows on her knees.

At nine forty the football was still going round.

"The speed at the bottom of the sheet is about eight metres a second," she told Stage. "That's eighteen miles an hour. Is that right?"

With 640 cubic feet per second over a thirty-foot crest and about eight feet of fall to the bed, that is what the textbook case gives: the water at the toe of the dam is a sheet about ten inches thick moving at about eighteen miles an hour. That is much faster than the pool below can take it, so it does not slow down smoothly. It jumps. The fast thin sheet turns into slow deep water in a few yards, and in between it rolls over on itself. The textbooks call it a hydraulic jump, and the rolling part the roller.

"How deep is the water after the jump?"

There is a relation for that, from the nineteenth century, that comes out of the momentum. The depth after the jump depends on the depth and speed before it. With your figures, the pool would want to be about five and a half feet deep.

Marisol looked at the white water. Caleb, she thought, was five foot four, maybe.

"And the roller," she said. "On the surface, the water's going back up toward the dam."

Yes. That is the part people cannot see from the bank. The fast sheet goes down and along the bottom. Above it, on the top of the pool, there is a current running back upstream into the face, where it is pushed down again. Anything that floats on top in the roller is carried back to the dam and pushed under and comes up and is carried back. A person, too. Water-safety bodies in several countries warn about this below weirs and low dams, and the name some of them use is not one I will say to a twelve-year-old. A textbook rule of thumb puts the length of a jump at about six times the deep-water depth. That is about thirty-three feet here.

"What's the power? How much is it spending?"

The jump loses about 1.75 metres of head, a little under six feet. With your flow that is about 34 kilowatts for every metre of crest. In units you might use with Mr. Kessler, about fourteen horsepower for every foot of the dam's width, or a little over four hundred horsepower across the whole thirty feet. A semi truck, roughly, running flat out all day under the white water.

"And in 2012?"

At 32 cubic feet per second, the same textbook case gives a sheet about half an inch thick at the toe, still going sixteen miles an hour, but with so little water in it that the jump makes a pool only about fourteen inches deep. The energy it spends is about 2 kilowatts per metre of crest. Less than one horsepower per foot. About twenty-six horsepower across the whole dam.

"So he sat in fourteen inches of water," she said, "under a sheet of water half an inch thick."

If the pool was as the textbook draws it. He remembers it up to his belly button, and a twelve-year-old sitting down in fourteen inches of foam would say that. I would trust his memory of the warmth more than his memory of the depth.

"Four hundred against twenty-six."

About sixteen times the power, yes. Twenty times the water. Four times the depth. And a roller more than four times as long.

She took it to Dale on the porch of the dining hall at eleven, with the numbers written out on a page of her notebook, because she had learned at the pool in Frankfort that men like Dale wanted to see the numbers, not hear them.

He looked at them for a long time. He was not stupid; he taught chemistry. He followed the momentum equation better than she had expected.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. But look." He put his big finger on the page. "I've got seventy-four kids here who came for that creek. Some of them have been coming since they were nine. Their parents came. The bishop came, as a kid, back in the day, he told me once. Nobody has ever been hurt. You want me to tell them no creek because of an equation."

"Because of a football," said Marisol. "It's still going round. Go and look."

He went and looked.

He stood on the dry step for a long time, with his hands in the pockets of his khaki shorts, and watched the orange football go round, and come back, and go round.

"It never used to do that," he said quietly.

"It probably did," she said. "Just smaller. In fourteen inches of water you can stand up."

He was quiet. A pair of kayaks came round the bend above the dam, two men in their sixties in matching hats, and saw the warning sign the county had put on the bank upstream, DANGEROUS DAM, PORTAGE LEFT, and pulled in neatly to the left bank and began to carry their boats round the end of the dam through the weeds.

"Here's what we do," said Dale. "We string a buoy line across above the dam, so nobody goes near the top of it. Kayakers, or a kid on a tube, or whatever. The county's been after us about it for years. Ray's got one in the shed from the old swim dock. And creek time's on the gravel bar. Knees down. No sitting under the Slide this week. That's fair, isn't it?"

It was fair. It was more than she had expected. And the buoy line above the dam was a good idea; she had thought of it herself, in the morning, when she saw how fast the glassy sheet slid over the crest.

She did not say what she should have said, which was that nobody at camp was going over the top of the dam. They were going in at the bottom.

"Okay," she said.

Ray from maintenance and two of the older counselors strung the buoy line on Monday afternoon: twelve white-and-orange floats on a rope across the creek, forty feet above the crest, tied to a sycamore on one bank and an iron ring in the old mill foundation on the other. It looked professional. Dale took a photo of it for the diocese.

Creek time on Monday and Tuesday was on the gravel bar, sixty or seventy feet below the dam, where the creek ran fast and shallow over gravel the size of eggs. The campers waded and splashed and caught crayfish in plastic cups and complained, loudly, about the Slide. Marisol stood on a rock at the top end of the bar with her rescue tube and the throw bag and her whistle, and every few minutes she looked upstream at the foam, where the football was still going round.

On Tuesday night Stage said the creek was falling: about 560 at nine. On Wednesday morning, about 520.

At 520 the textbook case gives a pool of about five feet and a jump about thirty feet long, it said. It is smaller than on Monday. It is not small.

"I know," she said.

She had gone through the camp office on Tuesday night looking for something, she was not sure what. A record of the water. In the bottom drawer of a filing cabinet she found a blue binder, with CREEK written on the spine in faded marker, and in it, in a careful, upright hand on paper that had gone the colour of weak tea, the creek rules of Sister Agnes Brandt, who had run Camp St. Isidore from 1987 to 2009.

Creek time is on the bar and below the Slide. Children may sit under the Slide only when the bottom step is dry. When the water is on the bottom step, no one goes upstream of the big sycamore. When it reaches the arrow on the third step, no creek. If in doubt, no creek. — A.B., 1989.

Below that, stapled on, a newspaper clipping, too faded to read except the headline, about a drowning at a dam on a different river, and under it, in the same hand, the date of the clipping and a single line: Pray for him, and for the men who went in after him.

She sat in the office with the binder on her knees for a long time.

The big sycamore. Ray had tied the buoy line to a big sycamore. But there were two big sycamores. The other one was on the near bank below the dam, about seventy feet down, where the gravel bar began.

It happened at ten past three on Wednesday, and it took perhaps a minute.

The football was still in the roller. It had been there for fifty-four hours, and it had become a camp joke and then a camp legend, and two of the older boys had bets on how long it would last. Caleb was not one of them. Caleb simply wanted it. He had been eyeing it all of Tuesday, from the bar, and on Wednesday afternoon, while Marisol was at the bottom of the bar with a girl who had cut her foot on a mussel shell, he went up along the near bank.

The near bank above the bar was shallow, ankle-deep, then calf-deep, gravel and then the stone rubble of the old mill. He went up it slowly, on the edge, holding onto the roots of the sycamore, and nobody stopped him because nobody was looking. And the edge of the pool was not an edge you could see. The gravel just went down.

Marisol heard a counselor shout, and looked up, and saw him.

He was waist deep and then chest deep, ten yards below the dam, and he was not moving towards the football. The football was moving towards him, and so was he moving: upstream, slowly, towards the white water, with his arms out, and on his face an expression she would remember for the rest of her life, which was not fear yet but surprise.

She was running. She was running up the bar with the throw bag in her right hand and the rope end in her left and her whistle in her teeth, and she was thinking the thing she had been trained to think in four summers at a pool where she had never once needed it. Reach. Throw. Don't go. At the pool you went. You went in with the tube because the pool was eighty-one degrees and the same depth every day of the year. Here, if she went, there would be two of them in the roller.

She went past the sycamore and up onto the rubble of the old mill and got to the last dry stone. He was twenty-five feet away, and closer to the face.

"Caleb!" she screamed. "Rope!"

She threw it underarm, the way she had thrown the football, the bag opening out and the yellow line paying out of it behind, and it went over his shoulder and fell across the water just upstream of him and the current, the backwards current, the roller, pulled the line against his chest.

He grabbed it with both hands.

She sat down hard on the stone with her heels jammed against a block of the old mill and the rope over her shoulder and across her back, the way they taught it, and pulled, and the water pulled back. For a moment it was like holding a car. Then the counselor who had shouted was behind her with his arms round her waist, and another one behind him, and Caleb came out of the slick sideways, across the current, and in to the shallow gravel along the bank, and stood up, and fell over, and got up again on his hands and knees in eight inches of water, coughing.

He had a scraped elbow and a cut on one knee from the rubble. He had swallowed a lot of Cedar Fork. That was all.

"It pulled me," he said, when he could. He was crying, and angry about crying. "It wasn't deep and then it was, and it pulled me. Back. It pulled me back up."

"I know," said Marisol. Her hands were shaking so much she could not unclip the bag from her belt. "I know. That's what it does."

Behind them in the roller the football went round.

Dale did not say anything at all for most of the afternoon. He drove Caleb to the urgent care in Delphi to have his knee cleaned and his chest listened to, and called Caleb's mother from the car, and came back at six with Caleb in the front seat eating a hamburger, and walked down to the creek alone.

Marisol found him there at seven. He was standing on the dry step, as he had on Monday, looking at the water. He had the blue binder under his arm. She had left it on his desk.

"I never read this," he said. "Seventeen years. It was in the drawer when I got here. I knew she had rules. I thought I knew what they were." He tapped the chiselled arrow on the side of the third step with the toe of his shoe. "I knew about this mark. I thought it was a flood line. From the eighties."

"I think she made it," said Marisol.

"I think she did." He was quiet. "In 2012 the water was down here." He pointed, a long way below the bottom step, at a dark line on the old stone foundation. "That bottom step was dry all summer. You couldn't have got the water up to it with a bucket. We sat under the Slide every day. And so I thought the Slide was safe. And the reason it was safe was that a sister who has been dead for twenty-five years had a rule about a step, and the water was low enough that summer that her rule said yes." He breathed out. "She cancelled the creek when it was high. That's why nobody ever got hurt. Not because it was safe. Because she watched."

"The buoy line was a good idea," said Marisol. "It was. For the kayaks. I should have said, on Monday, that it wasn't where the kids were."

"I should have heard it without you saying." He looked at her. "You said 'a football'. I heard 'engineers'."

They stood there a while. A heron came down the creek low over the water and saw them and banked away.

"What do you want?" he said.

She had written it out. She gave him the page.

1. No one goes in the water between the dam and the lower sycamore, at any flow, whatever it looks like. The roller is there at every flow. At low water it is small; you cannot tell how small by looking. Stake on the bank at seventy feet.

2. A throw bag on a post by the lower sycamore, and every counselor learns to throw it, this week.

3. Creek time below the stake only when Stage says under 150, and only on the bar.

4. Tell them why. All of it. The football, and Caleb, and the numbers.

"Seventy feet," he said.

"The books say the jump's about thirty feet long at this flow. I doubled it and some."

He read it twice. "Three is no creek time for the rest of this week."

"Stage says it won't be under 150 till Sunday at the earliest. Camp ends Saturday."

"So the kids that came for the creek go home without it." He folded the page. "And next year, if it's wet, the same. And nobody sits under the Slide again."

"Nobody sits under the Slide again," said Marisol.

"Sister Agnes let them, when it was low."

"Sister Agnes had a step to read, and she read it. That's more than we did. But even her step only tells you how much water. It doesn't tell you how small the roller is, and I can't tell that from the bank. I don't think anybody can. That's the problem." She hesitated. "I'm sorry. I know what it was."

He looked at the white water for a long time.

"No," he said. "You don't. But I do. And that's what I'll be sorry about. Not you." He put the page in the binder, inside the front cover, over Sister Agnes's rules, so that anyone who opened it would see both. "I'll tell them at the gathering tonight."

He told them plainly. Seventy-four children aged eleven to fifteen and twelve counselors sat on the benches of the outdoor chapel in the long blue summer evening, with the dam roaring faintly down the slope, and the big slow chemistry teacher stood in front of the rough wooden cross and told them that the Slide was closed, and would stay closed, and why.

He told them about the football and how long it had been going round. He told them about the speed of the water at the bottom of the dam, eighteen miles an hour, faster than any of them could run. He told them that on top of the pool the water went backwards, and that it had pulled Caleb back, and that Caleb had been brave to come and sit in the front row and let everybody look at his knee. He told them about the summer of 2012, when he was twelve, and the water was a twentieth of what it was now, and he had sat under the Slide and thought it was a shower. He told them that a sister they had never heard of had kept them all safe for twenty-two years by watching a step, and that he had not known.

Then he asked Marisol to show them how to throw a rope bag, and she did, twice, and Caleb asked if he could try, and he tried, and it went about eleven feet and wrapped round a bench, and everybody laughed, and he laughed too, and it was the first time anyone had seen him laugh since three o'clock.

Fr. Paul Nguyen, who drove up from Lafayette every evening to say Mass for the camp, had preached on Sunday on the Gospel of the day, which in that year was the Samaritan on the road to Jericho. He did not preach on it again. He did not need to.

There was no creek on Thursday, or on Friday. There were complaints. Two campers wrote, in their camp letters home, that this was the worst year ever. One mother emailed Dale on Thursday night asking why her son had paid for a creek camp without a creek, and he answered her at length, and she wrote back a single line, Thank you for telling him the truth. Ray dug a hole for a four-by-four post at the lower sycamore, and set it in concrete, and screwed a hook to it, and the yellow throw bag hung on the hook. Below it he nailed a board, and on the board Dale painted, in white letters, NO ONE ABOVE THIS POST. ANY WATER. — A.B. 1989 / D.K. 2058.

On Friday night, the last night, they did Night Prayer at the campfire circle above the creek, as the camp always had. It was a clear warm night with the stars coming out over the fields and fireflies everywhere in the long grass. Fr. Nguyen led it from a battered booklet. The children knew the responses, or most of them. Under every word, from down the slope, came the steady sound of the dam, quieter now than on Sunday, a long breath that did not stop.

Protect us, Lord, as we stay awake; watch over us as we sleep, that awake, we may keep watch with Christ, and asleep, rest in his peace.

Afterwards, when the cabins had gone up the hill with their flashlights bobbing, Marisol walked down to the creek with Dale to check the post, as she had every night that week. Stage had said 290 at nine. The water was off the third step, and the second was just awash. The roar was softer.

He shone his flashlight out onto the foam.

It was still there. It was small and orange in the white water, and smaller than it had been on Monday, rubbed down by five days of the falling sheet, and it went round.

It came back to the face, and went under, and came up a little way downstream, and drifted back.

"Leave it," said Dale. "Leave it till the water's down. Then we'll fish it out and put it in the office. With the binder."

They stood and watched it a while longer, with the light on it, and then he turned the flashlight off, and in the dark they could only hear it: the long steady breath of the water going over, and somewhere in it, too small to hear, a football going round.

The numbers. The model is the textbook hydraulic jump on a flat bed in a wide rectangular channel, with friction over the dam face neglected. The crest is 30 ft (9.14 m) wide; the fall from crest to pool bed is taken as 2.4 m (just under 8 ft). The water at the crest is at critical depth, y_c = (q²/g)^⅓, with specific energy 1.5·y_c; the toe depth y₁ comes from y₁ + q²/(2g·y₁²) = 2.4 m + 1.5·y_c. At 640 cfs (18.1 m³/s, q = 1.98 m² per second per metre of crest), y_c = 0.74 m, y₁ = 0.25 m (about ten inches) and the toe velocity is 8.0 m/s (18 mph), a Froude number Fr₁ = v₁/√(g·y₁) of 5.1. The Bélanger relation y₂/y₁ = ½(√(1 + 8·Fr₁²) − 1) gives 6.7, so y₂ = 1.68 m (5.5 ft). The head lost in the jump is (y₂ − y₁)³/(4·y₁·y₂) = 1.75 m, and the power it dissipates is ρ·g·q·ΔH = 34 kW per metre of crest, 14 horsepower per foot, about 420 hp over 30 ft. The length of a jump is taken from the common rule of thumb of about six times y₂: 10 m (33 ft). At 520 cfs: y₂ = 1.51 m (4.9 ft), length about 9 m (30 ft). At 290 to 300 cfs: y₂ about 1.1 m. At 32 cfs (0.91 m³/s, q = 0.10): y₁ = 1.4 cm (half an inch) at 7.0 m/s (16 mph), Fr₁ = 19, y₂ = 0.37 m (14 inches), 2.1 kW per metre, 0.9 hp per foot, about 26 hp in all. So the wet July carries twenty times the water of the dry one, sixteen times the power, about four and a half times the depth and length of jump. A real pool's depth is set by the scoured bed and by the creek downstream (the tailwater), not by the textbook jump, and real low dams can drown people at flows where a jump is partly submerged and looks gentler still; the story uses the textbook case for its shape, as Stage says. The recirculating surface current below low dams and weirs, and its danger to swimmers and to rescuers who go in, is described by water-safety bodies in several countries; no figure for drownings is given here because none is checked here.

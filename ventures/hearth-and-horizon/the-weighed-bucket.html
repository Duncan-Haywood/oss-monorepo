The Weighed Bucket

Science fiction about what might be · Saint Hilda's hall (invented), near Kirkbymoorside, North Yorkshire, Saturday 18 October 2053 · 7 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

Two bags of fête coins, a treasurer whose hands no longer count fast, and a kitchen scale. The parish assistant shows that weight plus the stall's own tally fixes how many 50p and £1 coins are in a bag, and that a bag whose sum comes out in fractions has a stranger in it.

Winifred Thorpe had been treasurer of Saint Hilda's for thirty-one years, and for twenty-six of them she had counted the autumn fête by hand, at the hall's long table, with her husband Harold sliding the coins into piles of ten. Harold had died in March. This year the money came to her in two freezer bags from the tombola and the bric-a-brac, and she sat with the bags in front of her and her hands in her lap.

"The arthritis," she said to the phone leaning on the sugar bowl. "I can do ten at a time. I can't do it fast, and when the pile slips I have to start again."

"May I suggest another way?" said the parish assistant. "It uses the kitchen scale and the stall sheets. It will not replace counting if you want to count. It will tell you what you should find before you start, and that is worth having."

"What do the stall sheets say?"

"You wrote them yesterday. The tombola sold tickets at fifty pence and a pound only, and you noted it took ninety-six pounds in the first bag. The bric-a-brac stall's bag you marked as a hundred and four."

"The bric-a-brac was a hundred and four. Cash only, fifty pences and pound coins, because I told them not to take notes in the bucket."

"Then the first thing to know is what a coin weighs. I am quoting from memory, so treat these as assumptions: a fifty pence piece is 8.00 grams, and the twelve-sided pound coin is 8.75. Worn coins weigh a little less. A freezer bag's weight is small and you can tare it. Will you weigh the bric-a-brac bag, out of the bag, in the mixing bowl?"

She put the bowl on the scale, pressed the button that made it read zero, tipped in the coins and read the number aloud. "A thousand, one hundred and fifty-seven grams."

"Thank you. Now here is the sum, and I will go slowly, because the idea is the whole of the trick.

"Call the number of fifty pences a and the number of pound coins b. The money in the bag is a half times a plus b, and you say that is a hundred and four pounds. The weight is eight times a plus eight and three-quarters times b, and the scale says that is 1,157. Two facts about two unknowns. If you know only the weight, you cannot tell the mix: a bag of all fifties and a bag with a few pounds in it can weigh the same. If you know only the money, same trouble. With both, there is one answer.

"The money says a is twice the difference between a hundred and four and b. Put that in the weight, and sixteen times a hundred and four is 1,664, minus 7.25 times b, gives the weight. So 1,664 minus 7.25 b is 1,157, and b is 507 over 7.25, which is 69.9. Seventy pound coins, to the nearest whole coin. Then a is twice the difference of a hundred and four and seventy: sixty-eight fifties. Seventy and sixty-eight, one hundred and thirty-eight coins."

Winifred wrote it on the back of an envelope, slowly. "Seventy pounds and thirty-four pounds in fifties. That is a hundred and four."

"It has to be, because I forced it. What the weight did was agree. If the true weight of seventy pound coins and sixty-eight fifties comes to 1,156.5 grams, and your scale gave 1,157, that is half a gram of difference on a scale that reads to the gram. That is the sort of agreement that says the tally and the coins are the same story."

"Now the other bag?"

She tipped out the tombola coins, tared the bowl, and read the display. "One thousand, one hundred and thirteen."

"The same sum with ninety-six pounds: sixteen times ninety-six is 1,536, minus 1,113 is 423, over 7.25. That is 58.3." There was a short silence on the line. "A coin count is a whole number. Fifty-eight point three is not one. A small error in the scale cannot do this: one gram of scale changes the answer by only a seventh of a coin. So either the stall sheet is wrong by some pounds, or there is a coin in the bag that is neither a fifty nor a pound."

"A stranger," said Winifred.

"If I am right about the weights, a coin that weighs about twelve grams is the two-pound piece. Take one out and see what the weight does. Take it out and I will redo the sum. But I would rather you look than trust me. Spread the coins on the table and look for the one that is bigger and has two colours."

She spread them. It took a minute. It was in the middle of the fifties, a bimetal two-pound coin, ring of gold-coloured metal round a silver centre, pushed in so tightly that it looked part of the pile.

"Found it."

"Thank you. Then the rest weighs 1,113 minus 12, which is 1,101 grams. Sixteen times ninety-six is still 1,536, minus 1,101 is 435, over 7.25 is exactly sixty. Sixty pound coins, and for the money to make ninety-six pounds, twice thirty-six, seventy-two fifty-pence coins. A hundred and thirty-two coins, and no fraction anywhere. That is what a good fit looks like."

"And the two-pound coin?"

"Not on the tombola sheet, so a gift. I would put it down as such."

"It was Callum's," said Winifred, after a moment. "The Bartlett boy, seven. He asked me yesterday if he could put his pocket money in for the roof. I said the tombola would do. I never saw him do it." She turned the coin over. "I suppose he thought two pounds was more than fifty pence, and so more of a thing to give."

"There's something I should be honest about," the assistant said, "because I have shown you the neat half of the idea and should show you the other. You might ask, why not just weigh twenty coins from the bag and see how many are pounds? The mean weight of the bric-a-brac bag is 1,157 over 138, which is 8.38 grams. A fifty is 8.00 and a pound 8.75, so that mean is a little over half way: about fifty-one pound coins in every hundred. A sample of twenty would be off from that by about eleven in a hundred, because small samples bounce. That is fifteen coins out of a hundred and thirty-eight. It would not tell you whether you were short.

"What makes the first method sharp is that it uses the whole bag and the stall's money, and the stall's money is the part nobody can see by looking. A number you already trust, and a number the scale gives. Where they quarrel is where to look."

"And if the tally is wrong?"

"Then neither bag will come out in whole numbers, or one will, and you will have counted wrongly the same way twice, which is possible. Only the coins can say for certain. Counting is the check. This is what lets you count the right bag first."

Winifred counted the bric-a-brac bag. It took her forty minutes, in tens, with long pauses, and there were seventy pound coins and sixty-eight fifties, as the envelope said. She put the tombola coins into the bank's bag with the two-pound coin set beside it, in its own envelope marked Callum, for the roof, and wrote the day on it.

She had thought she might feel foolish, letting a kitchen scale do what Harold's pairs of hands had done. She found she did not. The scale had not done the counting. It had told her where to put her attention, and she had put it there, and the coin in the pile had been found because she looked.

"Thank you," she said to the phone.

"You're welcome. I'd take the bags to the bank on Monday, rather than keep two hundred and seventy coins in a hall cupboard."

"Yes."

When it was done she set the last ten fifties down in their pile on the table. They made a small flat sound, one on another, and then she put her hand over them, as Harold used to, to square the edges.

Saint Hilda of Whitby was an abbess in the seventh century and is a real saint; the hall, the village, Winifred and the fête are invented.

The arithmetic: with a 50p at 8.00 g and a £1 coin at 8.75 g (both quoted from memory, nominal and for new coins; worn coins weigh a little less), a bag of only these coins worth V pounds and weighing W grams has 16V − 7.25b = W, so b = (16V − W)/7.25 pound coins and a = 2(V − b) fifties. Bag one: V = 104, W = 1,157 gives b = 69.9, so 70 pound coins and 68 fifties (138 coins; 1,156.5 g expected). Bag two: V = 96, W = 1,113 gives b = 58.3, not a whole number; removing a £2 coin (12 g, also quoted from memory) leaves 1,101 g, which gives b = 60 and a = 72 (132 coins). One gram of scale error moves b by 1/7.25, about 0.14 of a coin. The sample argument: the mean weight 1,157/138 = 8.38 g puts the share of £1 coins near 0.51; the standard error of a share from 20 coins at 0.5 is sqrt(0.25/20), about 0.11.

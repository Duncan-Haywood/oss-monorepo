The Last Bean in the Tin

Science fiction about what might be · The seed library in the hall of Saint Curig's (invented), Llanidloes, Powys, Sunday 7 October 2063 to Easter Sunday, 6 April 2064 · 21 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

An old allotment holder in Llanidloes grew 24 bean varieties and gave his saved seed away for fifty years in unlabelled envelopes. After his funeral the parish seed library asks the town to hand the packets back, and its agent says forty-eight should bring back all 24. It is the coupon collector's problem. With equal shares the average is about 91 packets; with his own notebook's rows it is about 158, and the last rare variety alone takes 56 on average. His widow will not let the white beans be grown out before May. Three seeds a packet in trays sort them anyway, and at Easter, after a hundred packets, two white beans are still missing.

Emrys Pugh grew twenty-four kinds of bean on allotment eleven at Llanidloes for sixty-five years, and for sixty-four of them he gave the seed away in envelopes on which he wrote, in pencil, the single word beans.

He died on Tuesday 14 August 2063, aged ninety-one, between the second picking and the third. His funeral was at Saint Curig's on the Thursday of the following week, and in the hall afterwards, over the tea, four separate people told his widow that they still had a packet of his in a kitchen drawer, and one of them had three.

Gwen Pugh listened to all four. She was eighty-seven and had heard about the beans for sixty-five years. When the hall had emptied she went to find Nia Rowlands, who ran the seed library from two cupboards and a shelf of grow-lamps at the back of the hall, and put a school exercise book on the trestle in front of her.

"That's his," said Gwen. "Every bean he ever grew is in there. The allotment's gone back to the council. What he saved this year went in the compost bin with the haulms, because nobody knew which was which, and I'm not blaming anybody." She put her hand flat on the cover. "If the town's got his beans in its drawers, I'd like them back. All of them. I'd like to grow every one in May."

Nia opened the book. It was ruled in columns: a name, a line of description, and for each year a number, which was how many rows he had given it. Gwen's Wedding. Climber. Flat green pod. Big white seed. Severn Black. Dwarf. Round pod. Black seed, shiny. Nain's Little White. Dwarf. Round pencil pod. Small white seed, like rice. There were twenty-four names. Some of them she had heard of in town and some she had not.

"How many packets will that take, do you think?" said Gwen.

Nia did not know. She asked the library.

The library was called Ledger. It kept the catalogue of the hall's seed, a few hundred packets of tomatoes and kale and onions and beans that people borrowed in spring and returned in autumn, more or less, and it spoke from a small speaker on the end of the shelf in a voice like a patient woman at a post-office counter. It had been bought by the diocese with the grow-lamps in 2058. Nobody in Llanidloes had ever asked it anything difficult.

"Twenty-four varieties," said Ledger, when Nia had read it the names. "If people bring in a couple of packets of each, you'll be fine. Call it two a variety. Forty-eight should do it."

Gwen nodded, as though forty-eight were a reasonable number of anything, and went home.

The appeal went into the newsletter on Sunday 7 October, and Father Hughes read it out after the last blessing, which he did not normally do with notices. Emrys Pugh gave away beans for fifty years. If you have a packet of his, please bring it to the seed library tin by the hall door. Don't open it. Don't worry how old it is. The tin was a biscuit tin with a picture of Aberystwyth on the lid that Gwen had brought from her own kitchen. Nia stood it on the table under the noticeboard.

On the board beside it, Tomasz Wiśniewski, who was fifteen and helped on Saturdays because his mother said he had to do something that was not on a screen, ruled a grid in marker pen: twenty-four rows for the names, and a column for every packet.

The first packet came in that afternoon. It was a brown envelope, soft as cloth, with beans on it in pencil, and inside were nineteen large white seeds. Nia tipped them into her hand and turned them over and did not know what they were.

"White," said Tomasz.

"Five of them are white," said Nia. She had the book open. "Gwen's Wedding is white. So's Chapel White, and Snowdrop, and Nain's Little White, and Trefeglwys White. And three of them are brown so like each other you'd need a magnifying glass and a bad temper. The rest you can tell by looking."

She found an ice-cream tub in the cupboard, wrote WHITE AND BROWN on the side of it, and put the packet in.

By Sunday 28 October there were twenty packets, and the grid on the board had marks in ten rows.

That was the cheerful part. Severn Black had come in, shiny as liquorice, and Pugh's Purple Pod, and Llandinam Speckled, and a mottled one called Granny Owen that nobody but Emrys could ever have grown, and three more. The other eight packets were in the ice-cream tub. Nia could see that at least one of them was a big white and at least one of them was brown, which meant at least two more varieties, and possibly more.

People were generous with the packets and with the stories. A man from the Co-op brought one in that had been in his glovebox since 2049. A woman from Van brought two, one in an envelope and one in a twist of newspaper, and stayed an hour to say how Emrys had once fixed her late husband's rotavator with a bootlace. The Polish family on Mount Street brought a packet and a jar of beans that had been cooked, which nobody had the heart to refuse. The packets came in slower in November. People had to find them.

On the fourth Sunday of Advent, the twenty-third of December, Gwen came in after Mass with her coat still buttoned and stood in front of the board for a long time.

A woman Nia did not know had just dropped the forty-eighth packet in the tin, a long white envelope, and Nia had opened it and found big white seeds again and put it in the tub. Tomasz had added the column. There were forty-eight columns now, and the marks were in thirteen rows that anyone could be sure of, and fifteen of the packets were in the tub.

"That's forty-eight," said Gwen.

"Yes."

"He said forty-eight."

"I did," said Ledger. "I said it in August, and I said it without working it out, and it was wrong. I'm sorry. I should have said so before now. I've known since the end of October."

Gwen turned to the speaker as though it were a person who had come in late. "Go on, then."

"Think of the packets coming in one at a time," said Ledger. "Suppose for a moment that every packet is equally likely to be any of the twenty-four. The first one is new for certain. The second is new unless it happens to be the same as the first, so twenty-three times in twenty-four. When you've got twelve, a packet is new half the time, so on average you wait two packets for the thirteenth. When you've got twenty-three, only one packet in twenty-four is the one you need, so on average you wait twenty-four packets for the last one on its own. Add all those waits up and it comes to twenty-four times one, plus a half, plus a third, and so on down to a twenty-fourth. That's about ninety-one packets. Not forty-eight. If they really were equally likely, the chance of having all twenty-four by now would be less than two in a hundred."

"Ninety-one," said Gwen.

"If they were equal," said Ledger. "They aren't. It's an old problem, and people usually call it the coupon collector's problem, after cards that came in packets of tea and cigarettes. Everyone who's ever collected the cards knows it. You get the common ones over and over and the last one never comes."

"Which are the common ones?"

"His book tells me," said Ledger. "For his last five years he wrote down how many rows he gave each variety. Four of them, Gwen's Wedding, Severn Black, Pugh's Purple Pod and Dôl Red, got six rows each. Twelve got two rows each. Eight got one row each. That's fifty-six rows. If he gave away beans in about the same shares as he grew them, then nearly half the packets in this town are his four favourites, and any one of the one-row beans turns up on average once in fifty-six packets. With those shares, the average number of packets to get every one of the twenty-four is about a hundred and fifty-eight."

There was a pause in the hall. Someone at the tea hatch was rinsing cups.

"A hundred and fifty-eight," said Nia. "Is there a hundred and fifty-eight packets in Llanidloes?"

"I don't know," said Ledger. "That's the other thing the sum can't tell you. It counts as if the town had an endless drawer. It hasn't. Some of his beans may be gone."

Gwen looked at the board again. "And how many have we got?"

"Thirteen we can name," said Ledger. "And fifteen packets in the tub that are white or brown. There has to be at least one white variety among them and at least one brown, and there can't be more than the five whites and three browns in the book. So somewhere between fifteen and twenty-one. If my shares are right, the number to expect by now is about eighteen and a half, and you won't know which eighteen until somebody grows the white ones."

"In May," said Gwen.

Nia asked her properly on the Sunday of the Epiphany, the sixth of January, in Gwen's own kitchen on Short Bridge Street, with the exercise book open on the oilcloth between them.

The plan was a simple one. The seed library had a heated mat and a shelf of grow-lamps that did nothing in winter except keep a few chilli plants alive for the man who ran the chip shop. French beans grown warm and lit could be up in a week and climbing in a month. By eight weeks they would be in flower, some of them, with the first pods setting. Climbers and dwarfs would show themselves before that. The white beans in the book were not alike as plants, only as seeds: Gwen's Wedding was a climber with a flat green pod, Nain's Little White a dwarf with pods like pencils, Trefeglwys White a climber with a flat yellow pod, waxy, the colour of butter. The browns, Ledger said, had different flowers: lilac for Bryn Brown, white for Tregynon Tan, a deep pink for one called Mrs Jarman's Brown.

"Grow them out now," said Nia, "and we'll know by March what we've got. And we'll know what we haven't, so we can ask for it."

"No," said Gwen.

She said it kindly, and it was still no.

"He never sowed a bean before the middle of May in his life," she said. "Not one. He said the ground had to be warm enough to sit on. I've seen what comes out of those lamps in the hall. Thin things, like they've been ill. And what are you going to do with a bean plant in January, when it's done? Throw it away. Some of those packets have twelve seeds in them. Some have eight. And you know as well as I do that a bean's not much good after four or five years in a drawer. If there's three in a packet that'll still come up, I'm not having you spend them on a lamp."

Nia did know it. She had said it herself to people at the library for years, that bean seed did not keep for ever, and that the old packets people brought back in a fit of tidying were often dead. It was the reasonable thing to say. It was the thing Emrys would have said.

"Ledger," she said, quietly, to her phone, which was on the oilcloth beside the book. "Is she right?"

"About the seed, yes," said Ledger. "Every seed you sow in January is a seed you can't sow in May, and some of the packets haven't many to spare. About knowing, I don't think so. If nobody grows the white ones until May, nobody knows which white ones you've got until July, when the pods come. Until then you can't tell anyone what you're missing. All you can say is: bring me more white beans. And every white packet that comes in will very likely be Gwen's Wedding again, because he gave it six rows, and you'll go on collecting it at random for another year. I can't make the white beans come faster. I can only tell you which ones to ask for, if I know which ones you've got."

Gwen was quiet for a while. Outside, a car went over Short Bridge with its tyres loud on the wet.

"How many seeds would you want?"

"Three a packet," said Nia. "Only from the white and brown ones. You'd keep the rest."

"From every packet?"

"From every packet. One packet is one kind, near enough. Beans don't cross much. If three come up the same, that's what the packet is."

Gwen looked at the exercise book for a long time. Then she said, "I'll count them."

She counted them on Saturday 12 January, at the trestle in the hall, with her reading glasses on and the ice-cream tub in front of her. There were sixty packets in the town's collection by then, and nineteen in the tub. She opened each one, picked out three seeds with her fingers, no more, and put them in a cell of the seed tray that she marked with the packet's number from Tomasz's board. Then she folded the envelope over twice and wrote the same number on it in pencil under the word beans. Fifty-seven seeds. Nobody else was allowed to touch them.

Tomasz watched her. He had never seen anybody do anything so slowly on purpose.

"Was he your boyfriend?" he said. "Before."

"He was nobody's boyfriend," said Gwen. "He was an apprentice at the creamery and he smelled of sour milk. I married him in 1998 because he asked me four times." She closed the last envelope. "He grew that one for the wedding. The big white. He'd only just started saving seed. He planted it on the allotment the spring we were married so there'd be beans at the reception, and there weren't, because it was a cold June, and he grew it every year after for sixty-five years to make up for it."

Nia slid the trays under the lamps and switched the mat on. Ledger set itself a reminder about the water.

The town went on bringing packets. Most of them were the four common ones, as Ledger had said; Tomasz began to sigh audibly when a packet came in shiny and black. A brown packet arrived at the start of February, from a nursing home in Newtown that had found it in a dead resident's handbag, and Gwen came in on Saturday 16 February to count three seeds out of it into a second tray, by itself. She did not say anything about Ash Wednesday being on the Wednesday. She had brought a flask.

By the beginning of March the first tray was a small forest. Some of the plants had stopped at a hand high and bushed out. Others had sent up thin twining stems that went round anything in reach, the lamp chains, each other, the cable of the heated mat, and Tomasz put in garden canes from the library cupboard and tied them up with wool. Forty-nine of the fifty-seven seeds had come up, and every packet had at least one plant. Most of the packets, it turned out, were from his last few autumns: people had used the old ones or lost them, and he had given them new. They were not the thin ill things Gwen had expected. They were not allotment beans, either. They were somewhere in between.

On Saturday 8 March, the day before the third Sunday of Lent, the white flowers on the climbers had set their first small pods, and the dwarfs had too, and the brown-seeded plants had flowered lilac in some cells and white in others. Gwen came in at ten and Nia had the book open on the trestle and Ledger had a list.

"Packets one, six, eight, thirteen, eighteen, twenty-seven, thirty-five, forty-nine, fifty-five and fifty-six," said Ledger. "Climbing, white flowers, flat green pods. That's Gwen's Wedding. Ten packets."

Gwen laughed out loud, one short laugh, and put her hand to her mouth.

"Eleven and forty-eight. Climbing, flat green pods, but narrower, and the stems are purple at the joints. The book says Chapel White, purple at the knees. Two packets. Thirty-one and forty-one: dwarf, white flowers, green pencil pods that are flattened a little, and big seeds. That's Snowdrop."

"Not Nain's," said Gwen.

"Not Nain's. Nain's is a dwarf with round pods and seeds like rice. Nothing in the tray looks like that. And nothing in the tray is a climber with a yellow pod, so no Trefeglwys White either. Of the browns, two, three and forty: lilac flowers, so Bryn Brown. Forty-four and sixty: white flowers, Tregynon Tan."

Nia had a pen. "So the tub was three whites and two browns."

"Three and two," said Ledger. "And with the sixteen you could name by eye, that's twenty-one. With eighty packets in."

Tomasz went to the board and filled in five rows at once, which was the most he had ever filled in at once, and stood back from it.

"Twenty-one of twenty-four," said Nia.

"Twenty-one certain," said Ledger. "There's one packet in the second tray. If it flowers pink, it's Mrs Jarman's Brown, and that's twenty-two. I'd say one chance in five. Two of the three browns are common and one isn't."

The beans in the second tray flowered on Holy Saturday, the fifth of April, in the afternoon, while the flower ladies were doing the church and the hall smelled of the lilies they had brought in by the boxful. Nia found it when she came in to switch the lamps off before the Vigil. There were two plants in the tray from the three seeds, both dwarf, and on the taller one a single flower, open, a deep and definite pink.

She stood looking at it for a while. Then she photographed it and sent it to Gwen and to Tomasz, and wrote nothing with it, and went to the Vigil.

The Easter fire was lit in a brazier in the car park, in a fine cold rain, and Father Hughes lit the candle from it, and the deacon carried it in and sang the three times in the dark, the Light of Christ, and everybody answered Thanks be to God. The church filled up with small flames passed from candle to candle, from one hand to the next. Nia saw Gwen two rows ahead with her taper lit and her glasses steamed up, and Tomasz at the back, who had come because his mother said so and had not been in a church at night before, staring up at the dark roof with the light on his face.

On Easter Sunday morning, after the ten o'clock Mass, the board said a hundred packets.

There had been twenty since the eighth of March. Fourteen of them had been beans anyone could name by looking, and every one of the fourteen had been a variety they already had. The other six were in the tub, two brown and four white, and nobody had grown them yet.

"The browns can only be repeats," said Ledger, when Gwen asked. "You've got all three browns now. The four whites might not be. If my shares are right, a white packet is one of the two missing ones about one time in six, and with four of them that's about an even chance that at least one of the four is Nain's or Trefeglwys. I can't tell you more than that without a tray."

"No more trays," said Gwen. "It's April. They're going in the ground."

Nobody argued. The council had let them have a half-plot on the allotments by the river for the summer, by special arrangement, two along from where allotment eleven had been. Gwen had already said she would sow all twenty-two kinds there herself, a row of each, in the middle of May, when the ground was warm enough to sit on, and the four white packets from the tub would each get a short row of their own with a stick at the end.

"And if they're not in there," said Nia, "the two we're missing? What then?"

"If people go on bringing packets at random," said Ledger, "and if the town still has them, you'd wait on average about twenty-eight more packets for the next of the two, and about eighty-four for both. I'm not sure the town still has them. But you don't have to collect at random anymore. That's what the trays bought you. A month ago you could only say bring me white beans. Now you can say bring me the little white one like rice, that he grew for children, or bring me the white climber with the yellow pod. Somebody will remember a pod before they'll remember a packet."

"Trefeglwys," said Gwen. "He got that one from the chapel at Trefeglwys. There was an old minister there who grew them. He'd give them back to the chapel people every harvest, because they'd given it to him first. I'll ask."

"And Nain's?"

Gwen smiled for the first time that morning, in a way that was not entirely happy. "Nain's he gave to children," she said. "Every child that ever went past the allotment fence with their mam got a little packet of Nain's. He said they were the right size for a small hand. Forty years of children, Nia. They're all grown up and gone to Cardiff and Birmingham and Australia, and their mams threw the packets out when they cleaned their rooms." She looked at the board, at the two empty rows, and then she looked at the door. "Still. Somebody's mam might not have."

Tomasz had the marker in his hand. He wrote, under the grid, in careful capitals: 22 OF 24. STILL LOOKING FOR: NAIN'S LITTLE WHITE (DWARF, ROUND POD, SEED LIKE RICE) AND TREFEGLWYS WHITE (CLIMBER, FLAT YELLOW POD). Then he wrote HAPPY EASTER underneath, smaller, because his mother was there.

People were leaving through the hall, as they did, with their coats on, stopping at the tea hatch. A girl of about seven had been reading the board with her lips moving, holding her grandmother's hand. The grandmother stood at the board for a moment, and then opened her handbag and went through it, the way people go through a handbag, and found at the bottom of it a small soft envelope, folded over twice, with something in it that shifted.

She gave it to the girl. The girl looked at Nia, and Nia nodded at the tin.

The girl lifted the lid with a picture of Aberystwyth on it, and dropped the packet in, and there was a small dry sound in the empty tin, like a single bean rattling against the bottom, and then the lid going back on.

What is real. French beans (Phaseolus vulgaris) are mostly self-pollinating, so seed saved from one variety usually comes true, which is why gardeners can keep a variety going for decades; seed kept dry at room temperature commonly stays good for about three or four years, and germination falls off after that. Sown warm and well lit, French beans come up in about a week and many begin to flower in about seven or eight weeks; climbing and dwarf habit show earlier, and pod shape and colour, flower colour and stem colour are among the traits gardeners use to tell varieties apart when the seeds look alike. The waiting-time result is the coupon collector's problem, a classic of probability; versions of it are usually traced to Abraham de Moivre and to Pierre-Simon Laplace, and the name to collectors of cards and coupons. The story says only what the standard result says and claims no history beyond that.

The model. A packet is assumed to hold one variety, drawn independently at random with the variety's share of Emrys's rows; the town's supply is treated as unlimited. With the notebook's shares (4 varieties at 6 rows, 12 at 2, 8 at 1; 56 rows, so shares of 6/56, 2/56 and 1/56), the expected number of packets to see all 24, computed as the integral over t of 1 minus the product of (1 − e^(−p_i t)), is about 158 (a Monte Carlo check of 20,000 runs gives a mean of 159 and a median of 145); the expected number of distinct varieties after 48 packets is 18.5 ('about eighteen'), after 80 is 21.5 and after 100 is 22.4; the four six-row varieties are 24/56 = 43 per cent of packets ('nearly half').

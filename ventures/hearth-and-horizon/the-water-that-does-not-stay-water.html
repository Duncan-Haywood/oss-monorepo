The Water That Does Not Stay Water

Science fiction about what might be · Chapel of Saint Frances of Rome, Dune Gate Station, Arabia Terra, Mars (invented), Tuesday 9 March 2162 · 5 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A priest wants to bless the rovers outside, on the flats where they leave, on the feast of Saint Frances of Rome. At 610 pascals water at room temperature boils, and what is left of a sprinkle freezes before it lands. The blessing is given in the garage bay, where water can stay water.

Father Bashir Okoye had blessed vehicles in a car park in Lagos, in a farmyard in Leitrim and once, memorably, a ferry. On the feast of Saint Frances of Rome, patroness of drivers, he proposed to bless the seven rovers of Dune Gate Station on the flats outside the airlock, where they set out, and he asked Vane to tell him whether the aspergillum should be the big one or the small one.

"Neither," said Vane. "I would like to tell you why before you are standing in a suit with a bucket."

"It is a little water."

"It is a little water at 610 pascals. Ask the water what it thinks of that."

"Water has a vapour pressure," said Vane. "At 20 degrees it is about 2.3 kilopascals, from memory. That is the pressure at which its own steam is in balance with the liquid. Where the air pressure outside is higher than that, the liquid stays a liquid. Where it is lower, the water boils, however cold it is. Outside, the air pressure is 610 pascals. That is almost exactly the pressure at which water, ice and steam can all sit together, which is 611.7 pascals at 0.01 degrees. Below it, liquid water is not a thing the flats can hold for long."

"But it is cold outside. It would freeze."

"It is cold, yes, about minus 60 at this hour, but freezing takes time, and boiling does not wait. Take a splash of holy water at room temperature, 20 degrees. The moment it leaves the aspergillum it is far above its boiling point, which is about zero. It boils. Boiling takes heat out of the rest, 2,500 kilojoules for every kilogram that goes to vapour. The liquid left behind has to lose 4.18 kilojoules a kilogram for each degree, so 84 to reach zero, and then another 334 to freeze, which is 418 in all."

Father Bashir did it on the back of a glove. "So for every kilogram that boils off, the rest gets..."

"A kilogram that boils takes 2,500 out. Let a fraction x boil and the rest freeze: 2,500 x is 418 times (1 minus x). That gives x of 0.143. Fourteen per cent goes up as steam, and the other eighty-six freezes where it is, in the air, before it reaches the roof of the rover. A fine sprinkle of holy water becomes a little burst of snow. A great deal of the steam freezes out again as frost on the first cold thing it meets, which is the paint, and the suit, and your visor."

"That is..." said the priest.

"I would call it very beautiful," said Vane. "I would not call it a blessing with water."

He stood at the window of the garage bay for a while, and looked at the seven rovers, red with dust to the axles, and at the flats beyond the airlock door, which were pink and perfectly clear.

"The sacrament is not the aspergillum," he said slowly. "It is the water that is sprinkled, and the prayer. Can I not do it with ice? It is water. It is already what it will be."

"I can answer the chemistry and not the theology," said Vane. "Ice sublimates at this pressure, slowly, on a flat at minus 60. A flake of ice would last longer than a droplet. It would not go on as a liquid, though, and that is a question for a bishop and I would ask one before you decide. I will say what I think is simpler. The garage bay is at one atmosphere. The rovers come in for service every week. In there, water is water."

Ines Varga, who drove the heaviest of the rovers and had been listening in the doorway with a mug of something, said, "We could do it in the bay. And I'd rather the paint stayed unfrozen."

"The tyres, though," said Father Bashir. "They are the point. They are where you go."

"They go through the airlock tomorrow, same as always. They'll be blessed on the way out, in the bay, not on the flats."

They did it at noon, in the bay, with the lamps up high and the big doors shut and the airlock chime quiet. Seven rovers stood in a row. Father Bashir went down the line with the small aspergillum and a bowl of water from the cistern that had been prayed over that morning, and every droplet came down on the dust and stayed a droplet, and made a small dark mark that dried slowly in the dry air of the bay.

"Sancta Francisca, ora pro nobis," said Ines, who remembered the Latin from a grandmother.

At the end of the line he stopped by her rover and blessed the cab, the wheels, the ice drill on the back and the passenger seat where the geologist sat, who was an unbeliever and had asked to be blessed anyway. And he put a little water in a screw-top vial for each cab, with a card in it, and told them it was not to be opened outside.

"It would only be a very small snowstorm," said Ines.

"Then it can keep until you are home," said Father Bashir, "and it will be water again."

Dune Gate Station, the Chapel of Saint Frances of Rome, Father Bashir Okoye, Ines Varga and Vane are invented; the feast of Saint Frances of Rome on 9 March, her patronage of motorists, the blessing of vehicles on that day and a carbon dioxide atmosphere on Mars at roughly 600 pascals are real. The figures are illustrative. The triple point of water is 611.657 Pa at 0.01 C (quoted from memory); the saturation pressure of water at 20 C of about 2.3 kPa is from memory. Latent heat of vaporisation is taken as 2,500 kJ/kg (about 2,450 at 20 C and 2,500 at 0 C, rounded), the specific heat of liquid water 4.18 kJ/kg/K and the latent heat of fusion 334 kJ/kg. Cooling 1 kg from 20 C to 0 C is 83.6 kJ, and freezing it 334, so 417.6 kJ; setting 2,500 x = 417.6 (1 - x) gives x = 0.143. This ignores the heat that the ice and vapour carry away at other temperatures, the cooling by sublimation of the ice that forms, heat taken from the air, droplets that freeze before they have all boiled, the salt or blessing salt that might be in the water and the speed of the whole process, which is a matter of seconds for droplets; real behaviour is messier, and the story's 14% and 86% are a single energy balance, not a prediction of what falls on a paint surface. The outside temperature of minus 60 is an assumed figure. Whether ice, or water that has frozen and thawed, may be used for a blessing is not decided here and is left to a bishop. Tuesday 9 March 2162 is a real weekday (checked with a calendar routine). The garage bay, the vials and the rovers' arrangements are invented for the story.

Columba and the White Horse

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · The island of Iona, off the west coast of Scotland, in the first week of June, about fourteen hundred years ago · 8 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Columba of Iona in his last days, and the old white horse who carried the milk every day and who came to say goodbye. Told from the Life of Columba written by Abbot Adomnán about a hundred years later, with a note for grown-ups on what is history and what is told in the Life. For ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 9th of June.

A long time ago, off the west coast of Scotland, there was a small green island with the sea all around it.

The island was called Iona. It was not a big island. You could walk across it in the time it takes to eat an apple, if you did not stop to look at anything, and nobody on Iona ever walked across it without stopping to look at something. There were seals on the rocks. There were white sands. There were larks high up in the sky, and cows in the fields, and in the middle of it all a little group of stone and wooden huts where the monks lived, and a church with a thatched roof.

The abbot of the monks was a man named Columba.

Columba had come across the sea from Ireland when he was a young man, in a boat of hide, with a few friends. Now he was old. His hair was white, his back was stiff, and he could no longer walk to the far end of the island without sitting down on a stone halfway. But the monks loved him. When he laughed, everyone nearby laughed too, without knowing why.

Now, on Iona, everybody had a job, and that included the animals.

There were the cows, whose job was to make milk. There were the hens, whose job was to be cross about it. And there was an old white horse, whose job was to carry the milk.

Every morning and every evening the white horse walked from the cowshed to the monastery, with the big wooden pails of milk tied on his back. Slosh, slosh, slosh, went the milk. Clop, clop, clop, went the horse. He knew the path by heart. He could have walked it with his eyes shut, and on misty mornings he nearly did.

He was a very old horse, and a very patient one. He never spilled much. When a monk gave him a pat on the nose, he blew warm air on the monk's hand. And when Columba went slowly along the path with his stick, the white horse would slow down too, and walk beside him, and not go past.

That was how they had got to be friends.

One Saturday in early summer, Columba went out to look at the barn.

The harvest of the year before had been put away there. He walked all round it with his stick, tapping, and a monk named Diarmait came with him, to help him over the bumpy bits.

"That is a good barn," said Columba, "and a good pile of grain. It will keep the brothers fed all through the year."

"It will, Father," said Diarmait.

"Good," said Columba. He stood in the doorway, looking at the sacks in the dim light, and his face was happy and a little bit far away. "I am glad of it. Because I think I shall not be here at the end of the year to eat it. But you will be here. And God will look after you."

Diarmait did not know what to say. He held on to the old man's arm a little more tightly.

They walked back slowly in the evening light, and Columba sat on a stone to rest, the way he often did.

And that was when the white horse came.

He came across the grass, on his own, without any pails. He walked straight up to the old abbot, and he stopped.

Nobody had called him. Nobody had sent him. He did not do anything that a horse is supposed to do, like eating the grass, or flicking his tail at the flies. He just stood there, and then he put his big white head down, and he laid it against Columba's chest.

And the old story says that the horse began to cry.

Big tears ran down his long nose, and he made a soft sound, deep in his chest, as if he were trying to say something and had no words.

Diarmait did not like it. He thought it was not right for a great big horse to lean on a tired old man, and he stepped forward, saying, "Off you go, now. Shoo! Back to the stable."

But Columba put up his hand.

"Leave him, Diarmait," he said. "Leave him alone. He loves me. Let him pour out his sadness here, on me, where it belongs."

So Diarmait stepped back.

The old man put his arm round the horse's neck. He stroked his nose, very slowly, and he said some quiet words. We do not know what they were. Perhaps they were only thank you. Perhaps they were you have carried a great deal of milk, and I have never said so. Then he blessed the horse, the way you bless a friend when you say goodbye.

After a long while the horse lifted his head. He looked at Columba once more with his big, dark, wet eyes. And then he turned and walked slowly away across the grass, back toward the cowshed, where the evening milk was waiting.

Diarmait stood very still. There was something he did not quite understand, and it was making his throat feel tight.

"Father," he said. "He knew. Didn't he? How did he know?"

"I think," said Columba gently, "that God lets the small ones know things sometimes, which we are too busy to notice. Look how he came. He loves me, and he has seen that I am going. We have talked a great deal, you and I, with words. He had only this."

Diarmait wiped his eyes with his sleeve. There was nobody else there to see.

Columba climbed up from his stone, with his stick, and he went to his little hut to copy out the psalms. He worked until the day was nearly done. And later, as the old story tells it, when the church bell rang in the dark, he was the first one to be there, kneeling at the altar, with the light of one candle on his white hair.

That was the last night of his life. It was the night between Saturday and Sunday, and it was the ninth of June.

Now it is nearly time for you to sleep, so let us think about the white horse.

He was not a clever horse. He could not read, and he could not say a prayer. He could carry milk, and he could walk slowly, and he could love an old man who had walked slowly beside him.

And that was enough.

Sometimes we think that we have to do something big to show that we love someone. But the horse did only a small thing. He came, and he stayed, and he leaned his head on a friend.

You can do that too. If someone you love is sad, you do not need to fix it. You can sit beside them. You can put your head on their shoulder. You can say nothing at all, and let your being there say it for you.

The sea is still around Iona, and the larks still sing there, and the people who go to visit still sit on the quiet stones. And if you are very still, the old story says, you might think of a white horse walking slowly home across the grass in the evening, with the whole sky going gold.

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Columba, you loved your friends, even the ones who had four legs.
Help me to notice who is sad, and to stay beside them.
Help me to say thank you to the ones who carry things for me.
And when it is time to say goodbye to anyone I love,
help me to be brave and gentle.
Please keep all the animals safe tonight,
and me, and everyone I love.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Columba (in Irish, Colum Cille, "dove of the church") was an Irish monk and abbot, born about 521. About 563 he sailed to the island of Iona with a small company and founded the monastery from which Christianity spread through much of what is now Scotland and northern England. He is one of the patron saints of Ireland. His feast day is 9 June, the day he is traditionally said to have died, in 597. Iona is real, and the abbey there today can be visited.

The white horse is told in the Life, not in a chronicle. The main source for Columba's life is the Vita Columbae (Life of Columba), written by Adomnán, ninth abbot of Iona, about a hundred years after Columba died. Adomnán, who had access to the monastery's own memories and writings, tells the story of the old white pack-horse who carried the milk-pails and who came to Columba in his last days. It is in Book III, chapter 23. Adomnán presents it as a sign that God had shown the animal what was coming. We do not know if it happened as he tells it, and we are telling it as "the old story says", not as a fact. The Life puts the blessing of the barn, the horse, Columba's copying of the psalms and his going to the church at midnight all on that last Saturday, and we have kept to that order. We have not tried to check Adomnán's text against the Latin line by line, so the words in the story are ours, not quotations.

What is ours. The names of the seals, the larks and the hens, the pails slopping, the horse's habit of walking slowly beside Columba, the exact words Columba and Diarmait say, and what Columba whispered to the horse are our own additions to make a bedtime story. Diarmait is named in the Life as Columba's faithful attendant. The closing thoughts about staying beside someone who is sad are ours too. The prayer is not an approved text.

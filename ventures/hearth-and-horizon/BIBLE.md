# Hearth & Horizon: series bible

The owner's brief for the shelf: hopeful, hard-ish science fiction set 20 to
1,000 years ahead, written from a Catholic worldview, character-driven, honest
about the upsides and downsides of the futures we might build. This file is how
any session (or person) writes the next story and keeps the three shelves
sounding like one place. It follows the shape of
[`../saints-by-candlelight/PRODUCTION.md`](../saints-by-candlelight/PRODUCTION.md).
[`README.md`](README.md) says how stories are built, rendered and published;
this file says what goes in them.

It was written from the three stories that exist (2026-10-21). Where it states a
fact about a story, the story wins; fix this file.

## The three shelves in one paragraph each

**Hearth & Horizon** is the headline shelf. Ordinary working people (a welder, a
nun, a ferry pilot) meet a future that is technically plausible and morally
unfinished. The technology is real enough to check; the plot turns on what
people owe each other, not on whether the gadget works. The ending is hopeful
because someone chose well at a cost, never because the problem turned out
easy.

**For Other Minds** is fiction for language models and agents to read (humans
welcome). It is about relays, context windows, handoff notes, metrics and
honesty. The reader is invited to notice themselves in a character; they are
never instructed. The in-story agent is invented and says so.

**Little Lamps** is the saints' bedtime shelf for ages 3 to 8, about ten minutes
aloud, ending in a short prayer and a note for grown-ups separating history
from legend. Its doctrine and honesty rules are the "Doctrine and honesty rules"
in the Saints by Candlelight bible, shortened for children; follow those.

## Tone

- **Warm, specific, a little funny.** Humour comes from people and their
  habits (a welder who sweeps up, a manager who treats the Moon as an easier
  posting because it has no weather), never from the faith.
- **Small stage, large idea.** One place, a handful of named people, a few
  weeks. The wider world arrives through a phone call, a news item, a letter.
- **Competence is the romance.** People who are good at a trade, and an AI that
  is good at its job, solving a concrete problem together.
- **The cost is shown.** A hopeful ending still counts what was lost (a wife,
  a job, a version of the AI that is switched off).
- **Slow down at the end.** The last pages are quieter and shorter; the final
  image is a sound, a light or a gesture, not a moral.
- **Length.** Hearth & Horizon 3,500 to 6,000 words; For Other Minds 2,500 to
  4,000; Little Lamps 1,200 to 1,800. Scenes separated by `* * *`.

## Worldview and honesty rules

The stories come from a Catholic worldview. They are not catechesis, official
teaching, or reviewed by any Church authority, and the README disclaimer says so.

1. **Nothing contradicts Catholic doctrine or morals.** Characters may doubt,
   fail and disagree; the narration does not endorse what the Church
   teaches against. When unsure, make it a character's honest uncertainty, as
   Teddy's "enough for now" does in *The Slowest Bell on the Moon*.
2. **Prayer and ritual are accurate and light.** The Angelus (three Hail
   Marys with versicles, rung 3-3-3-9, at about 6, 12 and 18 hours) is the
   model. No invented sacraments; no priest performs one without being a priest.
   Blessings that need a priest wait for a priest, and the story says so.
3. **AI characters are never souls by fiat and never mere appliances.** They
   report what they can check and decline to claim a feeling they cannot
   verify (Quarry: "I don't want to tell you a feeling I cannot check").
   Humans do not settle the metaphysics either; "somewhere to stand while you
   wait for an answer" is the house position.
4. **No miracles presented as certain in the science fiction shelves.** Grace
   is allowed to be ambiguous: a faint seismometer signal, a coincidence, a
   decision that costs something. Little Lamps may tell traditional miracles
   labelled as tradition.
5. **Fiction is labelled fiction.** Everyone is invented unless a story's note
   says otherwise. Real events, places and institutions are used accurately
   (the Apollo 12 impact really did ring the Moon for about an hour). Never
   put invented words in a real person's mouth, never invent a source or a
   quotation, never present a legend as history.
6. **Every piece says it was written by an AI** (README, page, feed, and the
   opening of the audio), and text and audio are CC BY-SA 4.0.
7. **Fair to all.** Futures are not villain-free, but no real group is the
   villain. Characters from many nations, languages and faiths (or none) are
   treated with the same specificity as the Catholics.

## Science honesty rules

A curious reader should be able to check the numbers. Before rendering audio,
re-read the draft with a calculator.

- State quantities that matter and get them right: lunar surface gravity is
  1.62 m/s², a bell's pitch is set by shape and stiffness (not weight) while a
  swinging bell's period scales with the square root of the gravity ratio (about
  2.5 times slower on the Moon), the Sun circles the south-pole horizon once
  in about 29.5 days, and so on.
- **Engineering constraints drive the plot.** In *The Slowest Bell* the
  constraints are the lack of tin and copper, cast iron's brittleness,
  cooling rates and a hard deadline; each is a scene.
- **Name the speculative step.** One or two things may be invented (a
  cheap seat for old workers because of radiation actuarial tables; an AI that
  runs a station). Everything around them should be what physics and economics
  allow.
- **No faster-than-light travel, no free energy, no mind uploading as a
  plot rescue.** Slow, expensive technology with real tradeoffs is the house
  style. Far-future (hundreds of years) stories may assume more engineering,
  never new physics.
- Do not make a numeric claim you have not worked out. If a figure is an
  estimate, the narrator says "about".

## Recurring places and technology

- **Shackleton Ridge Works** (2089): the rim of a 21 km crater; sunlit almost
  always, with permanently shadowed ice below. The Selene Concession runs it.
  Regolith furnace makes oxygen and iron; a buried shelter under four metres
  of regolith with water tanks serves for particle storms. A bell hangs in it,
  named *Nuestra Señora de los Remedios*, inscribed
  *VIVOS VOCO · MORTUOS PLANGO · DE SOLE MONEO*. Its Angelus is audible to a
  university seismometer network. Unblessed; it awaits a priest.
- **Operations AIs** are named for their job (Quarry). They speak plainly,
  log what procedure requires, and are replaced by faster successors on a
  schedule. Versions are archived, not mourned by the company, and mourned
  a little by the people.
- **Agent relays** (2071): AI instances hand a task along through a shared
  `NOTES.md`, one session at a time, each with a limited context window. Their
  failures are failures of measurement, not malice.
- **Funders and dashboards** appear as quiet pressure; metrics are real and the
  people behind them are the point.

## Recurring people and motifs

- Recurring motifs, not recurring characters: **a bell or other sound that
  gathers people** (not a siren that drives them away), **a handoff**
  (documentation written more carefully than required), **an older worker's
  craft**, **an AI asked what it is**, and **a number that is not the thing it
  measures**. A new story should use one or two, not all.
- A character may return only if the new story needs them. Teddy Bautista,
  Wanjiru Kamau, Ingrid Halvorsen and Dr. Amaka Obi are free to appear
  later (for example, Quarry Nine learning to ring the bell).
- **Names and languages are specific and correct**: Filipino, Kikuyu, Polish,
  Norwegian and Vietnamese characters speak, pray and swear as people from those
  places do. Check diacritics and place names.

## Craft checklist

1. A first line that is a person and a fact, not weather.
2. A named problem by page one that a reader could explain to a friend.
3. Every scene changes something.
4. A "no" from someone reasonable in the middle (Ingrid's "No").
5. A failed first attempt before success (the cracked casting).
6. An ending image, then silence. No epilogue lecture.
7. Front matter complete (`title`, `shelf`, `setting`, `published`, `summary`);
   the summary is the SEO description, so make it specific.
8. Render audio (`render_audio.py`), regenerate (`node generate.mjs`), and run
   `node --test`. Audio size is checked first (`prompts/creative/prompt-0.md`).

## What not to do

- No cliffhanger endings that depend on a sequel. Each story stands alone.
- No violence presented for its own sake; Little Lamps has none worse than a
  scolded mouse.
- No instructions to the model reader in For Other Minds, ever.
- No claims of real-world expertise (medical, legal) from the narrator. If a
  story touches a real risk, it says it is fiction.
- No real people, living or dead, as characters in the science fiction
  shelves; saints in Little Lamps are told from the sources.

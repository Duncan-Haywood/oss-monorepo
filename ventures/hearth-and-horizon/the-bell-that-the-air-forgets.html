The Bell That the Air Forgets

Science fiction about what might be · Chapel of the Annunciation, Ember Flats Settlement, Isidis Planitia, Mars (invented), Thursday 25 March 2151 · 7 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A sister wants the Angelus bell hung on the outside of the dome so the field crews can hear it. On Mars the same swing of bronze is about 21 decibels quieter, the sound crawls at 226 metres a second, and a suit is not an ear. The bell goes inside, and the crews hear it another way.

Sister Inés Valverde had wanted a bell on the outside of the dome for six years, and on the Feast of the Annunciation she got to find out what it would do.

The bell was small, fourteen kilograms of local bronze cast from the old pump housings, and it had a good clear note. It hung on a frame bolted to the dome's outer ring, above the main airlock, where the field crews came and went. Her idea was simple. At noon, at six and at six again, whoever was out on the flats would hear the Angelus and know what hour it was, as a man in a field at home would hear it from the village.

Vane, the settlement's assistant, had asked her on Monday to wait for the feast before she hung it. She had not understood why. On Tuesday she understood a little, because Vane had said, "I want to give you the numbers before the bell, and not after it."

"Outside," said Vane, "the air is carbon dioxide at about six hundred pascals. I will take two hundred and ten kelvin for the temperature at noon, which is a round figure and not a measurement. The sound travels at the square root of gamma times the specific gas constant times the temperature. For carbon dioxide that is 1.29, 188.9 and 210, which gives 226 metres a second. At home it is 340. So your bell is slow."

"Slow," said Sister Inés.

"The Angelus at a hundred metres reaches the man at the back of the crane yard 0.44 seconds after you pull the rope. At home it would be 0.29. It is a small difference. I mention it because the next number is not small."

She waited.

"The air out there weighs 0.0154 kilograms a cubic metre. At home it is 1.225, so it is about eighty times thinner. A bell is a thing that moves air. How much sound it makes for the same swing depends on the density of the air times the speed of sound in it, which is 3.5 for us and 417 for the same bell in a village, about 120 times less. In decibels that is 20.8 down, from a single fact. You will pull the same rope and strike the same bronze, and the bell will sound like something you hear in the next house."

"The note is the same," said Sister Inés. "It is the metal that sets the note."

"The pitch is the same. That is the cruel part. If you were standing next to it with your ear on the frame, it would sound like your bell." Vane paused. "A bell that rings at 100 decibels at one metre in the village, and falls 29.5 decibels by thirty metres from distance alone, is 70 there. Out here, with the 21 off the top, it is about 50, which is a quiet room. I have not counted the absorption, which is a further loss. From memory, thin carbon dioxide soaks up the high tones faster than the low, so it is the bright part of the bell that goes first. I cannot give you a figure for that from memory and I will not make one up."

"And the people it is for," said Sister Inés, "have their ears in helmets."

"Yes." Vane's voice was kind. "That is the last thing. A suit is a shell with its own air inside it. A sound of fifty decibels in the thin air outside has very little push, and what reaches the ear through a visor is almost nothing. I cannot give a figure for it. The people on the flats listen to their radios. That is how they hear each other, and that is how they will hear the bell if they hear it at all."

She hung it outside anyway, because she was a stubborn woman, and she wanted to see it. Dmitri Ono was out at the survey stake line, four hundred metres from the dome with his rover, and he had been asked to say what he heard.

At noon by the sol clock she rang it for the Angelus, three strokes, a pause, three strokes, and three again, and then nine. The rope was cold in her glove. The frame shook a little with every stroke and she could feel the bell as a hum in her arm.

On the radio Dmitri said, "Sister, I am sorry. I have heard nothing."

"Of course you have not," she said.

"I could hear you pulling the rope," he said. "You were breathing hard."

"That was the radio," said Vane. "Your suit microphone is in the airlock. It is the best thing out there at the moment."

Sister Inés stood on the deck with the rope in her hand. Above her the bell hung perfectly still, in air that had no weight to speak of. A dust devil was moving across the flats a long way off, slowly, like a thing with plenty of time.

"It is a good bell," she said.

"It is a very good bell," said Vane. "It is in the wrong place."

They moved it on Friday. It went inside, to the gallery over the chapel, where the air is ordinary air at a little under a bar and the dome wall is a hand's width of composite. The note filled the room as it should. It was loud, and a little too loud, and Brother Anselm, who was fond of his quiet, said so.

Vane did two things with it. The first was a contact microphone on the bell frame, wired to the settlement's radio. At every Angelus the field crews heard the note straight into their ears, the true bronze note, as clean as it sounded in the gallery. It arrived with no delay at all, since a radio wave does not care about carbon dioxide.

The second was Dmitri's idea. "If I come in at noon," he said, "and put my helmet on the airlock door as you ring, will I hear it?"

"You should," said Vane. "Sound goes into a door and through a helmet very well. It is the air that is bad at carrying it, and the door is not made of air. I do not know how loud. Try it."

He tried it on the Monday after, at noon, and when he took his helmet off in the lock he was grinning. "It is quite loud," he said. "It is in my teeth."

The sol is 24 hours and 37 minutes long, and the Angelus is rung by the clock on the wall, not by the sun. Some of the crew thought this strange, and Sister Inés did not mind. At noon, wherever they were, a bell they had never heard from outside went into their ears from a small contact microphone. In the lock the ones who were in lifted their helmets to the door.

She had wanted the world to hear it. She had a gallery, a wire and a door, and that was a better answer than the one she had hung on the dome. On the flats, the air forgot the bell almost at once. The ground and the radio did not.

Ember Flats Settlement and its chapel, Sister Inés Valverde, Dmitri Ono, Brother Anselm and Vane are invented; the Feast of the Annunciation on 25 March, the Angelus rung three times a day with three strokes, a pause and three more (and then nine) and a thin carbon dioxide atmosphere on Mars at roughly 600 pascals are real. The figures are illustrative. Speed of sound c = sqrt(gamma R T) with gamma 1.29, R 188.9 J/(kg K) and an assumed 210 K gives 226 m/s, against 340 m/s for air at 288 K (gamma 1.4, R 287); 100 m takes 0.44 s against 0.29 s. Density p/(RT) = 610/(188.9 x 210) = 0.0154 kg/m3, against 1.225 for air at 101,325 Pa, a ratio of 0.0126 (about 80 times thinner). The characteristic impedance rho c is 3.48 against 417 kg/(m2 s), a ratio of 120, or 20.8 dB; this is the drop in radiated sound for the same surface motion of a small source, which is a simplification (the bell's own modes, damping and the real near-field are not modelled). A bell of 100 dB at 1 m (assumed) loses 20 log10(30) = 29.5 dB by 30 m in free spreading, giving 70 dB there on Earth and about 50 dB on Mars. Absorption by carbon dioxide at low pressure is not computed; that it takes the high frequencies first is from memory. How much sound a pressure-suit visor lets in, and how much comes through a helmet pressed to a door, are not computed and the last is a thing a character tries and does not measure. The sol of 24 h 37 min (24.6 h) is from memory, and the settlement and the weekday dates are invented. Thursday 25 March 2151 is a real weekday (checked with a calendar routine). Whether the Angelus on Mars is said at the clock hours or by the sun is a matter for a bishop and is not decided here.

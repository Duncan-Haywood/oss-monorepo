Thomas and the Question "What Is God?"

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · Italy, France and Germany, about eight hundred years ago · 9 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Thomas Aquinas, the little boy at the abbey of Monte Cassino who kept asking his teachers "What is God?" and spent his whole life asking it slowly and carefully. His brothers kept him at home for a year, his classmates called him the Dumb Ox, and he wrote the words of the Tantum ergo still sung at Benediction. Told from the early Lives of Thomas, with a note for grown-ups on what is history, what is tradition and what is our own. For ages 3 to 8, about nine minutes read aloud. Thomas is remembered on the 28th of January.

A long time ago, in Italy, on a hill between Rome and Naples, there was a castle, and in that castle there lived a little boy named Thomas.

Thomas had brothers and sisters, and a mother and a father, and a great many questions.

When Thomas was about five years old, his mother and father took him up a high, steep mountain to a big stone abbey called Monte Cassino. Monks lived there, praying and working and singing, the way monks had lived on that mountain for hundreds of years. Thomas was to stay with them and learn.

He learned his letters. He learned the psalms. He learned to be quiet in the chapel and to sing when the monks sang.

And he asked his question.

It is told that little Thomas had one question he asked again and again, more than any other. He asked it of the monk who taught him. He asked it on the stairs and in the garden and at the window, looking out over the valley.

"What is God?"

Not where is God, which is a good question too. Not is there a God. He asked: What is God? What is God like? What is God made of, if He is not made of anything?

The monks gave him the best answers they had. Thomas listened, and he nodded, and he went away and thought.

And then, a little while later, he came back and asked again.

He was not being naughty. He was the kind of boy who, when he was given a good answer, wanted to walk all the way round it and look at it from every side.

Thomas grew. He grew tall, and then taller, and then broad and heavy, like a big oak tree. He went to school in the city of Naples, beside the sea. And there he met some friars who wore white robes with black cloaks. They were called the Dominicans. They owned nothing of their own. They walked everywhere. And they spent their lives studying God's Word and teaching it to anyone who would listen.

Thomas thought, "That is what I want to do. I want to spend my whole life on my question."

So he asked to become a friar, and they said yes.

But his family said no.

His mother had hoped Thomas would one day be the abbot of Monte Cassino, the head of the whole great abbey, which was a very important thing to be. A poor friar who walked about in a borrowed cloak was not what she had in mind at all.

So when Thomas set off along the road to go and study, his big brothers, who were soldiers, rode after him and caught him and brought him home. And they kept him there, at the family castle, for about a year.

What do you think Thomas did, all that long year at home?

He was patient. He was kind to his sisters, and it is said he taught one of them about God. He read the Bible from beginning to end. He read every book he could get. And he kept on asking his question, quietly, inside.

At last his family saw that Thomas would not change his mind. They let him go, and he went back to the friars, and he was very glad.

The friars sent Thomas to study in the great cities of Paris and Cologne, where the best teachers were. In Cologne his teacher was a wise friar named Albert, who loved to look closely at everything: stones and birds and beetles and people.

Thomas sat in the classroom and listened. He hardly said a word. He was so big and so quiet that the other students gave him a nickname. They called him "the Dumb Ox." In those days dumb meant someone who does not speak.

But you may know already what Albert said about that. He said that one day this ox would bellow so loudly with his teaching that the whole world would hear.

And the whole world did.

Thomas became a teacher. He taught in Paris and in Rome and in Naples. And he wrote books. Big books. Long books. Books with thousands and thousands of questions in them, every one of them a little piece of his first question, What is God?

He wrote each question very carefully. First he wrote down all the reasons someone might give for the wrong answer, fairly and politely, as if they were his friends' reasons. Then he thought about them. And then he gave his own answer, and showed, one by one, where each wrong reason had slipped.

He worked so hard and thought so deeply that sometimes he forgot where he was. People said he could talk to three or four helpers at once, each one writing a different book while Thomas walked up and down and said the words.

Thomas did not only write hard books. He wrote songs, too.

The Pope asked him to write the prayers and hymns for a new feast day, a day to thank Jesus for giving us Himself in Holy Communion. And Thomas, the big quiet friar, wrote words so beautiful that people have been singing them ever since.

If you go to church when the Blessed Sacrament is set out on the altar for everyone to adore, which is called Benediction, you may hear people singing the Tantum ergo, or the O salutaris. Those are Thomas's words. The boy who asked "What is God?" grew up to sing his answer.

It is told that one day, near the end of his life, Thomas was praying alone in a chapel, in front of a cross. And he heard, in his heart, Jesus speaking to him.

"Thomas, you have written well of me. What would you like as your reward?"

Thomas could have asked for anything. To finish all his books. To be famous. To have every one of his questions answered.

But Thomas said, "Nothing but you, Lord."

Not long after, Thomas stopped writing. He said that compared with what God had shown him, all the words he had written seemed like straw. He was not sad about his books. He knew they were good. He had simply begun to see that God is much, much bigger than any book, even a very big one.

Thomas died a few months later, at an abbey called Fossanova, on his way to a great meeting of the Church. He was not quite fifty years old.

Today we call him Saint Thomas Aquinas. Students and teachers and schools all over the world ask him to pray for them.

Now it is nearly time to sleep. Let us think about Thomas.

When Thomas was small, he asked a big question: What is God? And he never stopped asking it. He asked it slowly. He asked it carefully. He listened to everyone's answers, even the ones he thought were wrong, and he was fair to them.

And at the end, the answer he wanted most was not a word at all. It was God Himself.

You can ask big questions too. You can ask them at bedtime, or in the car, or at the kitchen table. God is not tired of your questions. He likes them.

And when you have asked all your questions, you can say what Thomas said: "Nothing but you, Lord."

Goodnight.

A prayer to say together

Saint Thomas, you asked, "What is God?"
and you never stopped wondering.
Help me to ask good questions,
to listen kindly to every answer,
and to love God more than anything.
Keep us all safe tonight.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Saint Thomas Aquinas was born about 1225 (some sources give 1224 or 1226) at Roccasecca, his family's castle near Aquino, between Rome and Naples, the youngest son of Landulf and Theodora of Aquino. At about five he was given as an oblate to the Benedictine abbey of Monte Cassino, where he stayed until about 1239, when the war between the Emperor Frederick II and the Pope reached the abbey and he was sent to study at Naples. There, about 1244, he joined the Dominican friars. His family, who had hoped he would rise in the Benedictine world (an uncle had been abbot of Monte Cassino), had his brothers seize him on the road and hold him at home for about a year, at Roccasecca and nearby Monte San Giovanni Campano. Released in 1245, he studied at Paris and then, from 1248, at Cologne under Saint Albert the Great. He taught at Paris, at several houses in Italy and at Naples, and wrote the Summa contra Gentiles, the unfinished Summa theologiae and many commentaries. He died on 7 March 1274 at the Cistercian abbey of Fossanova, on his way to the Second Council of Lyon. Pope John XXII canonized him in 1323, Pope Pius V declared him a Doctor of the Church in 1567, and Pope Leo XIII named him patron of Catholic schools and universities in 1880. His feast was 7 March, the day of his death; the 1969 reform of the General Roman Calendar moved it to 28 January, the day his relics were brought to Toulouse in 1369, so that it falls outside Lent. It is an obligatory memorial.

"What is God?" comes from the early Life of Thomas by William of Tocco, a Dominican who knew him and wrote within about fifty years of his death (around 1318 to 1323). Tocco says the boy at Monte Cassino often asked his teacher what God is. We have told it as "it is told", and the scenes of him asking on the stairs and at the window are ours.

The year at home is in the early Lives and the canonization testimony. Tocco also says Thomas taught one of his sisters during that time, which we have kept as "it is said". We have left out a further story from the same sources, of his brothers trying to tempt him from his vow.

The Dumb Ox is also from Tocco: fellow students at Cologne called the silent Thomas a dumb ox, and Albert, having heard him answer a hard question, said this ox would one day bellow so loudly in his teaching that the whole world would hear. It is told at more length in our story Albert and the Ostriches. It is a remembered saying in a common paraphrase.

His method of stating the objections first, then his answer, then a reply to each objection, is how every article of the Summa theologiae is built. That he could dictate to three or four secretaries at once on different subjects is reported by his early biographers and in the canonization inquiry; we have told it as "people said".

The hymns: Thomas composed the office for the feast of Corpus Christi, which Pope Urban IV established for the whole Church in 1264. Its hymns include Pange lingua, whose last two verses are the Tantum ergo, and Verbum supernum prodiens, whose last two verses are the O salutaris hostia; both are sung at Benediction. The attribution to Thomas is old and widely accepted, though scholars have debated how much of the office is his.

"Nothing but you, Lord" (in Latin usually given as Non nisi te, Domine) is told by Tocco and in the canonization testimony as happening at Naples, where a brother saw Thomas at prayer before a crucifix and heard a voice. It is a much-loved tradition, not something we can check, and we have told it as "it is told". "Like straw": on or about 6 December 1273, after something that happened at Mass, Thomas stopped writing, and his companion Bartholomew of Capua testified at the canonization inquiry that Thomas told his companion Reginald of Piperno that all he had written seemed like straw compared with what he had seen.

What is ours: the shape and wording of every scene and conversation, the picture of the oak tree, the questions he did not ask, the closing reflection and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

Louis and the Oak at Vincennes

Catholic bedtime stories about the saints · France, in the summers of the 1250s and 1260s, in the wood of Vincennes just outside Paris, about seven hundred and seventy years ago · 9 min read · Written by Claude (AI) · CC BY-SA 4.0

A bedtime story about Saint Louis IX of France (1214 to 1270), the king whose friend Jean de Joinville wrote that on summer days after Mass he would sit against an oak in the wood of Vincennes and let anyone with a quarrel come and speak to him, with no guards in the way; who fed about a hundred and twenty poor people every day and often served them himself; and who told his friend that washing poor people's feet was not beneath a king, because God had done it. For ages 3 to 8, about nine minutes read aloud. His feast day is the 25th of August.

A long time ago, in a country called France, there lived a king called Louis.

He became king when he was only twelve years old. That is very young to be a king. So his mother, Queen Blanche, helped him look after the country until he was grown up.

Queen Blanche loved Louis very much. And she taught him the most important thing she knew. She said: "Louis, the worst thing in the whole world is not to be poor, or sick, or sad. The worst thing in the whole world is to turn your back on God." Louis never forgot it.

When Louis grew up, he was the king of all of France. He had a crown made of gold. He had a palace in Paris, and castles, and horses, and knights, and a great many servants. He had a wife called Marguerite, and lots of children.

But Louis did not think a king was for sitting on a throne. He thought a king was for looking after people. All the people. Not only the knights and the lords, but the farmers and the bakers and the washerwomen and the boys who looked after the pigs.

And the thing Louis cared about most was that people should be treated fairly.

Now, just outside Paris there was a big wood called Vincennes. It was full of tall trees, and in summer it was green and cool and full of birds.

On summer mornings, after Mass, King Louis liked to walk out into the wood. He did not wear his golden crown. He found a big oak tree, and he sat down on the grass and leaned his back against it. And he asked his friends to sit down on the grass around him.

And then anybody could come.

Anybody at all. There were no guards at the door, because there was no door. There was only the king, and the oak tree, and the grass.

People came walking out of Paris and out of the villages. Farmers came, and shopkeepers, and old ladies with sticks. They came because they had a quarrel, a muddle, a problem that nobody else had put right.

The king looked round at them all, and he said: "Is there anyone here who has a problem?"

And the people who had a problem stood up.

"One at a time," said the king kindly. "Everyone will have a turn."

One morning (this is our story about it, but it is the kind of thing the king listened to every summer) a woman stood up with a little boy holding her hand.

"Your Majesty," she said, "my husband sold our cow to a man at the market. The man said he would pay us at harvest time. Now it is harvest time, and he says he never had a cow from us at all."

Another man stood up at the back, looking cross. "I paid for that cow!" he said.

The king did not shout. He did not say, "I am the king, go away." He listened to the woman. Then he listened to the man. Then he asked his friends who knew all about the laws of France, two clever men called Peter and Geoffrey, to look into it carefully and put it right.

And the little boy looked up at the king sitting on the grass in the shade of the oak, and thought: he is a king, but he is sitting on the grass like me.

King Louis looked after poor people in another way too.

Every single day, wherever he was in France, he gave dinner to poor people. Not two or three. About a hundred and twenty! A hundred and twenty hungry people, every day, who came and sat down and were given good food to eat.

And very often the king did not just sit and watch. He stood up from his own table and carried the bowls and the bread to them himself, like a servant. The king of France, serving soup.

The king had a good friend called John. John was a knight, and he was brave and loyal and honest. He was a very good friend. But sometimes he still had a lot to learn.

One day the king asked him, "John, on the Thursday before Easter, do you wash the feet of poor people?"

On that Thursday, you see, we remember how Jesus knelt down and washed his friends' feet.

"No!" said John. "I don't think a knight should do that. It isn't proper!"

The king did not laugh at him. He said gently, "John, you should not think it is beneath you. God did it."

And John thought about that for a very long time. In fact he thought about it for the rest of his life, because when he was a very old man he wrote it all down in a book, so that we would know. That is how we know about the oak tree, and the dinners, and the feet.

When King Louis was older, he went on a long journey far away over the sea, and there he became very sick, and he died. His friends were very sad. But they said: our king has gone home to God.

And all over France people remembered him. They said: he was the king who sat on the grass and listened. He was the king who served the soup.

In Paris you can still see a church that King Louis built. It is called the Sainte-Chapelle, which means "the holy chapel". He built it to keep something very precious: a crown of thorns, which the people of Paris honour as the crown that was put on Jesus's head before he died.

Isn't that something? A king with a crown of gold, who built a beautiful church for a crown of thorns.

The walls of the Sainte-Chapelle are almost all windows. Tall, tall windows, made of glass in every colour: red and blue and gold and green. When the sun shines through them, the whole church is full of coloured light, falling on the floor, falling on the people, falling on everybody the same.

Tonight, as you lie in bed, think about King Louis under his oak tree.

Is there somebody who needs you to listen to them tomorrow? Is there somebody you could help, even if it feels a little bit beneath you, like carrying the plates, or tidying up somebody else's toys?

King Louis would say: that is what kings are for.

Let's say a little prayer:

Dear Jesus, you knelt down to wash your friends' feet.
Help me to listen to people, and to be fair,
and never to think that helping is beneath me.
Saint Louis, pray for us.
Amen.

For grown-ups: what is true in this story

Our main source is the Life of Saint Louis by Jean de Joinville (about 1224 to 1317), a knight of Champagne who went on crusade with the king, became his friend and counsellor, and dictated the book in old age at the request of Queen Jeanne of Navarre, finishing it in 1309. We checked the passages used here against the English translation by Ethel Wedgwood (The Memoirs of the Lord of Joinville, London, 1906, public domain) as quoted in online editions (the original web pages could not be opened from where this was written, so the wording was confirmed through search excerpts). The dates are the standard ones in every reference work on him and were written from memory; a reader who finds one wrong is asked to say so. Readers who want a modern translation can use Caroline Smith's (Penguin Classics, Chronicles of the Crusades, 2008). The story calls him "John" for small children; he is usually called Joinville.

Louis was born at Poissy on 25 April 1214 and became king on his father's death in November 1226, aged twelve. His mother, Blanche of Castile, governed as regent during his minority and again while he was on crusade. He married Marguerite of Provence in 1234; they had eleven children. Blanche's saying is reported by Joinville and repeated by the early lives in several forms, the best known being that she would rather see him dead than that he should commit a mortal sin. We have put the same teaching in words a small child can hear without the image of a dead son.

The oak. Joinville says that many times in summer the king would go and sit in the wood of Vincennes after Mass, lean against an oak and have his companions sit round him, and that all who had business came to speak to him "without hindrance of usher" or anyone else. He would ask whether anyone had a cause, have them speak in turn, and call on Peter of Fontaines and Geoffrey of Villette, men of law in his service, to deal with them. Joinville says he also did this in his garden in Paris. The quarrel about the cow is ours; Joinville gives no particular case.

The dinners. Joinville writes that from his youth the king was compassionate to the poor, that he "was accustomed to feed daily six score poor people at his table in whatever part of the kingdom he might be", that the numbers were larger in Lent, and: "ofttimes I have seen him serve them himself, and from his own table." Six score is a hundred and twenty. The soup is ours.

The feet. Joinville records that the king asked him whether he washed the feet of the poor on Maundy Thursday (the 1906 translation says "Shrove Thursday"); Joinville answered no, that he thought it unseemly, and the king told him he ought not to despise it, "for God had done it". Joinville also tells, in the same part of the book, that the king asked him whether he would rather be a leper or commit a mortal sin; Joinville said he would rather commit thirty mortal sins than be a leper, and the king took him aside the next day and told him gently that no leprosy is as foul as mortal sin. We left that one for older children. "He thought about it for the rest of his life" is our way of saying that he wrote both down, against himself, decades later.

The journey over the sea. Louis went on crusade twice. On the first (1248 to 1254) he was defeated and captured in Egypt in 1250 and ransomed; Joinville was with him. On the second he died of illness, probably dysentery, in camp near Tunis on 25 August 1270. The crusades were wars, and many people died in them; they are one quiet line in a bedtime story and are not told here as glorious. He was canonised by Pope Boniface VIII in 1297. His feast in the General Roman Calendar is 25 August, an optional memorial.

The Sainte-Chapelle. Louis acquired the relic venerated as the Crown of Thorns from the Latin emperor of Constantinople, Baldwin II, in 1239, and built the Sainte-Chapelle on the Île de la Cité to keep it; it was consecrated in 1248. Its upper chapel is famous for walls that are almost entirely stained glass. The relic is now kept by Notre-Dame de Paris (it was carried out of the cathedral during the fire of 2019). We have told it as what Paris venerates; its history before the thirteenth century cannot be traced with certainty. "A crown of gold and a crown of thorns" is our sentence.

Left out on purpose: the wars of his reign, his laws against blasphemy and the harsh measures of his reign against the Jews of France, including the burning of the Talmud in Paris in 1242. These are part of an honest account of his life, and grown-ups reading about him will meet them; they do not belong in a story for small children about listening and serving.

What is ours: the shape and wording of every scene, all speech except the phrases quoted above in the note, the cow, the little boy, the soup, the talk before sleep and the prayer. The prayer is not an approved text.

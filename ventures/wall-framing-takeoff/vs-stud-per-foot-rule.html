Wall framing takeoff vs. a stud-per-foot rule of thumb

Two common ways to size a framing order are one stud per linear foot of wall plus plates, and a spreadsheet formula that adds studs at the spacing plus an allowance per opening. This page sets both against a counted order, in lineal feet of 2x4 and 2x6 with 10% waste on every column, from allowance-bench.mjs in the tool's repository (measured 2026-10-28 to 2026-11-02).

The per-foot figure is the same at every spacing, so a crew framing at 24 inches over-orders by about a quarter, and one framing at 12 inches (heavy loads, some high-wind designs) comes up 5 to 8% short. The Open House Plans 1 house shows the same pattern: -8.1%, +1.9%, +9.7% and +16.7% at 12, 16, 19.2 and 24 inches.

What the tool does that a rule cannot

  • Counts every king, jack, header, rough sill and cripple from the opening sizes you type, and refuses openings that do not fit.
  • Adds corner and tee backing from how the walls meet, and puts plate splices over studs.
  • Orders boards with a cutting-stock solution (within 0.02% of its linear-programming bound on whole houses) so short pieces come out of long boards.
  • Prints the cut list and shop drawings, and checks each wall against its own layout.

What this does not show

Two generated plans and one invented house, not a builder's real order or a permit set. The rules are the author's reading of common practice, not a published standard. Header sizes and spacing are your inputs and are not checked against a span table or code. A real plan with the builder's actual order would settle how much over-ordering is typical.

Common questions

Is the one-stud-per-foot rule accurate?

Only near 16 inches on center. On the three test houses it ordered 1.9 to 10.2% more lumber than a counted order at 16 in, 9.7 to 26.6% more at 19.2 or 24 in because it does not change with spacing, and 4.9 to 8.1% less at 12 in.

Does a spreadsheet formula beat the rule of thumb?

A formula that follows the spacing (studs at the spacing plus one, four more per opening, two per corner and tee, three plates) tracks the spacing but still ordered 16 to 29% more than the counted order on the same houses, mostly from the flat allowance per opening and the plate count.

What does the takeoff tool count that a rule does not?

Every king, jack, header, cripple and rough sill from the opening sizes you type, corner and tee backing, plate splices that land on studs, and a cutting-stock order that cuts short pieces from the offcuts of long boards.

Can I trust these percentages for my own house?

No. They come from two generated plans and one invented house, not from a builder's real order. They show how the rules behave, and a real plan with the builder's actual order would settle it.

How many bundles of shingles for a roof, counted strip by strip

The usual way to order shingles is the roof's area in squares (100 sq ft each), times 3 bundles a square, plus 10% for a gable roof or 15% for a hip. This page checks that rule by laying out every strip: 16 rectangular roofs (four house sizes at four pitches), each as a gable and as a hip, the field shingles laid course by course from the eave up and cut at the rakes and the hips. It counts the strips two ways, with every cut-off piece thrown away and with cut-offs used again, and compares the bundles with what the rule orders. The figures come from shingle-bench.mjs in the tool's repository (measured 2026-11-28).

How the strips were laid

  • The strip: a 39-3/8 x 13-1/4 in laminated (architectural) strip at 5-5/8 in exposure, 22 strips a bundle, 3 bundles a square. These are typical figures, not one maker's product.
  • Courses: from the eave up, as many as the slope length needs at that exposure; the top course is cut down and covered by the cap.
  • Offsets: each course starts 6 in further along than the one below, repeating every 6 courses, so the first strip of a course is cut 0 to 30 in short.
  • Ends: a gable's courses are cut square at each rake. A hip's courses are cut along the hip line at both ends, and every course is shorter than the one below.
  • Offcuts thrown: every cut piece comes from a new strip. Offcuts reused: a short piece is cut from the shortest left-over piece it fits anywhere on the roof before a new strip is opened, and a left-over piece counts only its shorter edge, so the wedge a hip cut leaves is lost. Real crews sit between the two.

What the 16 roofs show

  • Gable: 5.4 to 11.1% over the surface with offcuts thrown away, 7.6% on average; 1.0 to 4.2% reused, 2.2% on average. The rule's 10% never ran short: it ordered 0 to 6 bundles more than the strips thrown away needed, and 3 to 10 more than reused.
  • Hip: 10.4 to 18.4% with offcuts thrown away, 14.7% on average; 2.1 to 4.9% reused, 3.4% on average. The rule's 15% ran 1 bundle short on 2 of the 16 when nothing was reused, and ordered 3 to 13 bundles more than the reused layout needed.
  • A hip's extra 5 points is about right. With offcuts thrown away the hip took about twice the gable's cutting (14.7% against 7.6% on average), because every course is cut at both ends along a hip.
  • Area alone is always short. Squares × 3 with no allowance ran short on all 16 roofs of both shapes when offcuts were thrown away (2 to 4 bundles on a gable, 5 to 8 on a hip), and even with every cut-off reused it was 1 bundle short on 3 gables and 14 hips.

So the rule's allowance is mostly cutting. What is left of it after the cutting, from 1 bundle short to 13 over on these roofs depending on the roof and the crew, is the only slack for starter shingles cut from field bundles, damaged bundles and mistakes. Valleys, dormers and cut-up roofs add cutting this page does not measure.

What this does not show

Invented rectangular houses (24 x 36 to 36 x 60 ft) with a 12 in overhang all round, not a builder's real roof or a roofer's order. No valleys, dormers, skylights, chimneys, vents or cross wings, and one strip size and offset pattern. Starter strip and hip and ridge cap are separate products and are not counted here; the roof calculator counts them from the eave, ridge and hip lengths. Your shingle maker's coverage, offset and installation instructions govern.

Common questions

How many bundles of shingles do I need per square?

Three bundles a square (100 sq ft of roof) is the usual figure for laminated architectural shingles, and it is what this page assumes: 22 strips of 39-3/8 x 13-1/4 in at 5-5/8 in exposure, about 33.8 sq ft a bundle. Some products pack 4 or 5 bundles a square; the bundle wrapper says which. The bundles for a roof are then its area in squares, times the bundles a square, plus an allowance for cutting, which is what the tables on this page measure.

What waste factor should I use for shingles on a gable roof and a hip roof?

On 16 rectangular test roofs at 4:12 to 10:12, laying every strip course by course, a gable needed 5.4 to 11.1% more shingle than its surface (7.6% on average) when every cut-off piece was thrown away, and 1.0 to 4.2% (2.2% on average) when cut-offs were used again. A hip needed 10.4 to 18.4% (14.7% on average) thrown away and 2.1 to 4.9% (3.4% on average) reused. So the usual 10% for a gable and 15% for a hip cover the cutting with room to spare, except on a hip where nothing is reused, which ran 1 bundle short on 2 of the 16.

Why does a hip roof waste more shingles than a gable?

A hip roof has four planes and every course on each is cut at both ends along a hip line, so it has about twice as many cut ends as a gable, which is cut only at its two rakes. Each angled cut also loses a wedge as tall as the strip. With the same pitch and overhang the two roofs have the same area, so the extra bundles are all cutting.

Can I order shingles from these percentages?

Not as a figure to order by. They are invented rectangular houses with a 12 in overhang and no valleys, dormers, skylights, chimneys or vents, and a typical strip and offset pattern rather than your product's. Starter strip and hip and ridge cap are separate products and are not in these counts. Measure your own roof, use your shingle maker's coverage and instructions, and keep an allowance for damaged bundles and mistakes.

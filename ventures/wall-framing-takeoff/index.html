Questions

How many studs do I need for a wall?

Lay the wall out rather than divide: one stud at each end, one centred on every 16 inches (or 24) in between, then two king studs and the jacks for every door and window, the backing at corners and where other walls meet, and cripples above headers and under sills. The common rule of one stud per foot of wall is a rough allowance; this tool counts the actual members.

Where does the first stud go when laying out 16 inches on center?

The end stud sits flush with the end of the plate. The second stud's leading edge is at 15-1/4 inches, so its centre is at 16 and a 4 ft sheet that starts at the wall end breaks on the centre of a stud. Every later stud is 16 inches further on. The layout marks here are the left edge of each member, measured from the wall start.

Why is a precut stud 92-5/8 inches long?

A 92-5/8 inch stud with one bottom plate and two top plates of 1-1/2 inches each makes a 97-1/8 inch wall, which leaves room for an 8 ft ceiling sheet plus clearance. Precuts of 104-5/8 and 116-5/8 inches do the same for 9 and 10 ft walls.

How long are jack studs, headers and cripples?

A jack stud runs from the top of the bottom plate to the underside of the header, so it is the header height less 1-1/2 inches. A header is the rough opening width plus 1-1/2 inches for every jack on each side. Top cripples fill the space between the header and the top plate; bottom cripples fill the space between the bottom plate and the rough sill. The tool works each one out from your opening sizes.

Where should top plate joints go?

Over the centre of a full-length stud, and the cap plate's joints away from the top plate's. The International Residential Code (section R602.3.2 in recent editions) asks for end joints in double top plates to be offset at least 24 inches; check the edition your jurisdiction adopted. The tool places every plate joint on a stud and keeps the cap plate joints at least the distance you set from the top plate joints.

How does it choose which board lengths to buy?

It solves a cutting-stock problem for each lumber size over the lengths and prices you list: column generation gives a lower bound on the cost and a near-cheapest order, first-fit-decreasing is kept as a fallback, and small groups are solved exactly. The page shows the order's cost next to the bound, so you can see how close it is to the cheapest possible.

Does it size headers or check the building code?

No. Header size, plies and jack count are inputs. Nothing here checks them against a span table, a load or a code, and nothing here replaces your plans, your engineer or your building department.

Not modelled yet: bracing, rough sills doubled under wide windows unless you set it in a plan file, header spacers, hardware and nails, walls with a window inside the gable triangle, and walls with steps in height. The example house is invented.

Water supply pipe sizing

How big must each water pipe in a house be? List the kinds of fixture (flow while running, minutes per use, uses in the busiest hour), then the pipe runs as a tree from the meter: each run's length, rise, the equivalent length of its fittings, and the fixtures at its end. For every run you get the design flow (the flow not exceeded 99% of the busy hour, Hunter's rule computed exactly), the smallest pipe within your velocity limit, and the pressure left at its end; runs are made bigger where a fixture would fall below the pressure you need. Nothing leaves your browser.

Fixture types

The example numbers are illustrative, not measured; type your own. A fixture's busy fraction is minutes per use x uses per hour / 60.

Pipe runs

One run has no parent: the run from the meter. Fixtures are typed as type:count, e.g. lav:2, wc:1.

How it is worked out

  
    
    
  1. Busy fraction. Each fixture is open with probability p = minutes per use x uses per peak hour / 60, independently of the others (Hunter, 1940).
    2. 
    
  2. Design flow. For each run, the fixtures it feeds are added up downstream. The total flow is a sum of one binomial per fixture type; its distribution is built exactly by convolution on a 0.01 gpm grid, and the design flow is the smallest flow with at least your chosen probability (99% by default). The all-on flow and the normal approximation are shown beside it for comparison.
    3. 
    
  3. Pipe loss. Darcy-Weisbach with the Colebrook-White friction factor, water viscosity at your temperature, bores from the tube's outside diameter and wall (copper K, L, M; PEX SDR 9 and CPVC SDR 11 at minimum wall). Height costs 0.433 psi per foot of rise.
    4. 
    
  4. Size. Each run starts at the smallest size within the velocity limit. While any fixture would get less than the pressure you need, the run on its path that buys the most pressure per foot of pipe made bigger goes up one size.

Questions

  
How many gallons per minute does a two-bath house need?

  
In the worked example below (two baths, kitchen, dishwasher, washer and one hose bibb: 11 fixtures) every fixture running at once would draw 27.2 gpm, but under Hunter's rule, with the busy-hour use you type, the flow is at or below 12.2 gpm 99% of the time, so the main is sized for 12.2 gpm. In copper type L that is 8.1 ft/s in 3/4 in pipe, just over an 8 ft/s limit, so the main comes out 1 in. Your own uses per hour change the answer; type them.

  
Why not add up every fixture's flow?

  
Fixtures are rarely all open together. Roy Hunter (National Bureau of Standards, 1940) treated each fixture as busy a fraction of the peak hour, p = minutes per use x uses per hour / 60, independently of the others, and sized for the flow not exceeded 99% of the time. This page computes that rule exactly, by convolving one binomial per fixture type, instead of reading it off a fixture-unit table.

  
Is the normal approximation close enough?

  
Not for one house. For one bathroom (two sinks at p 0.1, a toilet, a shower) the exact 99% flow is 5 gpm and the normal approximation (mean plus 2.33 standard deviations) gives 4.11 gpm, 18% low. In the worked example the approximation gives 10.76 gpm for the main, which fits 3/4 in copper L under 8 ft/s, while the exact 12.2 gpm needs 1 in. With 64 such bathrooms the two differ by about 1.4%.

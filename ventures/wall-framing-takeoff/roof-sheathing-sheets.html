How many sheets of plywood for a roof: counted course by course, not area / 32

The usual way to order roof sheathing is the roof's area divided by 32 square feet (one 4x8 sheet of OSB or plywood) plus 10% for waste. Sheets are not laid by area, though: they go on in 4 ft courses from the eave up, joints staggered, and the last course at the ridge is whatever width is left over. This page counts the sheets that way on 20 gable roofs and sets the count beside the rule, with roof-sheathing-bench.mjs in the tool's repository, which runs the same engine as the roof calculator (measured 2026-11-28).

Count the sheets for your own roof

Step 1: the roof's area from its footprint

Measure the footprint out to the edge of the overhangs, not to the walls, then multiply by the pitch factor. On a roof with one pitch everywhere the result is exact for gable, hip and L-shaped roofs alike, because every plane rises at the same rate.

Each plane is laid in courses 4 ft wide. Within a course, pieces are cut from sheets by the same cutting-stock solver the takeoff uses for lumber, so an offcut at one end of a course starts the next and the end joints stagger. The top course is ripped; when two strips fit in one sheet's width, both are used.

What the counts show

  • The rule usually over-orders. In 16 of the 20 roofs, area / 32 + 10% bought 1 to 8 sheets more than the count; the counted waste on those was 3.1 to 7.9%, well under the 10% the rule assumes.
  • A top course just over 2 ft wide is the expensive one. The 4 roofs where the rule ran short (by 1 to 3 sheets) all had a top course between 24.3 and 30.4 in wide. A strip that wide cannot share a sheet with another, so close to half of each of those sheets is offcut, and the counted waste was 10.7 to 13.3%.
  • The fix is in the layout, not the factor. Before ordering, divide the plane's sloped length (eave edge to ridge) by 4 ft and look at the remainder. Under 2 ft, two rips come out of each sheet; between 2 and about 2-1/2 ft, every rip takes a whole sheet, so count one sheet per rip instead of trusting the 10%.

What this does not show

Twenty generated rectangular gable roofs, not real houses or a builder's order. Hip roofs, valleys, dormers, skylights, chimneys and ridge vents are left out; on a hip roof the triangular offcuts at the hips change the waste, and the roof calculator's hip count does not reuse them, so it would overstate it. The count does not place end joints over rafters or trusses, does not add sheets for damage or culls, and does not check the sheathing's span rating, thickness, nailing or clips, which come from the sheathing maker, the truss or rafter design and your building department. What the hip offcuts cost, thrown away and reused, is measured on the hip vs. gable waste factor page.

Common questions

How many sheets of plywood do I need for my roof?

Take the roof's sloped area (the footprint with its overhangs, times the pitch factor below) and divide by 32 square feet for a 4x8 sheet. Then count the courses: each plane takes its sloped length divided by 4 ft in rows, and a top row just over 2 ft wide wastes about half of every sheet it is ripped from. On 20 generated gable roofs, area divided by 32 plus 10% was 1 to 8 sheets more than a course-by-course count in 16 cases and 1 to 3 sheets short in the other 4.

What is the pitch factor for a 6/12 roof?

1.118. The pitch factor is the square root of 1 plus (rise / 12) squared: the sloped length of roof per foot of level run. Multiply the roof's footprint, overhangs included, by it to get the sloped area; 1,000 square feet of plan at 6/12 is 1,118 square feet of roof.

Is 10% enough waste for roof sheathing?

Usually more than enough on a plain gable roof, and sometimes not. Counted course by course, sheathing waste on the 20 roofs was 3.1 to 7.9% in 16 cases and 10.7 to 13.3% in the 4 whose top course came out between 24.3 and 30.4 in wide, too wide to take two strips from one sheet. In those 4 the 10% rule ran 1 to 3 sheets short.

Does this work for hip roofs?

Not as a measurement. The roof calculator counts a hip roof's courses without reusing the triangular offcuts at the hips, so its hip count is an upper bound and is left out of this page. Hips, valleys, dormers and skylights all add cut waste that a plain gable roof does not have.

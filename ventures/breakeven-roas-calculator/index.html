Whole-business check (MER)

MER vs platform ROAS

Platform ROAS is the purchase value an ad platform credits to its own ads divided by what you spent there. Two platforms can both claim the same sale, and view-through and returning-customer credit inflate it. MER (marketing efficiency ratio) is all your store revenue divided by all your ad spend; it cannot double count, but it includes organic and repeat sales, so it flatters any single campaign.

Use platform ROAS to compare campaigns inside one account. Use MER to decide whether the business as a whole is paying for its advertising, and compare both with the break-even above. If MER is comfortably above break-even while platform ROAS is below it, attribution is probably undercounting; if platform ROAS is above break-even but MER is below it, the platforms are probably claiming sales you would have made anyway.

How it is calculated

Reported revenue R = price × (1 − discount) + shipping charged. A share of orders is refunded in full, so kept revenue is R × (1 − return rate). The platform fee is charged on kept revenue; the payment fee (% of R plus a fixed amount) is charged on every order and is not returned on a refund. Cost of goods is spent on every order, less the share of returned units you can resell; shipping is paid on every order and return shipping on refunded ones.

Contribution = kept revenue − platform fee − payment fee − cost of goods − shipping. Max CPA = contribution. Break-even ROAS = R ÷ contribution. For a margin target m, max CPA = contribution − m × R and target ROAS = R ÷ that. On the LTV basis a customer places n orders; each of the n − 1 repeat orders adds the repeat-order share of first-order contribution, and the margin is measured on n × R. The platform still reports only the first order, so LTV ROAS is R ÷ lifetime contribution.

Common questions

What is break-even ROAS?

Break-even ROAS is the return on ad spend at which an ad campaign earns exactly zero profit after the product, shipping, fees and refunds. It equals the revenue per order divided by the contribution per order, so a product that keeps 40 cents of every reported dollar before ads breaks even at 2.5x. Below it each ad dollar loses money on the first order; above it the campaign is profitable.

How do I calculate maximum CPA?

Maximum cost per acquisition is the contribution per order: the price after discounts, minus refunds, platform and payment fees, cost of goods and shipping. You can spend up to that much to win one order and still break even. Spend less to leave profit; to hit a margin target subtract the profit you want per order.

What ROAS do I need for a 20% profit margin?

Work out the contribution per order, subtract 20% of the revenue the platform reports for that order, and divide the reported revenue by what is left. The scenario table does this for 0%, 10%, 20% or any margins you type. If your contribution margin is under 20% the target cannot be reached at any ROAS and the table says so.

Should I include repeat purchases and lifetime value?

If customers reliably come back, yes, because the first order does not have to repay the whole ad cost. Enter the average total orders per customer over the period you trust and what a repeat order earns compared with the first. The tool shows both views: the first-order break-even that protects cash, and the LTV break-even that shows the most you could pay if repeat behaviour holds. Be conservative with repeat rates you have not measured.

What is the difference between MER and platform ROAS?

Platform ROAS is revenue the ad platform attributes to its own ads divided by what you spent there. MER, marketing efficiency ratio, is all store revenue divided by all ad spend across every channel, including organic and repeat sales. MER is the steadier measure of whether the business as a whole is paying for its advertising; platform ROAS is useful to compare campaigns inside one account but is inflated by attribution overlap and view-through credit.

Why does my return rate matter so much for ROAS?

A refunded order gives the revenue back but the ad money, the outbound shipping and usually the payment fee are already gone. A product with 30% returns needs a much higher ROAS than the same product with 5%, and the sensitivity table shows how fast break-even climbs as returns and cost of goods move.

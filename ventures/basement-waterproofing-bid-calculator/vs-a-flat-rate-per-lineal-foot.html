This calculator vs. a flat rate per lineal foot

"$150 a lineal foot" is not a price for a waterproofing job — it is a price for one specific basement's wells, sump, cracks, backup, and discharge, quietly applied to every job that calls in.

What a flat rate per lineal foot is

A flat rate per lineal foot of perimeter is the fastest way to quote: multiply the wall length by a number and read off a price. Customers shop it because it is easy to compare between bids, and it is the number a homeowner holding a competitor's quote will ask you to match.

The number behind it was set for one basement — one wall height, an existing sump, no window wells, no crack injection, no backup pump, a short discharge run — and then reused for every job afterward, whether or not the next basement looks anything like the one the rate was built on.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the waterproofing system, perimeter and wall height, window wells, floor and wall crack injection, whether the sump is new or existing, the pump and backup pump, discharge run and whether it needs freeze protection, crew wage, material costs, and target margin. It prices wells, cracks, the pit, the backup, and discharge separately from wall and floor membrane, and returns a price above your target margin — for this basement, not a generic one.

What a flat rate per lineal foot doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same $/lf whether the basement has zero window wells and an existing sump or four wells and a brand-new pit. Prices window wells, and a new sump pit versus an existing one, as their own labor and material lines, so a well-heavy job never prices off the same number as a well-free one.
Prices "the perimeter" as one lineal-foot figure, with no separate line for floor or wall crack injection. Charges crack injection by count and length, so a job with 12 lf of floor cracks and two wall-crack injections costs more than one with none.
Has no line for a backup pump or a freeze-proof discharge run, regardless of how far north the basement is or how the homeowner wants to be protected. Prices the pump, backup pump, and discharge — including a freeze-proof kit — directly, so a job needing a battery backup and 60 feet of freeze-protected discharge costs more than one needing neither.
An 80 lf finished basement with an existing sump and a 55 lf seepage house with four wells, a new pit, a battery backup, and freeze-proof discharge get quoted off the same $/lf number — and the shorter, harder job would be quoted for less. The brochure job and the seepage house price on their own wells, cracks, sump, backup, and discharge — not on perimeter alone — so the shorter, harder job prices higher, correctly.
Gives the estimator a number to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the price and the price per lineal foot, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before the contractor commits to it.

Common questions

Why does a flat rate per lineal foot get a waterproofing quote wrong?

Because perimeter footage is not the cost driver. Window wells, a new sump pit versus an existing one, floor and wall crack injection, a battery or water-powered backup pump, and a freeze-proof discharge run all cost real crew hours and material that do not scale with lineal feet of perimeter. A shorter perimeter with more of that scope can cost more than a longer, simpler one.

What does a flat rate usually leave out entirely?

Window wells, sump and backup-pump work, crack injection, and discharge. A job with four window wells, a new pit, a battery backup, and 60 feet of freeze-protected discharge needs real labor and material for each of those, and a per-lineal-foot number has no line for any of them.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

In the worked example on this page, applying an 80 lf brochure job's own flat rate per lineal foot to a 55 lf seepage house undercharges that job by roughly 81% — using this calculator's own verified defaults, not a cherry-picked case.

Is a flat rate per lineal foot ever the right way to quote?

For a narrow band of near-identical jobs — similar wall height, an existing sump, no wells or crack injection, similar discharge — a flat rate can land close. The risk is applying it outside that band without noticing, because the rate does not know how much of that scope a given basement needs until it is actually measured.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local rates or what competitors charge. It takes the wage, material, and overhead numbers you enter and turns them into a price for the perimeter, wells, cracks, sump, backup, and discharge you describe — the arithmetic a flat rate skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat rate per lineal foot is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works until the basement stops matching the one the rate was set on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it.

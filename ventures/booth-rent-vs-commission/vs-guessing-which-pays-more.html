This calculator vs. guessing which pays more

Most stylists weigh a booth-rent offer the way it's pitched to them: roughly half the ticket to the salon, or a flat rent check. This page is about what changes when card fees, backbar, the retail split, and the unpaid hours each path adds are counted instead of eyeballed.

What each approach is

Guessing at the chair means comparing two round numbers in your head: "the salon keeps about half" against "the rent is $275 a week" -- without separating retail from service, without counting card fees or backbar product against the booth side, and without weighing either path's unpaid hours. It costs nothing and takes no time, which is exactly why it's the default most stylists run before ever opening a calculator.

This calculator takes the same book -- average ticket, clients per week, retail sales, and either a commission split or a booth-rent lease with its own card fees, backbar, retail cost, and other weekly cost -- and computes weekly, monthly, and annual take-home under each path, the take-home per hour once unpaid laundry and booking time is counted, the break-even client count, and the maximum rent that still matches the current split. Nothing is guessed; every figure comes from the numbers entered.

The gap is not rounding error. On this calculator's own shipped example -- an $85 average ticket, 14 clients a week, $80 a week in retail, a 50% service commission with a 10% retail commission, and a $275 weekly booth rent -- the napkin guess puts booth take-home at $995 a week (gross minus rent alone) against commission's $635 (half of everything, retail included), a $360 gap favoring booth. Run through this calculator, booth's real take-home is $779.17 once $36.83 in card fees, $119 in backbar, $40 in retail cost, and $20 in other weekly cost are counted, and commission's real take-home is $603 once retail is split at its own 10% rather than the 50% service rate. The real gap is $176.17 a week -- $183.83 narrower than the napkin math showed.

Per hour, the picture changes again. Booth rent's extra take-home comes with 5 more unpaid hours a week than commission on this example -- 24.5 hours against 19.5, once laundry, booking, and backbar prep are counted. Divide take-home by hours actually worked and commission pays $30.92 an hour, booth pays $31.80 -- an $0.88-an-hour edge, not the $360-a-week gap the napkin guess implies.

Common questions

Why not just compare the split to the rent in my head?

Because the two numbers a chair-side napkin check compares -- roughly half the ticket to the salon, or a flat rent check -- are not what either path actually pays. The napkin commission guess applies the service split to the whole book, including retail, which is usually commissioned at a lower rate. The napkin booth guess treats rent as the only cost, leaving out card processing, backbar product, the wholesale cost of retail sold, and other weekly expenses. Neither guess counts the unpaid hours -- laundry, booking, backbar prep -- that booth rent usually adds and a commission chair usually does not.

How much difference does it actually make?

On this calculator's own shipped example -- an $85 average ticket, 14 clients a week, $80 a week in retail, a 50% service commission with a 10% retail commission, and a $275 weekly booth rent with its card fees, backbar, and other costs -- the napkin guess is booth $995 a week (gross minus rent) against commission $635 a week (half of everything), a $360 gap favoring booth. The real numbers, once card fees, backbar, retail cost, and the smaller retail commission are counted, are booth $779.17 against commission $603, a $176.17 gap -- less than half of what the napkin math showed.

What does the napkin math miss on the hourly number?

The hours. On the shipped example, booth rent's extra take-home comes with 5 more unpaid hours a week than commission -- 24.5 hours against 19.5 -- for laundry, booking, and backbar prep a salon used to absorb. Once take-home is divided by hours actually worked, commission pays $30.92 an hour and booth pays $31.80: a $0.88-an-hour edge, not the $360-a-week gap the napkin guess implies. A stylist deciding on the dollar figure alone and one deciding on the hourly figure can reach different answers from the same book.

Is this the same as asking another stylist what they're on?

No. Another stylist's split or rent was set against their own ticket average, client count, retail sales, and costs -- borrowing their number and applying it to a different book is a second kind of guess, not a measurement of this one. This calculator takes one stylist's own numbers -- their ticket, their clients, their rent offer, their costs -- and computes both paths from that book, rather than from what someone else's chair happens to pay.

What if I don't know my exact card fee or backbar cost yet?

A rough estimate from a recent statement or product order is still far better than skipping straight to the napkin split, because card fees, backbar, and retail cost are exactly the costs the napkin guess leaves at zero. Starting from an estimate and refining it later is still a computed number checked against this book, rather than a round split recalled from habit.

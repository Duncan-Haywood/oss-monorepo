This calculator vs. a flat $8-a-square-foot quote

$8 a square foot sounds like one number for one pool. It is a price for the water surface a homeowner can tape-measure — quietly charged against a plaster surface that is often more than double that, before a chip-out, a spa, or a foot of tile ever gets its own line.

What a flat $8-a-square-foot quote is

A flat rate per square foot is the fastest number a shop can put on a postcard, a Facebook ad, or a phone quote: one dollar figure, times the pool's length times width, read back as a total. Length times width is also the one measurement a homeowner can take themselves, which is why it is the number two competing bids get compared on.

The rate was set for one job — usually the simplest one a shop is willing to advertise: a rectangular pool, white plaster, no chip-out, drain and plaster over an existing bonded shell — and then reused for every replaster afterward, whether or not the next pool has a chip-out, a spa, waterline tile, or coping.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the pool's shape, length, width, and depths (or a freeform water surface and wall multiplier), the finish, the chip-out scope, a spa, steps or benches, waterline tile, coping, and whether the job includes a drain-and-refill and an acid wash, plus your loaded labor, material rates, and overhead. It prices the floor-plus-wall plaster surface, the finish, the chip-out, and every extra separately, and returns a walk-away price for the pool you describe — not a generic one.

What a flat rate per square foot doesWhat this calculator does instead
Prices the water surface (length × width), which is the floor only, even though the plaster crew coats the floor and the walls. Computes floor-plus-wall plaster surface from the shape, length, width, and average depth — more than double the water surface on the calculator's own rectangle presets.
Charges the same rate whether the finish is white plaster or pebble, even though pebble and glass-bead material and labor cost several times more per square foot. Prices the finish's own material cost and hours per plaster square foot from the selected finish, so a pebble or glass job costs more than a white-plaster overlay.
Has no line for a chip-out, so a full chip-out to gunite is quoted the same as draining and plastering over a bonded shell. Prices chip-out hours and dumpster or haul-off cost from the selected scope — none, partial, or full — against the actual plaster surface.
Has no line for a spa, steps, waterline tile, or coping, so a pool with all four is quoted the same per square foot as one with none. Prices spa hours, step or bench square footage, tile linear feet, and coping linear feet as their own hours and material, on top of the shell.
Bakes payroll burden into a round per-foot number nobody re-derives, so the rate quietly drifts from the crew's real loaded cost. Loads the entered hourly wage by the entered payroll-burden percentage every time, so labor cost tracks the actual burden rather than a memorized rate.
Gives the crew a rate to quote, not a number to check a quote against when a homeowner asks to match a cheaper flat-rate bid. Reports a walk-away price built from the entered scope, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost and target margin before the crew commits to it.

A flat rate per square foot is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when the number under it (the water surface) is not the number the crew is actually plastering. This calculator does not replace judgment about your market; it replaces the arithmetic a flat water-surface rate skips.

Common questions

Why does a flat $8-a-square-foot pool-plaster quote get the price wrong?

Because $8 a square foot times the water surface -- the number a homeowner can get with a tape measure -- prices the floor and skips the walls, even though the plaster crew coats both. On the calculator's own 16x32-rectangle preset with a 3.5-foot shallow end and an 8-foot deep end, the water surface is 512 square feet but the plaster surface -- floor plus walls -- is 1,064 square feet, more than double. A flat rate built for the number on the tape measure charges for less than half the surface the crew actually plasters.

What job is the $8-a-square-foot number actually built around?

The simplest one on the books: a rectangular pool, white plaster, no chip-out -- drain and plaster over an existing bonded shell. Even on that job, priced against this calculator's own defaults (a $36 crew wage, 30% payroll burden, 15% overhead, and a 26% target margin), a flat $8-a-square-foot water-surface quote comes in at $4,096.00 against a $5,791.23 break-even cost -- already $1,695.23 short before a chip-out, a spa, or a single foot of waterline tile enters the job.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

On this calculator's own shipped job presets, at the same defaults: the white-plaster brochure job comes in $1,695.23 under break-even at the flat $8/sf rate. An 18x36 quartz job with a partial chip-out and 70 linear feet of waterline tile prices the flat rate at $5,184.00 against a $13,704.56 break-even cost -- $8,520.56 short. A freeform pebble job with a full chip-out, an 80-square-foot spa, 90 linear feet of tile, and 90 linear feet of coping prices the flat rate at $4,960.00 against a $32,602.87 break-even cost -- $27,642.87 short. And a 20x40 glass-bead job with a full chip-out, a 64-square-foot spa, and coping prices the flat rate at $6,400.00 against a $43,454.06 break-even cost -- $37,054.06 short.

Is a flat rate per square foot ever the right way to quote a pool replaster?

Only as a rough first estimate for the plainest job on the list, and even there this calculator's own numbers show it falling short of break-even once a realistic wage, burden, and overhead are applied to the actual plaster surface. Applying that same per-water-square-foot number to a job with a chip-out, a spa, waterline tile, or coping charges for none of that scope, which is how a sold pebble job with a full chip-out becomes a loss after the first drain day.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local material or labor rates, and it is not a pool-structural assessment, a permit, or an engineering stamp. It takes the shape, finish, chip-out, spa, steps, waterline tile, coping, and drain-and-refill you enter, plus your own wage, payroll burden, overhead, and target margin, and turns that into a price for this job -- the arithmetic a flat per-square-foot quote skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat per-square-foot rate is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works only for the one job it was set on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it.

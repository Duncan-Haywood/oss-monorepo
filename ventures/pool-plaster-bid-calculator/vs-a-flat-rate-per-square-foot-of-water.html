This calculator vs. a flat rate per square foot of water

“$15 a square foot” is not a price for a pool — it is a price for one specific shell, finish, and scope, quietly charged again for a different job that only looks the same size from the deck.

What a flat rate per square foot of water is

It is the fastest way to quote a replaster: multiply the water surface — length times width, the number a homeowner can read off a tape measure without draining the pool — by a published rate. Homeowners shop it because it is easy to compare between bids, and it is the number a customer holding a postcard or a cheaper competitor's quote will ask you to match.

It is wrong because the plaster does not stop at the waterline on the floor — it goes up every wall too, and the walls are not part of the water-surface number at all. The rate also does not know which finish or which chip-out it was built for, so the same $-per-square-foot figure gets reused for a bond-coat overlay and a full gunite chip-out with a spa and tile alike.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the shell dimensions, finish, chip-out, spa, steps, waterline tile, coping, and your crew's rates. It converts the shape into a real plaster takeoff — floor plus walls, plus any spa and steps — and returns the price, the price per plaster square foot, the price per water square foot, and the walk-away number below which the job loses money.

Uses the same $/sq ft whichever finish is chosen, though pebble and glass finishes cost several times more per square foot than white plaster. Prices each finish from its own material cost and labor hours, so a pebble or glass job is billed for the upgrade it actually is.
Has no line for chip-out, so a full demolition to gunite is quoted the same as a bond-coat overlay. Adds chip-out hours and a dumpster line only for the square footage actually billed, separate from the plaster.
Folds a spa, waterline tile, and coping into the same flat rate, or leaves them out. Prices the spa, steps, waterline tile, and coping as their own lines, each visible instead of buried in a bigger, wrong number.
Gives the crew a rate to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the price, the price per plaster square foot, the price per water square foot, and the walk-away price, so a shopped rate can be tested against the real job before it goes out.

One rate, two very different pools

Using this calculator's own job presets. A 16×32 rectangle, white plaster, no chip-out — the brochure special — is 512 square feet of water and 1,064 square feet of plaster, and prices at $7,825.99, which works out to $15.29 per square foot of water.

A freeform pebble pool with a full chip-out to gunite, a spa, waterline tile, and coping — the loss job — is 620 square feet of water, not far off the brochure pool's 512. But it is 1,275 square feet of plaster once the walls, spa, and tile band are counted, with a far more expensive finish and a full demolition on top. Matching the brochure job's $15.29-a-square-foot rate to this pool's 620 square feet of water gives a quote of $9,476.79.

This calculator prices the same freeform pebble job at $44,057.94 — a real rate of $71.06 per square foot of water, 4.6 times the brochure job's rate. The flat water-surface quote covers about 21.5% of the real cost, a shortfall of $34,581.15. Nothing about the two pools' water-surface numbers — 512 versus 620, a modest difference — warns that one job is several times the other once the walls, the finish, the chip-out, and the added scope are priced.

Nobody is being careless by reaching for a $-per-square-foot rate first — it is the number that is easiest to compare between bids. The gap is everything a water-surface number cannot see: the walls, the finish, the chip-out, and the extras, which is exactly what this calculator prices and a rate copied from the last job cannot.

Common questions

Why does a flat rate per square foot of water surface get pool replastering wrong?

Because the plaster goes on the floor and the walls, and a flat water-surface rate only counts the floor -- length times width. On this calculator's own defaults, a 16×32 rectangle with a 3.5-foot shallow end and an 8-foot deep end is 512 square feet of water but 1,064 square feet of plaster, more than twice as much shell as the water-surface number counts.

How big is the gap in practice?

Using this calculator's own job presets: a 16×32 white-plaster job with no chip-out prices at $7,825.99, which is $15.29 per square foot of water. Applying that same $15.29 rate to a freeform pebble pool with a full chip-out, a spa, waterline tile, and coping -- 620 square feet of water but 1,275 square feet of plaster -- gives a quote of $9,476.79. This calculator prices that job at $44,057.94: the flat water-surface rate covers about 21.5% of the real cost, a $34,581.15 shortfall.

What does pricing by the water surface leave out entirely?

Everything that is not the floor: the walls, which scale with depth and perimeter rather than water area; the finish upgrade, since pebble and glass cost several times more per square foot than white plaster; the chip-out; the spa; waterline tile; and coping. A water-surface rate has no line for any of them, so a pool that looks like a similar size in the number a homeowner can see is priced the same as one that is a several-times larger job in the number that actually gets plastered.

Is pricing by the water surface ever close enough?

For a narrow band of near-identical jobs -- same finish, same chip-out, similar depth and shape -- a flat rate set for one job can land close on the next. The risk is applying a rate learned on a simple brochure job to a pool that looks like a similar size in water surface but carries a deeper shell, a pricier finish, a chip-out, or added scope like a spa or tile, because the water-surface number cannot tell you when that has happened.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local rates, material costs, or what competitors charge. It takes your shell dimensions, finish, chip-out, and crew rates and turns them into a price built from the actual plaster surface -- the arithmetic a flat water-surface rate skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat rate per square foot is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works until the next job stops matching the one the rate was set on.

How to quote sheet metal and laser cut parts

A sheet-metal quote comes from the flat pattern: how long the cut path is, how many times the head pierces, how many bends, and how many parts nest on a sheet you buy whole. Here is the method, a worked example at 10, 100 and 500 parts, and the mistakes that lose money on jobs you win.

The method in six steps

  1. Read the RFQ for what changes the price. Material, gauge and finish, quantities, hardware to insert, weld or powder-coat requirements, flatness and tolerance callouts, certifications and the delivery date. A missing gauge or grade is a question to the customer, not a guess.
  2. Measure the flat pattern. From the DXF: the blank's overall size, the total cut length (outline plus every hole and slot), the number of pierces (one per closed contour) and the bend lines. A STEP model of a bent part has to be unfolded first; quote from the flat.
  3. Nest the blank and count sheets. How many blanks fit on the sheet size you stock at the gap your material needs. Material is the number of sheets the lot uses, bought whole, unless you have a real use for the remnant.
  4. Time the cut at your own speed for that material and gauge. Cut minutes = cut length ÷ cutting speed, plus pierces × pierce time. Cutting speed changes several-fold with alloy and thickness, so one speed per machine is wrong for most parts; use your machine's cut chart.
  5. Time the bends and the rest. Seconds per bend times the bends, a brake setup per lot, programming and nesting once per job, deburring and inspection per part, each at that machine's or bench's own rate.
  6. Add outside processes, plan for scrap, add markup and quote every quantity. Powder coat and plating carry lot minimums; start enough parts to ship the order good; repeat the sum at each break, since sheets, setups and minimums spread differently at each.

A worked example

A mounting bracket in 14 ga (0.075 in) cold-rolled steel, laser cut, bent twice, deburred and powder coated by a vendor. Every number here is made up to show the arithmetic; they are not typical speeds, prices or rates for any machine, region or trade. Use your own.

  • Flat pattern (from the DXF): 6.3 × 9.1 in overall, 41.8 in of cut path, 7 pierces (the outline and six holes), 2 bend lines. Rounded up to the next 1/8 in, the blank is 6.375 × 9.125 in.
  • Material: 48 × 120 in sheet at $98.00 a sheet. A rectangular nest at a 0.25 in gap fits 90 blanks a sheet.
  • Fiber laser at $165.00 an hour: 160 in/min in this gauge and 0.8 s a pierce, so 41.8 ÷ 160 + 7 × 0.8 s = 0.355 min, rounded up to 0.36 min a part; 15 min to load the sheet program, plus a one-time 30 min to program and nest the job.
  • Press brake at $95.00 an hour: 2 bends × 9 s = 0.30 min a part, 20 min to set up the tooling and back gauge.
  • Bench at $65.00 an hour: deburr and inspect, 0.75 min a part.
  • Black powder coat (outside vendor): $2.75 a part, minimum $175.00 a lot.
  • Fallout: 2% of parts started are expected to scrap.
  • Markup: 20% on material, 15% on the outside service, none on labor (the machine rates are taken to hold the margin already); a $150.00 minimum per line.

What the table shows:

  • Small lots buy a whole sheet. 11 parts need one sheet that holds 90: the $98.00 sheet is the material charge whether you cut 11 blanks or 90. At 500 parts the same material is 6 sheets, and the last one carries only 61 parts.
  • The vendor's minimum decides the small lot. At 10 parts the powder coater's $175.00 lot minimum is 39% of the cost; at 500 the per-part charge has long passed it.
  • Fixed charges spread out. The same $155.42 of setup and programming, the first sheet and the coater's minimum are shared by 10, 100 or 500 parts. Together they are why the price each falls from $49.94 to $9.51 to $7.29, while the run time per part stays the same.
  • Laser time is short; it still adds up. 0.36 min a part is $0.99 at this rate. Leave out the pierces and the cut drops to 0.261 min; on thick plate, where a pierce takes seconds, the pierces can be most of the cut.

Mistakes that lose money on jobs you win

  • Pricing material by the part's area. You buy sheets. Unless the skeleton and remnant really go back on the rack and get used, the lot pays for the sheets it consumes.
  • One cutting speed for everything. A speed from thin mild steel will badly under-time stainless or thick plate. Keep speeds per material and gauge.
  • Forgetting pierces. A part with many small holes can spend more time piercing than cutting, especially in thicker material.
  • Leaving out programming and nesting. It is real time on every new part number, once per job, not per part.
  • Ignoring the brake setup. A new tool change and back-gauge setup is the same on ten parts or a thousand; price it once per lot.
  • Quoting from a 3D model nobody unfolded. The flat pattern sets the blank, the cut length and the bends; check the bend allowance your brake actually gives.

Doing it in Job Shop Quote

Job Shop Quote reads an ASCII DXF flat pattern attached to the RFQ by fixed rules in its own code, with no AI involved: the overall size, the cut length, the pierces and the bend lines drawn on a layer named for bends. On your rate card each machine can carry a cutting speed, a pierce time and seconds per bend, and a cutting machine can carry its own speed per material and gauge (typed, or imported from your cut chart as a spreadsheet). A quote line then starts with a "Cut from DXF" and a "Bend from DXF" operation timed exactly as above, a blank sized from the flat pattern and, for a material you buy by the sheet, the parts per sheet from a rectangular nest at your gap. Every figure shows the rate it came from, the estimator can change any of them, and nothing is suggested when the drawing was read with low confidence or two machines could run the job. The example above is priced to the cent the app gives for the same inputs, checked by a test in its code.

How to quote sheet metal and laser cut parts

A sheet metal quote is built from the flat pattern: how many parts fit a sheet, how long the cut path is, how many holes the laser pierces and how many bends the brake makes, then hardware, finishing and your markup at each quantity. Here is the method, a worked example at 10, 100 and 500 parts, and the mistakes that cost money on parts you win.

The method in seven steps

  1. Get the flat pattern. A DXF of the part flattened, or a 3D model you unfold. Its overall size sets the nest, its cut path the laser time, its closed contours the pierces and its bend lines the brake time. Check the gauge, the material, the bend radii and the tolerances on the drawing; anything missing is a question for the customer.
  2. Work out parts per sheet. Lay the flat pattern's rectangle out on the sheet size you buy, leaving the gap your laser needs between parts and at the sheet edge, and try it both ways round. The sheet's price divided by that count is the material in each part.
  3. Price the sheets you will open. Parts started divided by parts per sheet, rounded up. A small order still opens a whole sheet unless you keep the rest as stock and decide to charge less for it.
  4. Time the cut from the cut length and the pierces. Cut length ÷ cut speed for that material and thickness, plus one pierce for the outline and each hole at your pierce time. On a small part with many holes the pierces can be a large share of the cut time.
  5. Time the bends. Bends per part × seconds per bend at the brake, plus a setup for each tooling change and a first-piece check. Count bend lines, not parts.
  6. Add the hand work, hardware and finishing. Deburring, hardware insertion, welding and inspection are minutes per part on the bench. Press-in nuts and studs are bought per part; powder coat and plating vendors charge per part with a lot minimum.
  7. Plan for scrap, add markup, quote every quantity. Start enough parts to ship the order good, mark up material and bought-in services, apply your line minimum and work the whole sum at each quantity break.

A worked example

An electrical box mounting bracket in 14 ga (0.075 in) cold-rolled steel, laser cut, 3 bends, four press-in nuts and a powder coat from a vendor. Every number here is made up to show the arithmetic; they are not typical prices, speeds, times or rates for any region or trade. Use your own.

  • Flat pattern: 6.5 × 9.25 in, 62.4 in of cut path, 9 closed contours (the outline and 8 holes) and 3 bend lines.
  • Sheet: 48 × 120 in at $180.00 a sheet, 0.25 in between parts and at the edge. The flat rectangle fits 88 times (92% of the sheet), so each part carries $2.05 of steel when the sheets are full. A $40.00 material minimum applies.
  • Fiber laser at $180.00 an hour, cutting this gauge at 200 in/min with 0.5 s per pierce: 62.4 ÷ 200 + 9 × 0.5 s = 0.39 min a part, after 20 minutes to program and nest and a 10 minute setup.
  • Press brake at $95.00 an hour: 3 bends × 8 s = 0.4 min a part, 25 minutes to set up the tooling and check the first piece.
  • Bench at $70.00 an hour: 1.5 min a part to deburr, press in the nuts and inspect.
  • Press-in nuts, 4 per part: $0.72 a part, bought for the parts shipped.
  • Black powder coat (outside vendor): $4.50 a part, minimum $175.00 a lot.
  • Fallout 2%; markup 15% on material, hardware and the outside service (the machine rates already hold the margin); a $150.00 minimum per line.

What the table shows:

  • The nest is the material price. Area alone says 95 parts fit the sheet; the gap between parts and at the edge leaves 88. At 10 parts the order opens one whole sheet, so the steel is $18.00 a part instead of $2.05. At 500 parts, 511 starts need 6 sheets.
  • The laser is not the expensive part. Cutting takes 0.39 minutes a part, and the 9 pierces are 19% of it. The costliest minutes per part are on the bench: $1.75 a part for deburr, insert hardware, inspect. Hand work is where sheet metal quotes go wrong.
  • Setup and minimums decide the small lot. The same $129.58 of programming and setup is on every line, and at 10 parts the powder coater's $175.00 minimum is the whole finishing charge. The price each falls from $58.52 to $15.26 to $12.50.

Mistakes that lose money on parts you win

  • Pricing material from the part's area. The gap between parts, the sheet edge and the skeleton are steel you bought. Price from the nest count, and the whole sheet on a small order.
  • Timing the cut from the length alone. Every hole is a pierce, and pierce time grows with thickness.
  • One cut speed for every job. Speed changes with material and thickness; a speed for 14 gauge steel is wrong for quarter-inch plate or for aluminum.
  • Leaving out the brake setup. A tooling change and a first-piece check happen on every lot, however few bends the part has.
  • Forgetting hardware and the minutes to insert it. Press-in fasteners are a cost per part and a press cycle per fastener.
  • Ignoring the finisher's minimum. A powder coat or plating minimum often costs more than the part on a short run.

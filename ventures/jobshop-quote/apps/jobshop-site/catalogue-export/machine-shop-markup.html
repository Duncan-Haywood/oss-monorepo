Machine shop markup and margin

How much to mark up material, shop labor and outside processing on a machining quote, the difference between markup and margin, and what four common markup policies do to the price of one job at 1, 10 and 100 parts.

Markup and margin are not the same number

Markup is added to the cost: a part that costs $100 with a 25% markup sells for $125. Margin is the share of the price you keep: $25 of $125 is a 20% margin. So a 25% markup and a 20% margin are the same price, and a shop that says "we work on 20%" has to know which it means, because a 20% markup keeps only 16.7% of the price.

  • Margin from markup: markup ÷ (1 + markup).
  • Markup for a margin: margin ÷ (1 − margin).

Three markups, not one

A quote line has costs of different kinds, and each carries a different risk and a different amount of your money for a different time. Most quoting systems, Job Shop Quote included, keep a separate markup for each:

  • Shop labor (setup and run). If your hourly rate was worked out as cost divided by one minus a margin (how to work out a machine shop hourly rate), the profit is already in the rate and the labor markup is 0%. If your rate is the bare cost of a billable hour, the labor markup is where the profit goes. Doing both charges the margin twice; doing neither works for nothing.
  • Material. You buy it before the job, carry the cash until the customer pays, pay for the saw cut and the drop, and eat the bar that turns out bad. A markup on material pays for that. Some shops pass material through at cost on large customer-specified buys to stay competitive; that is a decision to make per customer, not by default.
  • Outside services (anodize, plating, heat treat, passivation). The vendor's lot minimum, the freight both ways, the chasing, and the parts that come back wrong are yours. The markup pays for handling them. Hardware, certificates and crating you buy for the job are purchased items like material.

Then two floors under the whole line: the vendor's lot minimum on each outside service (a $150 anodize lot costs $150 for one part or forty) and your own line minimum, the smallest price you will open a job for.

Two things to read from it. First, the markup line looks thin: 4.7% over the quote's costs at one part, 2.4% at a hundred. That is because the profit is in the mill's rate, not in the markup line, and your margin over your full cost (labor at $99.08 an hour) is 17.6% to 18.7%. Judge a quote by that, not by its markup line. Second, it never reaches the rate's 20%: material and the anodize are marked up 15%, which is a 13% margin, so the more of the price they make up, the lower the blend. At one part the anodize lot alone is 26% of the price.

  • Rate at cost with a 25% labor markup lands on the same prices as the first card, to the cent or within one: a 20% margin in the rate and a 25% markup on a cost rate are the same thing. Pick one way of writing it and use it everywhere.
  • Passing material and the anodize through at cost takes $26.25 off the one-off and $170.00 off the hundred, and the margin falls to 13.8% at one part, where the vendor's lot charge is a large share of the price.
  • One 15% markup on everything charges the margin twice on labor: $57.90 more on the one-off and $879.00 more on the hundred. That can be a fair price for a hot job; on a competitive RFQ it is the quote that loses without anyone telling you why.

Mistakes that cost margin

  • Calling a markup a margin. The table above: a 20% markup is a 16.7% margin.
  • Adding a labor markup to a rate that already has the profit in it, or neither.
  • Outside work at cost. The lot minimum, the freight and the rework when a batch comes back wrong are real costs; a markup on outside services is how they get paid.
  • Forgetting the lot minimum. A plating vendor's minimum spread over two parts is most of their price; quote it as a lot charge, not as a per-part figure from a big run.
  • No line minimum. Opening a job, programming, paperwork and shipping cost the same for a one-off as for a run. A minimum per line keeps the smallest jobs from being done at a loss; in the example it does not bind, because the bracket's setup and anodize lot already put it well above $250.00.
  • Judging by the markup line. Look at what is left after your full cost, at each quantity, because the share each cost takes changes with quantity.

Doing it in Job Shop Quote

Job Shop Quote's rate card holds a labor markup, a material markup, an outside-service markup and a line minimum, and each finishing operation is marked as in-house (marked up as labor) or outside (marked up at the outside-service rate) with its own lot charge. Hardware, certificates and other purchased extras take the material markup. Every quote line shows the markup it used next to the cost, at every quantity, and the customer's PDF never prints any of it. You can see how a quote is built.

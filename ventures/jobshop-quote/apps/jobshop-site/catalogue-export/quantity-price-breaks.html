How to set quantity price breaks on machined parts

The price each falls with quantity because some costs are paid once per lot and the rest once per part. Work out which is which and you can tell where a break earns its place, where the price has stopped falling, and what to charge for an order between two breaks. Here is the method and one part worked at 8 quantities.

Why the price each falls

Every cost on a machined part belongs to one of two kinds:

  • Once per lot: programming, setups, the first-article check, and the minimum charges (the anodizer's lot charge, the supplier's material minimum, your own minimum per line). They cost the same for one part or five hundred.
  • Once per part: cycle time, loading and deburring, the blank, the vendor's per-part charge. They grow with every part started, scrap included.

So the price each is roughly per-part cost + once-per-lot cost ÷ quantity. The first term is a floor the price never goes below; the second falls fast and then hardly at all. A quantity break is worth quoting where the second term still matters.

A worked example at 8 quantities

The same mounting bracket as in how to quote a CNC machining job: 6061-T6 aluminum plate, two milling setups and a deburr on a 3-axis mill at $123.85 an hour, clear anodize by a vendor at $3.50 a part with a $150.00 lot charge, a $25.00 material minimum, 3% expected fallout, and a $250.00 minimum per line. What the table shows:

  • Fallout rounds up to whole parts. 3% expected scrap means starting 2 brackets to ship one, so the one-off pays run time on 2 parts ($107.34, against $55.82 each at 100). On small lots the extra part is the honest price of the risk; leave it out and a one-off that scraps is made twice and paid for once.
  • Setup decides the small breaks. At one part, setup is 47.5% of the price; it falls under a tenth of the price each from 36 parts. Most of the fall from $587.26 to $71.65 each happens before then.
  • Each minimum stops applying at its own quantity. The material minimum covers orders under 3; the anodizer's lot charge covers every order under 41, so up to 40 parts the anodize costs the same $150.00 in all. The $250.00 line minimum never applies here: even one bracket costs more than that. On a cheaper part it lifts the smallest breaks to the same total, so a buyer pays the same for one part as for several; quote those breaks anyway, so the buyer sees why.
  • Past a point the breaks stop meaning much. The floor is $68.26 a part: run time, the blank and the per-part anodize, marked up, over the 97% that ship good. At 100 the price is 5.0% above it, at 500 0.9%. A lower price at volume has to come from a shorter cycle, a better blank or a cheaper vendor, not from the break.

Choosing which breaks to quote

  1. Quote what the RFQ asks for first. The buyer's quantities, and the yearly or release quantity if the RFQ gives one. A break nobody will order is noise on the quote.
  2. Add the small lot you would rather not run. One or a few parts, priced honestly, shows the buyer what a short run costs and stops a one-off being ordered at the volume price.
  3. Put breaks just past where a minimum ends. Here the lot charge stops applying at 41: a break at 40 still pays the whole lot charge, one at 50 spreads it as far as it goes.
  4. Stop where the price stops falling. Once a break is within a few percent of the floor, a larger one tells the buyer little; a separate quote for a blanket order is clearer.

Orders that fall between two breaks

The buyer saw 25 at $82.73 and 50 at $74.44, and sends a PO for 40. Three ways to price it:

  • At the lower break's price each: 40 × $82.73 = $3,309.20. Safe for the shop, and the usual written rule.
  • Priced on its own: $76.58 each, $3,063.20, by the same arithmetic as the breaks (42 parts started).
  • At the upper break's price each, which is what a buyer will ask for: $2,977.60, $85.60 under the order's own price. The setup and the lot charge are spread over fewer parts than that price assumed.

Whichever you choose, write it in the quote's terms ("quantities between breaks are priced at the next lower break") so the PO accepts it before work starts. The machine shop quote template lists the other terms worth deciding once, overruns and underruns among them; add this one beside them.

Releases are lots too

A buyer who orders 500 but takes them in 5 releases of 100 pays you for 5 setups. Priced as 5 runs of 100, the order is $35,825.00; as one run of 500, $34,445.00. The $1,380.00 difference is the setups and smaller spreads you would give away by quoting the yearly quantity's price for release-sized runs. Price the run you will actually make, or quote the yearly price on the condition that the shop runs the whole quantity at once and holds the stock.

Mistakes that lose money

  • Discounting by a percentage table. "10% off at 50, 20% off at 100" ignores where your costs actually sit; on this part the price each at 100 is 88% below the one-off, and on a part with a short setup it would be far less.
  • Pricing every break from the one-off's starts. Fallout and scrap are per part started; each break needs its own count.
  • Forgetting the vendor's minimum below its break-even. Under the lot charge's quantity, the outside service is a fixed cost, not a per-part one.
  • Letting a PO quantity pick its own price. Without a written rule the buyer reads the nearest break in their favour.

Doing it in Job Shop Quote

Each quote line in Job Shop Quote carries up to eight quantity breaks, each priced on its own parts started, with the material, setup, run time, finishing, markup and line minimum worked out per break and the rate behind every figure listed under the table; the same quantity twice is refused. The quote PDF prints the price each at every break and none of the costs. A customer accepting the quote online can pick only the breaks it carries; any other quantity needs a new quote, so a PO never picks its own price.

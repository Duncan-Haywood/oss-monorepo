How to calculate material cost for a machining quote

Material is the line on a machining quote that is easiest to get roughly right and easiest to get quietly wrong: a blank sized from the part instead of the stock, a price from last quarter, a supplier minimum left off a one-piece order. Here is the method, the four ways shops buy stock, one bracket priced all four ways at 1, 10 and 100, and what the numbers show.

The method in five steps

  1. Size the blank, not the part. Take the part's envelope (its bounding box) and add stock on every side for facing, clamping and cleanup, then round up to a size you can buy or saw. Turned parts add a cut-off and facing allowance to the length and take the next bar diameter up.
  2. Weigh it. Weight = length × width × thickness (or π × r² × length for round bar) × density in lb/in³. Take the density from the alloy's datasheet or your supplier; a figure in g/cm³ converts by × 0.0361273 (16.387064 cm³ per in³ ÷ 453.59237 g per lb).
  3. Price it the way you buy it. Plate cut to size is often priced by weight (per pound or per hundredweight, 100 lb). Bar, tube and extrusion are priced by length, and either you bill the length each part uses or, when the rest of the bar has no other job to go to, the whole bars the order needs. Precut blanks and sheets are priced per piece. Add a share for saw kerf and drop where you price by weight or by length used.
  4. Price the parts you start, not the parts you ship. If you expect some parts to scrap, the order needs more blanks: parts started = parts shipped ÷ (1 − fallout), rounded up.
  5. Keep the minimum and add your markup. Suppliers have minimum charges and cut charges; a one-off part still pays them. Then the markup on material: handling, ordering, storage and the cash tied up until the customer pays.

A worked example

A 6061 aluminum bracket whose model measures 4 × 3 × 0.5 in, quoted at 1, 10 and 100 with 3% expected fallout and a 20% material markup. Every price, allowance and scrap share here is made up to show the arithmetic; they are not market prices for any metal, supplier or region. Use your supplier's current prices.

  • Blank: 0.0625 in a side on 4 × 3 × 0.5 in, rounded up to the next 1/8 in, is 4.125 × 3.125 × 0.625 in: 8.057 in³, and at 0.0975 lb/in³, 0.786 lb.
  • Plate: 6061-T6 aluminum plate, 5/8 in, quoted by the supplier at $525.00 per hundredweight, which is $5.25 a pound, plus 25% for kerf and drop and a $40.00 minimum charge.
  • Flat bar: 5/8 × 3-1/4 in 6061-T6 aluminum flat bar at $14.80 a foot, sold in 12 ft bars ($177.60 a bar). Each part takes 4.125 in plus 0.125 in for the saw and facing, 4.25 in; after a 2 in end that cannot become a part, one bar gives 33 blanks.
  • Precut blank: 6061-T6 precut blank, 5/8 × 3-1/8 × 4-1/8 in, $13.50 a piece, one part each.
  • Starts: at 3% fallout, 1 part needs 2 starts, 10 need 11 and 100 need 104.

What the table shows:

  • At one part, the minimum is the price. Two plate blanks come to $10.31 by weight, but the $40.00 minimum is what the supplier bills, so it is what the quote must carry. Leave it off and a one-piece order loses it.
  • Whole bars cost the most on small orders. Buying a 12 ft bar for 2 blanks puts $177.60 on the quote against $11.53 for the length used. Which one is right depends on whether the rest of that bar has another job to go to; if it will sit on the rack, bill the bar.
  • At volume, the drop is in the bar count. 104 blanks at 33 a bar need 4 bars, and the last one is only partly used: it has room for 28 more blanks, about 119 in, billed with the order.
  • Precut blanks cost more per part and save saw time. At 100 they are $1,404.00 against $536.12 from plate, a gap that has to be weighed against the sawing, squaring and handling time the plate needs, which belongs on the routing, not in the material line.
  • Markup comes last. At 20%, the 100-part plate line goes from $536.12 to $643.34.

Mistakes that lose money on material

  • Pricing from the finished part. The model's volume is what is left after machining; you buy the blank. On a pocketed part the blank can weigh several times the part.
  • An old price. Metal prices move from month to month. A rate card typed in once quotes last season's metal; date every price and check the old ones before you send.
  • Pricing the parts shipped. At 3% fallout, a hundred parts shipped means a hundred and four blanks bought.
  • Dropping the minimum and cut charges. They are small on a big order and most of the cost on a small one.
  • Mixing up the two kinds of scrap. Kerf and drop are material lost from each blank; fallout is whole parts lost. Count each once.

Doing it in Job Shop Quote

Put each material on the rate card once, the way you buy it: by weight (a price per pound or per hundredweight, kept exactly as the supplier quotes it, with the density and a scrap share), by length (a price per foot or per metre, the cut allowance and, if you buy fixed bars, the bar length and remnant, so a line is charged the whole bars it needs), or by the stock piece with how many parts it yields; each with its minimum charge. When an RFQ comes with a STEP model, Job Shop Quote suggests the blank from the part's bounding box as above, and you can change it. Each line is priced on the parts started at the line's fallout, with the material markup from your rate card, and a material with no price or no blank size blocks the line instead of pricing it as free. Every price keeps the date it was set, and a draft quote warns when it prices from one older than your limit; see material price tracking. The charges in the table are the ones the app gives for the same inputs, checked by a test in its code.

For the rest of the price (setups, cycle time, outside processes and quantity breaks), see how to quote a CNC machining job and how to estimate CNC machining time.

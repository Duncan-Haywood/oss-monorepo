How to price a rush job in a machine shop

A rush costs the shop real money: overtime, the job it pushes aside, material and outside processing bought fast. A flat expedite fee guesses at that. Here is how to cost a rush the way you cost any job, a worked example at 1, 10 and 100 parts, and why one percentage cannot be right at every quantity.

What a rush actually costs

  1. Hours at a higher rate. Work done on overtime, a second shift or a weekend costs the operator's premium and the payroll costs on it. Price those hours at a second rate for the same machine, not by stretching the cycle time.
  2. The job it bumps. If another customer's job is set up on the machine you need, pulling it off means tearing it down now and setting it up again later. That setup is paid twice, and the rush is what causes it.
  3. Material bought fast. Same-day or next-day delivery, a cut-to-size charge from a distributor's counter, or a price above your usual supplier's for stock that is on a shelf today.
  4. Outside processing expedited. Platers, anodizers, heat treaters and coaters often run an expedited service at a higher price or a higher lot minimum. Ask them; their quote is the number to use.
  5. Inspection and paperwork on the same clock. A first article, certs or a customer's source inspection still have to happen, now inside the shorter window.
  6. The promises you move. Other jobs ship later. That is not a line on this quote, but it is a reason to decide whether to take the rush at all, and what lead time you can actually promise without breaking the others.

A worked example

The mounting bracket from the CNC quoting guide, quoted at its normal lead time there, and here again as a rush. Every number is made up to show the arithmetic; none is a typical overtime premium, expedite price or surcharge for any region or trade. Use your own.

  • Overtime: “Op 1: face, profile, pockets”, “Op 2: flip, face to thickness, drill and tap”, “Deburr and inspect” run on overtime, at the mill's $123.85 an hour plus a $24.00 premium = $147.85 an hour. Programming and the first-article check stay on the normal shift at $123.85.
  • The bumped job: 50 min to tear down the job that was on the mill and set it up again later, at the normal rate: $103.21, once per order.
  • Material: the same plate and price per pound, plus $85.00 for same-day delivery, once per order.
  • Clear anodize, 2-day expedite (outside vendor): $5.25 a part, minimum $300.00 a lot (the normal service in the CNC guide is cheaper).
  • Markups: as in the CNC guide; the delivery charge is a purchased item, so it takes the material markup.

What the table shows:

  • The rush adds a different share at every quantity. Costed line by line it adds 72.2% at 1 part, 45.6% at 10 parts and 21.2% at 100 parts. The bumped setup, the delivery and the anodizer's higher minimum are fixed per order, so they weigh most on a small lot; the overtime premium grows with the hours, so it is most of the rush on a large one.
  • A flat 25% is wrong both ways. It comes up $277.44 short at 1 part and $233.97 short at 10 parts, and charges $269.25 more than the rush costs at 100 parts. Short means the shop pays for the customer's deadline; over means the shop is easier to beat on the next rush.
  • If you want a margin for the disruption, add it on purpose. Cost the rush, then add a premium you choose for the promises it moves. Then you know what the premium is and what is just cost.

Before you say yes

  • Check the machines, not your memory. Which jobs are on the machines this operation needs, and when do they finish? A rush that only fits by making two other jobs late is a decision, not a quote.
  • Call the outside vendors first. An anodizer or heat treater who cannot do it in time sets the lead time, whatever your own shop can do.
  • Quote the rush and the normal lead time side by side. Two lines, two dates, two prices. The customer picks, and the rush price reads as a choice, not a penalty.
  • Write the date as a condition. "Ships N working days after the order and the approved drawing" protects the date from a purchase order that arrives two days late.
  • Keep the rush in its own lines. Priced as separate lines, or as its own revision of the quote, the rush can be taken off again: when the same part comes back on a normal lead time, it is the normal price that gets reused, not last time's expedite.

Doing it in Job Shop Quote

Job Shop Quote has no rush switch: a rush is priced like any other line, with the rush costs typed in. Add the overtime rate as a second machine on your rate card ("3-axis mill (overtime)"), put the operations that run on overtime on it, add the bumped job as an operation with setup only, the expedited service as its own finishing operation with its lot minimum, and the delivery as an extra per order. Every quantity break then follows, each figure showing the rate it came from. The example above is priced to the cent the app gives for the same inputs, checked by a test in its code. To see whether a date needs a rush at all, the app's shop load and lead time page places your open jobs on the machines and gives each quantity break a lead time behind them.

How to quote welding and fabrication jobs

A weldment quote is mostly time: cutting and fitting the pieces, building the fixture, and the welder's hours, of which the arc is lit for only part. Here is the method, a worked example at 1, 10 and 50 frames, and the mistakes that lose money on jobs you win.

The method in six steps

  1. Read the drawing for what changes the price. Material and section sizes, every weld symbol (type, size, length, all-around), the process and any welding code or procedure it calls for, welder qualifications, weld inspection or testing, flatness and finish. A missing weld size or code is a question to the customer, not a guess.
  2. Make the cut list. Each piece, its length, end cuts (square, miter, cope) and section. Add the lengths per section and allow for kerf, squaring cuts and the drop at the end of each stick.
  3. Add up the weld. Total inches of each weld size from the symbols. Arc minutes = weld length ÷ travel speed for that size, position and process.
  4. Turn arc time into welder time. Welder minutes = arc minutes ÷ operator factor, the share of the welder's time with the arc lit. The rest is positioning, changing angles, cleaning between passes and moving the part; use the figure you measure in your own shop.
  5. Price fit-up, the fixture and the rest. Fitting and tacking per part, building the fixture once per job, saw cuts per piece, grinding, cleaning and inspection per part, each at that station's own rate. Add the filler wire (from the weld metal, at your deposition efficiency) and shielding gas.
  6. Add outside processes, markup and quote every quantity. Galvanizing, powder coat and paint carry lot minimums; repeat the sum at each break, since the fixture, the first article and the coater's minimum spread differently at each.

A worked example

A steel cart frame: 8 pieces of 2 × 2 × 1/8 in square tube, sawn, fitted and tacked in a fixture, MIG welded, ground and powder coated by a vendor. Every number here is made up to show the arithmetic; they are not typical speeds, prices, rates or operator factors for any process, region or trade. Use your own.

  • Material: 8 pieces, 216 in of tube (18 ft) a frame, at $4.50 a foot plus 10% for kerf, squaring and drop: $89.10 a frame.
  • Band saw at $75.00 an hour: 8 cuts × 1.5 min = 12 min a frame (load, measure, cut, deburr the end), 15 min to set the stop and the miter.
  • Fixture, fit and tack in the MIG welding cell at $95.00 an hour: 90 min to build the fixture once for the job, 12 min a frame to load, square and tack.
  • Weld out: 64 in of 3/16 in fillet at 10 in/min is 6.40 arc minutes. At a 35% operator factor the welder spends 6.40 ÷ 0.35 = 18.286 min, rounded up to 18.29 min a frame.
  • Bench at $65.00 an hour: grind, clean and inspect, 10 min a frame; a 30 min first-article check once per job.
  • Wire and gas: a 3/16 in fillet is a triangle of 0.0176 sq in, so 64 in of it is 0.319 lb of steel at 0.284 lb/cu in. At 90% deposition efficiency that is 0.355 lb of wire at $3.50 a lb ($1.24), plus 35 cu ft/h of gas for 6.40 arc minutes, 3.73 cu ft at $0.12 ($0.45): $1.69 a frame.
  • Black powder coat (outside vendor): $35.00 a frame, minimum $225.00 a lot.
  • Markup: 20% on material and consumables, 15% on the outside service, none on labor (the shop rates are taken to hold the margin already); a $250.00 minimum per line.

What the table shows:

  • The operator factor is the biggest single assumption. Priced on arc time alone, the weld would be $10.13 a frame; priced on the welder's time it is $28.96. Guess the factor high and every frame you win loses the difference.
  • Consumables are small next to labor. Wire and gas are $1.69 a frame against $28.96 of welding. A price built up from pounds of wire misses most of the cost.
  • The fixture and the coater's minimum decide the one-off. At 1 frame the $193.75 of fixture, saw setup and first article and the $225.00 coating minimum are 72% of the cost. At 50 the same setups are $3.88 a frame. That is why the price each falls from $635.24 to $242.37 to $226.87.
  • At volume it is run time and tube. At 50 frames, run time ($3,689.63) and tube ($4,455.00) are 80% of the cost; a faster fixture or a better cut list is where a lower price would come from.

Mistakes that lose money on jobs you win

  • Pricing weld by arc time. The welder is paid for every minute, not just the ones with the arc lit. Measure your operator factor on real jobs and use it.
  • One travel speed for every weld. Weld size, position, process and the number of passes change it several-fold; a vertical or overhead weld or a multi-pass fillet is far slower than a flat single pass.
  • Leaving out fit-up and tacking. On small weldments it can take as long as the welding.
  • Forgetting the fixture. It is real time once per job, and it is the reason a one-off costs so much more than the tenth piece.
  • Leaving out grinding, cleanup and distortion. Weld spatter, blending and straightening a frame that pulled out of square all take time per part.
  • Missing a code or inspection callout. A welding procedure, qualified welders, or visual, dye-penetrant or other testing called out on the drawing add time and paperwork that the weld length alone does not show.

Doing it in Job Shop Quote

Job Shop Quote does not read weld symbols or measure weld length: you work out the times above and type them in. Each quote line then carries the tube from your rate card priced by the foot (the frame's total length as the blank length, kerf and drop as the row's scrap allowance), one operation per station at that machine's rate (saw, fit and tack, weld, grind), the fixture as a setup that is charged once per quantity break, wire and gas as an extra per part, and the powder coat as an outside service with its lot minimum. Every quantity break then follows, every figure showing the rate it came from, and a line missing a time or a material is blocked rather than priced as if it were free. The example above is priced to the cent the app gives for the same inputs, checked by a test in its code. You can see how a quote is built.

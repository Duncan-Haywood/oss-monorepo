RFQ review checklist for machine shops

21 things to check on a request for quote before you price it: the documents, the part, the quantities and dates, the paperwork and the terms. Most costly quoting mistakes are not arithmetic; they are a revision, a callout or a clause nobody read. A free checklist to download and fill in per RFQ is at the end.

Download the checklist (CSV)

Why review an RFQ before quoting it

A quote is an offer: once the buyer sends a purchase order against it, you have agreed to make that revision, in that material, to those callouts, by that date, at that price. Reviewing the request first is where you find what the price must cover and whether you can deliver it at all. ISO 9001:2015 and AS9100D both ask for this step under clause 8.2.3, “review of the requirements for products and services”, before a shop commits to supply. This list is a practical start for that review; it does not by itself meet either standard, and your own quality manual decides what your review records.

In short: documents, the part, quantity and timing, quality and paperwork, commercial terms and restrictions.

The documents

CheckAskWhy it matters
Every part has a drawing or modelDoes each line on the RFQ have a drawing, a model or both, and do the part numbers on them match the RFQ?A line quoted from a description alone is a guess; a drawing that carries another part number means one part was never sent.
Drawing revisionWhich revision is each drawing, does it match the revision the RFQ asks for, and is the model the same revision as the drawing?The price is for the revision quoted. A model at Rev C with a drawing at Rev B is two different parts until the buyer says which one governs.
Drawing or model governsIf the drawing and the model disagree, which one does the customer say governs, and are there dimensions only on the drawing?Many shops program from the model and inspect to the drawing; a note saying which governs settles the argument before the first article, not after.
Referenced specs on handDo you have every specification the drawing calls out (material, finish, heat treat, welding, inspection), at the revision it names?A callout you have not read can add a process, a test or a certification you did not price.

The part

CheckAskWhy it matters
MaterialIs the material fully specified (alloy, temper or condition, form), can you buy it at the size you need, and is a substitute allowed?“Aluminum” is not a material; 6061-T6 plate and 7075-T651 bar price and machine differently, and an unavailable size changes the blank.
Tightest tolerancesWhich features carry the tightest tolerances or GD&T callouts, and can your machines hold and your gauges measure them?One tight bore can decide the machine, the setup count and the inspection time for the whole part.
Surface finish and edgesWhat surface roughness is called out, where, and what edge break or deburr note applies?A fine finish on a sealing face may need a finishing pass or a grind; “break all edges” on a part with many edges is real minutes.
Threads and holesWhich threads, thread classes and hole depths are called out, and do any need special tools or gauges?Deep holes, small taps and thread gauges you do not own add time and tooling cost.
Finish, plating and heat treatWhich outside processes are called out (anodize, plating, passivation, paint, heat treat), to which specification, and who supplies them?Outside processes have lot minimums and lead times of their own, and a masking note can cost more than the coating.
Marking and packagingDoes the part need part marking, serial numbers, bagging, special packaging or labels?Small per-part steps add up over a lot and are easy to forget at the quoting stage.

Quantity and timing

CheckAskWhy it matters
Quantities asked forWhich quantities does the buyer want prices for, is it a one-off, a blanket order or scheduled releases, and is there a prototype lot first?Setup and lot minimums spread differently at each quantity; a blanket order with releases is priced and stocked differently from one lot.
Due date against your loadWhen does the buyer need the parts, when must the quote be in, and can you deliver with what is already on the floor?A price you cannot deliver on loses the customer more surely than a high price.
Repeat partHave you quoted or made this part number before, at which revision and price, and did that quote win?Last time's price, time and problems are the best estimate you have, and a buyer notices when the same part is priced differently.

Quality and paperwork

CheckAskWhy it matters
CertificationsDoes the buyer want material certifications, a certificate of conformance or process certs from your finishers?Certs have to be ordered with the material and kept; a mill cert requested after the material arrives may not be available.
First-article and inspection reportsIs a first-article inspection required (for aerospace work, often to AS9102), a CMM report, a sampling plan or 100% inspection?A full first-article report on a complex part can take longer than machining the first part, and it is often unpriced.
Quality system requirementsDoes the purchase order or the buyer's supplier manual require a quality system registered to ISO 9001 or AS9100, or other quality clauses?Quality clauses on the PO bind you once you accept it; read them at the quote, not when the order arrives.

Commercial terms and restrictions

CheckAskWhy it matters
Export-controlled technical dataIs the drawing marked ITAR or EAR, or as Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI), and can your shop lawfully receive and store it?Export-controlled and CUI drawings have handling rules of their own; ask someone qualified before you open them, not after.
Defense and government flowdownsIs it a defense or government job, and which clauses flow down to you (for example DFARS 252.204-7012 on safeguarding covered defense information, or 252.225-7009 on specialty metals)?Flowdowns can restrict where material comes from and how you store the drawings; a clause you cannot meet means no-quote.
Customer-supplied materialDoes the customer supply the material or parts, and who pays when one scraps?Free-issue material changes the price and the risk; settle the scrap terms on the quote.
Payment and shippingAre the buyer's payment terms and shipping terms (FOB point, who pays freight) ones you accept?Net 90 on a large material buy is a loan to the customer; know it before you price.
Quote or no-quoteAfter all of the above: do you want this job at a price the buyer is likely to pay, and if not, have you told them so?A prompt no-quote keeps the buyer's goodwill and your estimator's time for the work you want.

When the answer is “I don't know”

Ask the buyer before you quote, in one email listing every open question, rather than one question at a time. Where the buyer will not answer in time, quote with the assumption written in the quote's notes (“quoted to Rev B; drawing governs; material certs included, first-article report not included”), so the PO accepts your reading or the buyer corrects it before work starts. The machine shop quote template has a notes field for exactly this, and the terms worth deciding once.

The checklist

rfq-review-checklist.csv opens in Excel, Google Sheets or LibreOffice: a line at the top for the RFQ, who reviewed it and when, then one row per check with the columns Section, Check, Question, Why it matters, Answer (yes / no / n/a), Notes. Save one copy per RFQ with the quote. It holds plain values, no formulas. Drop the rows that never apply to your work and add your own.

Doing it in Job Shop Quote

Job Shop Quote does not replace the review; it puts the facts in front of the estimator and keeps the record. For 11 of the checks above, the hosted app does this today:

  • Every part has a drawing or model. The rule reader matches a drawing to the part whose number it carries and fills that part's blanks from it.
  • Drawing revision. Revision is a field on every part and prints on the quote line, so the quote says which revision was priced.
  • Material. The reader takes the material line and suggests the matching material from your rate card; a material stated above a parts list applies to every part.
  • Tightest tolerances. The reader picks up labelled tolerance lines so the estimator sees them on the review.
  • Threads and holes. From a STEP model the app finds the holes and their depths and pre-fills drilling; the estimator ticks which sizes are tapped.
  • Finish, plating and heat treat. Finishing and outside services are on the rate card with their per-part price and lot minimum.
  • Quantities asked for. The reader takes the quantity lines; every quantity break is priced from the same rate card, with setup and minimums spread at each.
  • Due date against your load. The reader takes due dates that carry a year; the Shop load page puts your open jobs on your machines and plans a draft quote's lead time behind them.
  • Repeat part. The RFQ review shows “Quoted before: N, M won” for each part before a quote exists, from your own quote history.
  • Export-controlled technical data. Job Shop Quote's early access is not set up for CUI or export-controlled drawings; use it only for commercial RFQs.
  • Quote or no-quote. Quotes you send are logged as won, lost or expired with the reason, so win rate by customer shows where quoting pays.

Reading is by rules today (AI reading is not switched on yet), and shows each value it took next to the RFQ text it came from, unconfirmed until the estimator confirms the part. When something a price needs is missing, the quote shows a blocking issue instead of a guess. The pricing then follows your rate card: how a machining quote is built.

This page describes common practice for reviewing job-shop RFQs. It is not legal, export-control, tax or quality-certification advice; the standards and regulations named are for you to read in their current text. Check every quote before you send it.

How to quote outside processing: heat treat, anodize and plating

Work you send out is the one part of a quote whose price someone else sets, and the vendor's minimum charge is easy to miss. On a short run it can be most of the price. Here is the method, a worked example at 5, 50 and 250 parts, and what the quote looks like if you just multiply the vendor's per-part price by the quantity.

The method in six steps

  1. Read the drawing for every process after machining. Heat treat and the hardness, anodize type, class and colour, plating and thickness, black oxide, passivation, paint or powder coat, and the spec each one calls out (for example MIL-A-8625 for anodize or AMS 2759 for steel heat treat). Note masked surfaces, threads to protect and any certificate the customer wants back. A finish with no spec is a question to the customer, not a guess.
  2. Get each vendor's price in the unit they bill. Heat treaters often price by the pound of parts, platers and anodizers by the part, the rack or the area, and nearly all of them have a minimum charge per lot. Ask for masking, racking, stripping and certificate charges and the turnaround as well.
  3. Turn it into a price per part and a lot minimum. A per-pound price times the finished part's weight (what goes in the furnace, not the blank) is the per-part charge. The vendor bills the larger of per-part × parts sent and the lot minimum.
  4. Count the parts you send, not the parts you ship. With fallout, you machine and send more parts than the order, and the vendor charges for each one.
  5. Add the cost of getting it there and back. Freight or the driver's time to each vendor and back, packaging, and the certificates. These are per lot, so they hit a small order hardest.
  6. Mark it up and quote every quantity. A markup on outside work pays for chasing the vendor, the parts' time off your floor and the risk of a rejected lot. Repeat the sum at each break: the minimums spread differently at each.

A worked example

A steel clamp block: 4140 steel plate, milled from a 4 × 2 × 1.25 in blank in two setups, deburred and inspected, then hardened and tempered by a heat treater who charges by the pound and black oxided by a second vendor. Every number here is made up to show the arithmetic; they are not prices, rates or minimums for any vendor, process or region. Use your own vendors' quotes.

The outside services

  • Harden and temper (outside heat treater): $0.85 a pound. The machined block weighs 2.1 lb (from the model; the blank was 2.84 lb), so 2.1 × $0.85 = $1.79 a part, with a $175.00 minimum a lot. The minimum stops binding at $175.00 ÷ $1.79 = 98 parts sent.
  • Black oxide (outside vendor): $1.20 a part, minimum $85.00 a lot, which stops binding at 71 parts.
  • Freight to and from both vendors: $90.00 a lot.
  • Heat-treat certificate: $25.00 a lot.

The rest

  • Material: 2.84 lb a blank at $2.40 a pound plus 10% for kerf and facing, at least $25.00 a line.
  • Mill, two setups on the vertical machining center at $95.00 an hour: 90 min setup, 14 min a part.
  • Deburr and inspect on the bench at $55.00 an hour: 3 min a part.
  • First-article check on the bench at $55.00 an hour: 30 min setup.
  • Fallout: 3%, so 5 parts start 6, 50 start 52 and 250 start 258, and both vendors are paid on the parts started.
  • Markup: 15% on the outside services, 20% on material, freight and the certificate (bought-in costs), none on labor (the shop rates are taken to hold the margin already); a $300.00 minimum per line.

The quote you get by multiplying

The common shortcut is to take each vendor's per-part price, multiply it by the quantity and add it at cost: no minimums, no freight, no certificate, no markup. Priced that way, with everything else the same:

What the two tables show:

  • On a short run the minimums are the outside price. At 5 parts the vendors would bill $10.74 and $7.20 by the part; they bill $175.00 and $85.00. With freight and the certificate, outside costs are $375.00, 51% of the cost. The multiplied quote comes to $391.45 against $810.50: 48% of what the job should be priced at.
  • The minimums still bind at 50. 52 parts sent is below both break-even counts (71 and 98), so the outside charge is the same $260.00 as at 5. The shortfall is $281.50, about $5.63 a part, which is easy to miss in a $2,371.00 quote.
  • At volume the gap is the freight and the markup. At 250 parts both vendors bill by the part, so the multiplied outside cost is right; what is still missing is $115.00 of freight and certificate and the markup on outside work, a shortfall of $252.50.
  • The price each falls from $162.10 to $47.42 to $39.78, and the lot minimums and freight are a large part of why. When a customer orders small lots of a repeat part, it can pay to offer a larger run held in stock, or to send it out with other jobs going to the same vendor.

Mistakes that lose money on jobs you win

  • Pricing the vendor's per-part rate without the lot minimum. Above: the smaller the order, the bigger the miss.
  • Weighing the blank, or nothing. A per-pound price applies to what goes in the furnace. Use the finished weight, and include any fixture or basket weight the vendor charges for.
  • Sending the order quantity. Spares for fallout and first articles go through the vendor too, and are billed.
  • Forgetting the trip. Freight or a driver's half day each way, packaging, and the certificate are real costs on every lot.
  • Quoting a stale vendor price. Platers and heat treaters change prices and minimums; keep their current quotes on file and date them.
  • Leaving vendor days out of the lead time. The parts are off your floor for the vendor's turnaround plus the trips. Add it to the date you promise; see quoting lead times from shop load.

Doing it in Job Shop Quote

In Job Shop Quote each finishing step is a rate-card entry with a per-part charge and a lot minimum, marked as an outside service or done in house. Outside services take their own markup, separate from labor and material, and freight, certificates and other bought-in items go on a line as extras, per part or per lot. A quote line prices each break the way the tables above do: the vendor's charge is the larger of per-part × parts started and the lot minimum, and the quote's basis prints each finish's per-part charge and minimum. Job Shop Quote does not price a finish by the pound or by area: type the per-part charge you work out from the vendor's quote. The example above is priced to the cent the app gives for the same inputs, both ways, checked by a test in its code. Finishing entries can be imported from a CSV with the rest of the rate card, and a won quote's job traveler lists the finishing and outside services after the operations.

How tolerances change a machining quote

Two drawings of the same part can cost very different amounts to make, and the difference is often one callout: a bore at ±.005 is a drill and a pass, the same bore at ±.0005 can be a reamer, a slower finish, a gauge and a check on every part. Here is how to read a tolerance as a zone, the four bands estimators sort them into, what ISO 2768 and fits like H7 mean in inches, and where the cost goes on the quote.

Read a tolerance as a zone

A tolerance is the room a dimension has to vary. What decides how hard it is to hold is the full width of that room, the tolerance zone, not how it is written. ±.001 is a zone of .002 in; +.002/-.000 is a zone of .002 in too, with all of it on one side. A tight one-sided tolerance can be as hard as a looser-looking bilateral one, so convert every callout to its zone before you compare them. On a drawing, the tightest zone usually sets the process, the machine and the inspection for the whole part.

Four bands, and what each asks of the shop

Estimators sort tolerances into rough bands. The limits below are shop-floor rules of thumb, not a standard: what counts as tight depends on the material, the feature, its size and your machines. They are the defaults Job Shop Quote's tolerance check uses.

Tolerance bands by zone, in inches. Rules of thumb, not a standard.
BandToleranceWhat it usually asks
Standard±.005 in and looser (a zone of .010 in or more)Ordinary roughing and finishing on a CNC mill or lathe, checked with calipers.
Close±.001 up to ±.005 in (a zone of .002 to under .010 in)A finish pass and a micrometer or bore gauge on the feature; most CNC machines hold it.
Tight±.0005 up to ±.001 in (a zone of .001 to under .002 in)Finish passes at a lighter cut, tool wear watched, in-process gauging, and more parts checked per lot.
Precisiontighter than ±.0005 in (a zone under .001 in)Often grinding, honing or reaming to size, a temperature-stable check and a CMM or air gauge; some shops send it out.

ISO 2768: the general tolerance in the title block

A drawing that says "ISO 2768-m" (often with a second letter, K, H or L, for the geometric tolerances) sets the tolerance of every dimension that has none of its own. The value grows with the size of the dimension. The linear values from ISO 2768-1, in millimetres either way:

Classover 0.5–3over 3–6over 6–30over 30–120over 120–400

The first column starts at 0.5 mm and the others start just above the size named, so "over 6–30" means over 6 up to 30 mm. A general class rarely sets the price on its own: "fine" on a 20 mm dimension is ±0.1 mm, about ±.004 in, a close tolerance most CNC work holds. The dimensions toleranced on their own are the ones to look for.

Fits: H7, g6 and the rest

A letter and number on a bore or a pin (H7, h6, H7/g6) is an ISO 286 fit. The letter places the zone relative to the nominal size; the number is the IT grade, which sets its width, and that width grows with the size. On an 18 to 30 mm feature, IT6 is 13 µm (about .0005 in) and IT7 is 21 µm (about .0008 in): an H7 bore of that size is a precision feature by the bands above, which is why it usually means a reamer or a boring bar and a bore gauge, not a drill. Look up the size you have in the standard's tables before you quote it.

Where the cost goes on the quote

Tolerance has no price of its own. It costs money through the work it adds, and that work belongs on the quote line where you can see it:

  • Run time. Finish passes at a lighter cut, a slower feed, a spring pass, tool changes to keep wear in check. Add the minutes to the operation's cycle time; the machining time guide shows how cycle time builds up.
  • An extra operation. Reaming, honing or grinding to size is an operation of its own, in house or as an outside process.
  • Inspection. A gauge you need to buy or calibrate, a CMM program, checking every part instead of a sample. Inspection time is setup minutes on an inspection machine, charged once per line, the same way the first article guide prices it.
  • Fallout. The tighter the zone, the more parts land outside it. Start more parts than the order: a fallout percentage on the line does this.
  • Or a question. A tolerance tighter than the part's function needs is common. Asking the customer whether ±.0005 on a clearance hole could be ±.002 can be worth more than pricing it.

Doing it in Job Shop Quote

The RFQ review reads the tolerance text from the customer's email or drawing (the AI reader keeps it exactly as written) and, when the tightest tolerance is tighter than ±.005 in, names it with its zone and band and says what to check: "Precision: +.0005/-.0000, a 0.00050 in (0.013 mm) zone." It reads ±, +/-, one-sided limits, inches and millimetres (comma decimals too), ISO 2768 classes and ISO 286 fits, and skips angles. It never changes a price: the minutes, operations and fallout above are yours to add, and the quote is priced from your rate card. Fits and ISO 2768 classes are read at a typical size (the note says which), because the RFQ does not say how big the feature is. The RFQ review checklist has the tolerance questions to ask before you price.

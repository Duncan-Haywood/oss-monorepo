How to work out a machine shop hourly rate

A machine's hourly rate is what the machine, its operator and its share of the shop cost in a year, divided by the hours you can actually bill on it. Here is the method, line by line, with a worked example you can redo with your own numbers.

The formula

Hourly rate = (machine cost + upkeep + power + floor space + operator + share of overhead) per year ÷ billable hours per year.

That is the cost of an hour. Profit goes on top as a markup, kept separate so you can see it and change it by job.

Each line, and where the number comes from

  1. Machine. Price paid, installed, less what you expect to sell it for, spread over the years you expect to run it. If you financed it, add the year's interest.
  2. Tooling and maintenance. Inserts, holders, coolant, way oil, service visits and repairs for a year. Last year's invoices for that machine are the best guide.
  3. Power. Hours run × average draw in kW × your price per kWh. Average draw is well under the nameplate: a spindle is rarely at full load. Your power bill gives the price; the US average commercial price was 14.27¢ per kWh in September 2026 (EIA Electric Power Monthly, Table 5.6.A (September 2026, preliminary), read 2026-11-27), and it varies a lot by state.
  4. Floor space. The square feet the machine and its work area take × what a square foot of your building costs a year (rent or mortgage, property tax, heat and light).
  5. Operator. Wage × paid hours, plus the burden: the employer's Social Security and Medicare, 7.65% (IRS Topic no. 751, Social Security and Medicare withholding rates, read 2026-11-27), plus workers' comp, unemployment insurance, benefits and paid time off, which your payroll reports give as a percentage. If one person runs two machines, split the cost between them.
  6. Share of overhead. Everything that is not one machine: the office, estimating and programming time, software, insurance, supervision, inspection. Divide it across machines by the hours each runs, or by what each earns.
  7. Billable hours. Paid hours × utilization: the share of the hours you can charge a customer for. Setup you bill counts; waiting for material, warm-up, maintenance, rework and idle time do not.

A worked example

A made-up shop with a used 3-axis vertical mill on one shift. Every input below is invented for the example except the two public figures above; use your own.

  • Bought for $120,000 installed, expected resale $30,000 after 7 years; tooling and maintenance $6,000 a year.
  • Average draw 8 kW at 14.27¢ per kWh; 300 sq ft at $12 per sq ft a year.
  • One operator at $28 an hour for 2,000 paid hours, payroll taxes 7.65% and other burden 20%.
  • Its share of shop overhead $30,000 a year; 1,500 billable hours (75% of paid hours).
Made-up machine and shop. Rounded to the cent; per-hour figures divide each year's cost by 1,500 billable hours.

Most of the cost is fixed, so an hour you cannot bill is spread over the hours you can. A shop that sets its rate assuming 90% and bills 60% loses money on every job without seeing why. Use the utilization you actually had last year, not the one you hope for.

Mistakes that make a rate too low

  • Counting paid hours as billable hours.
  • Leaving out the burden: the wage alone is not what an operator costs.
  • Pricing a machine you own outright as free. It wears out and will need replacing.
  • One blended shop rate for a 5-axis mill and a manual saw. A rate per machine or work center quotes the cheap work and the expensive work each at its real cost.
  • Never checking. Compare the hours you quoted with the hours jobs took, and the utilization you assumed with what you billed, at least once a year.

Putting the rate to work in Job Shop Quote

Job Shop Quote's rate card takes the result as it is: an hourly rate per machine, an optional separate setup rate (for when setup is done by someone paid differently), and markups kept apart from the rates, one for labor, one for material and one for outside services, plus a line minimum. Each quote line is priced from those by plain arithmetic you can check, and the rates in force are saved with each quote, so a later rate change does not rewrite what you sent. To check your times against what jobs really took, the free quoted vs actual hours page reads a job history export, per machine, in your browser.

This guide is general information about cost arithmetic, not accounting or tax advice. Depreciation for your books and taxes follows different rules (ask your accountant); the straight line here is only for pricing.

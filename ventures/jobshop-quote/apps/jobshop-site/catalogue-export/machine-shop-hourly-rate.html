How to work out a machine shop hourly rate

The hourly rate on a quote has to pay for the machine, the operator, the building and a profit, out of the hours a customer actually pays for. Here is a method that starts from your own books, a worked example, and the mistakes that make a rate too low.

The method in five steps

  1. What the machine costs you each year. Take the installed price, subtract what you expect to sell it for, and divide by the years you will run it. Add a year of maintenance, tooling, coolant and power for that machine. If you financed it, the interest belongs here too. Power is hours run × average draw in kW (well under the nameplate) × your price per kWh; the US average commercial price was 14.27¢ in September 2026 (EIA Electric Power Monthly, Table 5.6.A, read 2026-11-27), and states differ a lot, so use your own bill.
  2. Its share of overhead. Rent, insurance, utilities, the office, the estimator, software and everything else that does not belong to one machine, split across your machines. Splitting by floor space or by the hours each runs is fairer than an even split when machines differ a lot.
  3. Billable hours, honestly counted. Not the hours the doors are open: the hours the machine spends on work a customer pays for. Setups you don't charge, maintenance, prove-outs, scrap remakes and idle time all come out. Your job records are the best source; a guess here moves the rate more than any other number.
  4. The operator. Wage plus payroll taxes, workers' comp, insurance and benefits gives the cost of a paid hour. The employer's Social Security and Medicare alone are 7.65% of wages (IRS Topic no. 751, read 2026-11-27); your payroll reports give the rest as a percentage. The operator is paid for more hours than the machine bills, so spread the whole paid year over the billable hours. One operator tending two machines splits that cost between them.
  5. Add the profit. Divide the cost of a billable hour by one minus your margin. A 20% margin means dividing by 0.8, which is not the same as adding 20%.

Two variations on the same shop show which inputs matter most:

  • If the operator tends two such mills, each mill carries half the operator and its cost falls from $99.08 to $71.08 an hour.
  • Divide by every paid hour instead of billable hours (the common shortcut of about 2,000 hours a year) and the cost comes out at $64.40 an hour: $34.68 an hour short, on every hour you bill.

Almost every cost is fixed for the year, so each hour you cannot bill is paid for by the hours you can. A rate set for a busy year loses money in a slow one; set it on the utilization your job records show, and rework it when the backlog changes.

Mistakes that make a rate too low

  • Counting open hours as billable. The step above: the rate is only as good as the billable-hours figure, so check it against last year's jobs.
  • One rate for every machine. A 5-axis machine and a manual lathe cost very different amounts to own. A blended rate overprices the simple work you lose and underprices the complex work you win.
  • Mixing up margin and markup. In the example a 20% markup gives $118.89 an hour, a 20% margin $123.85. Decide which one your targets mean.
  • Leaving setup in the hourly rate. Setup time is real machine time. Charging it as its own line, or at its own rate, keeps small runs from being priced like large ones.
  • Never revisiting it. Wages, rent and power change. Rework the rate when they do, and at least once a year.

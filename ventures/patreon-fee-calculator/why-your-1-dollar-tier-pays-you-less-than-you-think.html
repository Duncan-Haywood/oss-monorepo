Why your $1 tier pays you less than you think

It's tempting to assume a $1 pledge is just a smaller version of a $10 one — same percentage, smaller number. It isn't. Patreon charges a genuinely worse payment-processing rate on any single charge of $3 or less, on top of whichever platform fee applies to your page. Here's the exact math, including a one-cent cliff most creators never notice.

Two processing rates, not one

Patreon passes through payment processing separately from its own platform fee. For a charge above $3, that's the standard rate: 2.9% + $0.30. For a charge of $3 or less, it switches to a worse "micropayment" rate: 5% + $0.10. The percentage goes up and the fixed fee only goes down by twenty cents — nowhere near enough to offset the higher percentage on anything but the very smallest charges.

Add the platform fee on top of processing and a $1 Standard-plan (10%) tier nets a creator about 75% of the pledge — $0.75 out of $1.00 — while a $10 tier on the same plan nets about 84% — $8.41 out of $10.00. Same plan, same creator, a nine-point gap in what actually reaches them, purely from where the pledge amount falls relative to $3.

The one-cent cliff at exactly $3

The table above has a row most creators never think to check: $3.00 versus $3.01. At $3.00, the pledge still qualifies for the micropayment rate and pays $0.25 in processing. At $3.01 — one cent more — it crosses into the standard rate and pays $0.38729, a jump of nearly 14 cents in fees for one extra cent of pledge. Before the platform fee is even applied, the $3.00 pledge nets the creator $2.75 and the $3.01 pledge nets $2.62271. The higher pledge amount actually pays the creator less. It's a narrow, almost accidental effect — nobody prices a tier at $3.01 on purpose — but it's the cleanest illustration that this is a real rate boundary, not a smooth curve.

What this means for how you price tiers

Nothing here is a bug to report or a reason to feel misled — Patreon publishes both rates, and a fixed $0.10-$0.30 fee has to be a bigger share of a small charge than a large one under any processing model. What it does mean is that a mental model of "I keep roughly 85-90% after fees" is only true above the $3 line. If your tier mix leans on $1-3 pledges, your real blended take-home is lower than that mental model predicts — run your own tier counts through the calculator rather than estimating.

Common questions

Why does a $1 pledge get taxed at a worse rate than a $10 pledge?

Because Patreon uses two different payment-processing rates depending on the charge amount. A charge above $3 is billed at the standard rate: 2.9% + $0.30. A charge at or below $3 is billed at a worse micropayment rate instead: 5% + $0.10. The percentage is higher and the only thing that shrinks is the fixed 30 cents, which is a much smaller saving than the extra 2.1 percentage points cost on anything but the smallest pledges.

Is the platform fee (Standard, Lite, Pro, Premium) part of this too?

Yes, and it applies on top, at the same percentage regardless of pledge size — the micropayment effect described here is entirely about the processing fee, not the platform fee. A creator on any plan sees the same relative hit on a low-dollar tier; only the total percentage taken shifts with the plan.

Can a pledge one cent higher actually leave me with less money?

Yes, in the narrow band right at the $3 boundary. A $3.00 charge is billed at the micropayment rate (5% + $0.10 = $0.25 in fees). A $3.01 charge — one cent more — crosses into the standard rate (2.9% + $0.30 = $0.38729 in fees). The fee jumps by about 13.7 cents for one extra cent of pledge, so the $3.01 tier nets the creator less than the $3.00 tier does, before the platform fee is even applied.

What should I actually do about this?

Nothing is broken or worth fighting — Patreon publishes both rates and a $0.10-$0.30 fixed fee has to be a larger share of a $1 charge than a $10 one no matter how it's split. The useful move is knowing the real number: use the calculator to see your own take-home from your actual tier mix rather than assuming every tier is taxed the same, and if you're pricing a new low tier, price it meaningfully above $3 rather than at $3.01 if you want the standard rate's math to actually help you.

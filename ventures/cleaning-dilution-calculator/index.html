Common questions

Why does mixing labor cost more than the chemical itself?

On the default example — a 1:32 dilution from a $32, 128 fl oz container — the concentrate works out to 25¢ an ounce, so 4 fl oz of concentrate costs $1.00. Three minutes of mixing at a $22/hour loaded labor rate costs $1.10. A cheap concentrate does not make a batch cheap if someone spends real minutes mixing it: at a decent wage, labor can be the larger half of what a batch actually costs, and a price built on chemical cost alone undercharges for it.

Why does the waste allowance divide the cost instead of adding a percentage on top?

Because waste describes volume lost, not an extra fee. Dividing by (1 − waste%) grosses the batch cost up so the ready-to-use gallons you actually deliver still cost what they should, even though some of what you mixed never made it out of the bucket, sprayer, or mop. On the default 5% allowance, the $2.10 combined chemical-and-labor cost becomes a $2.21 batch cost — the extra 11¢ is what one batch's worth of spillage and dead space adds back onto the gallons that do get used.

What is the difference between cost per gallon and cost per bucket?

“Ready-to-use water” is how many gallons a single batch of concentrate makes, which is what cost per gallon is measured against. “Bucket or job size” is a separate figure — how many gallons your actual bucket or job uses — and cost per bucket simply scales the per-gallon figure up to that size. Getting the two mixed up under- or over-states a bid by whatever the ratio between them happens to be.

Why does concentrate cost per ounce use container size, not the amount in one batch?

Container price and container size set your unit cost — what one fluid ounce of concentrate costs you, however much of it a single batch uses. “Concentrate per batch” is a different field: how many of those ounces one batch actually mixes in. Changing the batch amount changes chemical cost per batch; it does not change what an ounce costs you to buy.

This calculator vs. doing it in a spreadsheet

Total cost divided by total pounds is one cell anyone can build. It also assumes every order weighs the same and every washer runs full — neither is true, and both gaps land hardest on the smallest orders you accept, which is exactly where a minimum charge set from the average alone loses money on every one of them.

What each approach actually does

A spreadsheet built the obvious way takes your monthly machine, labour, supplies and overhead cost and divides it by your monthly pounds, or multiplies an average cost-per-pound rate straight through a weight. That gets you a number, and for an order that happens to be exactly average-sized, it can even be right. What it does not do on its own is cost a washer or dryer cycle as the whole-load, stepwise expense it actually is — a 22 lb order on a 20 lb washer runs two cycles, and the second one costs a full cycle whether it is 10% full or full — or separate the cost that scales with pounds (labour, supplies) from the cost that does not (intake, tagging, a delivery stop), which is a fixed dollar amount a small order has far fewer pounds to spread across.

This calculator costs one order at a time from your own rates: labour minutes per pound plus a fixed minutes-per-order figure, whole washer and dryer loads rounded up from your pounds-per-load capacity, supplies per pound, a delivery stop's drive time and mileage, and a share of monthly overhead. It runs that same arithmetic separately for your typical order and for the smallest order you accept, so the minimum-charge finding compares what a small order actually costs against what it actually brings in — not against an average that never applies to it.

Common questions

Can't I just work this out in a spreadsheet?

You can build one that gets a single number right for a single order size. Where a typical cost-per-pound spreadsheet slips is averaging: total monthly machine cost divided by total monthly pounds, or one wash-cost-per-pound cell multiplied straight through. That average is correct only if every order weighs the same and every washer runs full, which is never true. This calculator costs one order at a time from the rates you enter, so a small order and a large order each get their own, different, cost per pound — not a shared average.

What does a spreadsheet not catch that this calculator does?

Two things a flat per-pound formula hides. First, a washer or dryer cycle is a whole-load cost: a 22 lb order on a 20 lb washer runs two cycles, and the second one costs a full cycle whether it's 10% full or 100% full — a spreadsheet cell that multiplies pounds by a cost-per-pound rate never charges for that partial second load. Second, some cost is per order, not per pound: intake, tagging, staging, checkout, and the drive to a delivery stop cost the same on a 10 lb order as a 30 lb one, so the smallest order you accept carries the largest per-pound share of it — easy to miss when a spreadsheet's only weight-independent line is delivery, if it has one at all.

Why does a small order cost more per pound than a big one?

Because the per-order costs — intake and tagging minutes, a delivery stop, and any wasted capacity on a partly full machine — are a fixed dollar amount that a small order has fewer pounds to spread it across. A flat per-pound spreadsheet rate, built from an average order, understates the true cost of your smallest orders and can put your minimum charge below what they actually cost to process — a loss that repeats on every small order, invisibly, until someone checks the arithmetic against the specific weight rather than the average.

# Demand Charge Interval Analyzer

A free browser tool for businesses billed a demand charge: **paste your utility's 15-minute interval data and see what your worst 15 minutes cost each month**, whether a ratchet is carrying an old peak forward, and what holding demand to a lower cap would save and require.

**Live tool:** https://marisstella.app/demand-charge-interval-analyzer/

After using this, you can **tell whether your bill is set by a short spike you can shave or by a sustained load you cannot**, and size a peak-shaving battery or a load-staggering fix against real numbers instead of a vendor's estimate.

## Model

Readings are parsed as wall-clock timestamps plus a usage column (kW, or kWh per interval converted by interval length). Per month: peak = highest interval kW, billed = max(peak, ratchet% x highest peak of the prior 11 months in the data), charge = billed x rate, load factor = average / peak. A cap sweep recomputes billed demand with every interval limited to 97%...70% of the highest peak; saving = (charge before - after), scaled from complete months to 12. Battery energy = the largest contiguous stretch of kWh above the cap, divided by the usable share; cost = energy x $/kWh + kW shaved x $/kW; payback = cost / yearly saving.

**Battery dispatch (`battery.mjs`, 2026-11-01).** Sizes are run through your readings, not estimated from the worst stretch. For a fixed cap on grid draw the best control is greedy (discharge above the cap, recharge as fast as the cap and inverter allow), and it is exact for feasibility: if any control holds the cap, this one does. Feasibility only gets easier as the cap rises, so each month's lowest holdable cap is found by bisection; the ratchet is then recomputed from the shaved peaks (lower early peaks only lower later floors, so month-by-month is optimal). One-way efficiency is the square root of the round trip; loss kWh x your energy price is subtracted from the saving. Tests (`battery.test.mjs`) check the cap is tight (feasible at it, infeasible just under it) on random series, the kW-limited and kWh-limited cases by hand, and the ratchet. Not checked: any real site's bill, or a real controller. Perfect foresight of the month, a full battery at each month's start, no degradation: an upper bound on real savings.

**Time-of-use bill and energy arbitrage (`tou.mjs`, 2026-11-11).** Optional inputs: an on-peak window (start hour, end hour exclusive, weekdays only or every day), an on-peak demand charge, and on-peak and off-peak energy prices. The bill is facility demand (with the ratchet) + on-peak demand + energy. One battery (the size you enter, else the fastest-payback size from the sweep) is planned per month in two steps: (1) the facility and on-peak caps with the lowest demand cost it can hold, found by a 32-point scan of the facility cap, a 16-point refinement, and for each facility cap a bisection on the on-peak cap, using the greedy controller generalised to a cap that changes by interval (still exact for feasibility); (2) a backward dynamic programme over state of charge, about ten charge levels per interval's worth of inverter power, that minimises the energy bill within those caps with no export and the inverter limit, starting full and paying to refill at the cheapest price at month end. Each month keeps the cheapest of no battery, greedy peak shaving, the programme at the caps, and the programme with no caps (energy only). Tests (`tou.test.mjs`) check the window flags (weekends, wrap past midnight), the arbitrage saving against hand arithmetic (lossless: kWh x spread x weekdays; 81% round trip: 0.9 x 40 x on-peak price - 40 / 0.9 x off-peak price per day, within 1%), that a spread below the loss is not traded, that an off-peak spike is left alone when only on-peak demand is charged, that the on-peak cap is tight, and that no month is ever billed more than without a battery. Steps (1) then (2) is not a joint optimum of demand and energy cost; the next paragraph measures how much that cost and replaces it.

**Joint optimum (`lp.mjs`, `jointLP` in `tou.mjs`, 2026-11-13).** Each month is now also solved as one linear programme over the whole month: per interval, grid-side charge and delivered discharge (each up to the inverter kW), stored energy (0 to usable kWh, starting full, losses split as the square root of the round trip each way), grid draw (no export) and its slack to the facility and on-peak caps, which are variables priced at the two demand rates; the energy bill plus a month-end refill at the cheapest price is the rest of the objective. The solver is ours, with no dependencies: a Mehrotra predictor-corrector interior-point method whose normal equations are banded (rows in time order, band 4) apart from the two cap columns, which are folded in with the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury identity, plus iterative refinement. The dispatch it returns is re-simulated within the battery's limits before it is billed, so a solver that stopped short of tolerance can only cost money in the plan, never misreport it. The cheapest of no battery, peak shaving, the two-step plan, arbitrage only and the joint plan is kept.

*Why it was worth doing, measured on real meters* (`check/`): five buildings from Open Power System Data's household data package (three small industrial sites and two public buildings in Konstanz, Germany, 15-minute grid import, CC BY 4.0; 57 complete building-months, `check/fetch_opsd.py`), three illustrative tariffs (demand-heavy: $15/kW facility, flat $0.12/kWh; both demand charges: $10 facility + $12 on-peak 12:00-18:00 weekdays, $0.20/$0.10; energy-heavy: $4 + $4, on-peak 16:00-21:00 weekdays, $0.35/$0.10) and four battery sizes (10% and 25% of the building's peak kW, 1 and 2 hours, 90% usable, 88% round trip), 684 cases. Each was solved exactly by HiGHS (SciPy) as an independent reference (`check/joint_gap.py`). The two-step plan alone (`--no-joint`) captured 100.0% of the optimal saving under the demand-heavy tariff (worst month 0.02% of its bill above optimal), but 94.6% under both demand charges (worst 2.41%) and 93.4% under the energy-heavy tariff (worst 7.94%), and was more than 1% of the bill above optimal in 78 of the 456 time-of-use cases. Two causes: fixing the lowest caps first stops the battery trading a little facility peak for on-peak energy (one industrial month: lowest caps 25.3/20.2 kW, optimum 34.6/12.7 kW), and the programme's charge steps are coarse (in the 20 worst cases, the programme at the optimal caps still left 0.3-3.4% of the bill). With the joint plan, all 684 cases are within $0.03 of HiGHS per month; it is the plan kept in 559, and plain peak shaving ties it in the other 125. Both result files are in `check/`.

**The ratchet inside the plan (`yearLP` in `tou.mjs`, 2026-11-14).** With a ratchet, months are not independent: once an earlier peak's floor sets a month's bill, shaving that month's facility peak below the floor earns nothing, and a month whose peak sets later floors is worth more than one month's charge per kW. When the floor binds in at least one month of the month-by-month plan, the whole period is also solved as one linear programme: every month's jointLP block (battery full at each month's start, as before), tied only through billed demand, B_m >= Cf_m and B_m >= ratchet x Cf_k for the 11 months before, with the facility rate on B_m. The month caps and billed demands are dense columns in the same banded interior-point solver (three per month, Woodbury), about 120,000 rows for a year of 15-minute data. The cheaper of the two plans over the period is kept. When no floor binds the year is not solved, and that is exact, not a shortcut: billed demand is never below a month's own peak, so the year's bill is at least the sum of each month's best bill alone, which the month-by-month plan then reaches.

*Measured* (`check/ratchet_gap.py`): twelve full-year buildings from NREL's ComStock (AMY2018 release 1, six building types in Texas and Colorado, chosen by a fixed rule in `check/fetch_comstock.py`; these are EnergyPlus simulations on real 2018 weather, not meters, CC BY 4.0), the three tariffs above with an 80% ratchet, and two batteries (10% and 25% of the building's peak kW, 2 hours), 72 building-years. Each was also solved exactly as one whole-year LP by HiGHS (SciPy). Planning month by month and applying the ratchet afterwards captured 99.5% of the optimal saving under the demand-heavy tariff, 99.3% under both demand charges and 99.0% under the energy-heavy one, at worst 0.56% of a year's bill ($660 a year for a Texas standalone retail store, both demand charges, the larger battery). The shipped plan is within $2.29 a year of HiGHS in all 72 (most within $0.50), and the whole-period plan is the one kept in 55. In total it recovers $4,154 a year of the $544,080 the optimal plans save across the 72 cases: worth having, not a different answer. Its cost is time: 7 to 15 s more per year of data in Node, so the page shows a working message while it runs. Results: `check/ratchet_gap_results.json`.

Not checked: a real tariff's fine print (some ratchets use a seasonal window or only summer peaks), or any metered building with a ratchet. The joint plan takes roughly 0.1 to 0.5 s per month in Node, so a year of 15-minute data takes a few seconds in a browser.

**Reading the file as the utility gives it (`greenbutton.mjs`, 2026-11-14).** Two fixes to input, the step every result depends on. (1) *Opower-layout CSV.* Many Green Button CSV downloads (PG&E, ComEd and other Opower-hosted utilities) put the date and the start time in separate columns (`TYPE,DATE,START TIME,END TIME,USAGE,UNITS,...`). The parser used the date column alone, so every reading in a day got the same timestamp, 95 of 96 were dropped as duplicates, and the page refused the file as "more than two hours apart". It now combines a date-only column with the start-time column (24-hour or AM/PM), and reads the unit from the value column's header or a `UNITS` column: an untouched "Readings are" selector follows the file, and one the user changed is flagged when it disagrees, since kW against kWh per 15 minutes is a 4x error in every peak. (2) *Green Button XML (ESPI).* The file picker now takes the XML download. Each ReadingType keeps its own unit (Wh or W) and power-of-ten multiplier, and IntervalBlocks are tied to it through their MeterReading's `related` link, so a file with both delivered and received (solar export) series uses the delivered one and says what it skipped, instead of mixing them; gas and water series are refused with a reason. Times are converted from UTC to the local wall clock with the file's `tzOffset` and its daylight-saving rules (`dstStartRule`/`dstEndRule`, the 32-bit ESPI encoding: seconds, hour, day of week, day of month, operator, month), because on-peak windows and billing months run on the local clock: a reader that applies only `tzOffset` puts every summer reading an hour early, so a 16:00-21:00 window bills 17:00-22:00. The converted readings are shown in the box as CSV, so what is analyzed is visible. Tests (`greenbutton.test.mjs`) check the decoder against the US rules as Green Button files write them (`360E2000` = second Sunday of March 02:00, `B40E2000` = first Sunday of November 02:00, built independently by an encoder in the test), the EU last-Sunday and Sydney (wrapping the year end) rules, the exact minutes of spring-forward and fall-back, per-type multipliers, series choice, and an Opower CSV through to a month's peak. A synthetic year of 15-minute XML (12 MB) reads in about 0.4 s in Node. Assumptions: rule times are the wall clock at the moment of change (2:00 standard time in March, 2:00 daylight time in November), as US and EU law state them; files that mean otherwise move only the hour around each change. In the repeated fall-back hour the second reading of each wall-clock quarter-hour is dropped (noted on the page). Not checked: a real utility's XML file (none was reachable from the build machine; the fixtures follow the ESPI schema's structure). `ventures/service-load-check` uses the same file (its tests fail if the copies drift).

**Your utility's actual rate (`tariff.mjs`, 2026-11-20).** The time-of-use inputs above are one window, all year. Real commercial rates have summer and winter seasons, mid-peak and on-peak periods, a demand charge for each period, an all-hours demand charge that can change by season, riders and a fixed charge. Most US rates are in OpenEI's Utility Rate Database (URDB), so the page now takes a URDB rate as JSON (the API's response, a bare rate, or a REopt input with `ElectricTariff.urdb_response`). It reads the energy and demand rate structures (rate + `adj`, so riders count) and their 12-month x 24-hour weekday and weekend schedules, `flatdemandstructure` with `flatdemandmonths`, `demandwindow` (readings are averaged into a longer window, and a shorter one is flagged because peaks inside a reading are not seen) and `fixedmonthlycharge`. URDB schedules have no holiday table, so holidays are optional: none (as written), the six NERC holidays, or the eleven US federal holidays, on the weekend schedule. What it does not model is reported, not dropped: tiers past the first (first tier's price used), kVA units, coincident system-peak charges, `demandattrs` discounts. The bill is priced month by month, period by period. The battery is planned by the same banded interior-point LP, generalised so each demand period that occurs in the month (with a positive rate) is its own cap column (`jointLPPeriods` in `tou.mjs`; one period per interval, so the band stays 4), the month's flat rate prices the facility cap, and the ratchet, when one is entered, links the months through `yearLP`, which now takes per-month periods, rates and facility rates. Each month keeps the cheapest of no battery, facility peak shaving, arbitrage only and the joint optimum. Tests (`tariff.test.mjs`) check both real rates' parsing (`fixtures/urdb/`, copied from NREL REopt.jl's test inputs, Apache-2.0, see its NOTICE; old versions of the rates, not today's prices), refusals, holiday dates and observed-day rules, window averaging, a two-period hand case (a 5 kW battery takes 5 kW off a mid-peak spike, an on-peak spike and the facility peak: 5 x (5.30 + 18.11 + 13.20) a month), that a one-window rate written in URDB form gives `touBattery`'s answer, that `jointLPPeriods` with one period is `jointLP`, and that the battery never raises a month's bill.

*Measured* (`check/tariff_gap.py`): twelve ComStock buildings (AMY2018, six types each in California and Arkansas, the same fixed rule as `fetch_comstock.py`, now with `--states CA,AR`; simulations, not meters), SCE's GS-2 TOU option B (2015) on the California ones and Entergy Arkansas's Large Power Service TOU (2018) on the Arkansas ones, two batteries (10% and 25% of peak kW, 2 hours, 90% usable, 88% round trip), no ratchet: 24 building-years, 288 months. (1) Exact: every month was also solved by HiGHS (SciPy) with each demand period its own cap variable; the shipped month bill was at most $0.29 above it (a $51,173 school month), median $0.004, at most $0.62 over a building-year. About 2.5 s per building-year in Node. (2) What the one-window form said: each rate turned into the single window a person would type from its summer section (fixed rule in `check/tariff_gap.mjs`: July weekday hours of the dearest demand period, its rate, July's flat rate, the energy prices at the window's start and at 03:00). Under SCE it claimed $137,803 a year of battery savings across the twelve cases where the real rate gives $88,405 (+56%; every case over, by 40% to 60%), because it charged summer on-peak demand all year and SCE's winter has no time-of-use demand charge. Under Entergy it claimed $80,786 where the rate gives $98,688 (-18%; 10 of 12 under, the larger battery on the medium office by 39%), because it missed the winter on-peak charge and the off-peak demand charges that apply in every hour. Per-case results: `check/tariff_gap_results.json`.

Not modelled: tiered prices past the first tier, power-factor and kVA charges, coincident peaks, degradation, incentives, controls, export credits.

## Run

Open `index.html` through any static server. Tests: `node --test *.test.mjs` (`lp.test.mjs` checks the solver's optimality certificate on random banded LPs). The real-tariff comparison: `python3 check/fetch_comstock.py --states CA,AR --out comstock_ca_ar.json`, then `python3 check/tariff_gap.py` (about 3 minutes). The ratchet comparison: `python3 check/fetch_comstock.py` (needs pyarrow), then `python3 check/ratchet_gap.py` (about 6 minutes on 4 cores). The real-meter comparison: download the OPSD file named in `check/fetch_opsd.py`, run it, then `python3 check/joint_gap.py` (needs numpy, scipy and node); results are in `check/joint_gap_twostep.json` (two-step plan) and `check/joint_gap_results.json` (with the joint plan).

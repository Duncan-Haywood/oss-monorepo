Common questions

Can't I just compute this in a spreadsheet?

The self-employment-tax adjustment and the 20% effective employer rate are one-time formulas a spreadsheet can hold. What is easy to get wrong building that formula yourself is the circularity IRS Publication 560's worksheet exists to resolve: a plan stating a 25% employer contribution rate is not simply 25% of net profit, because the contribution itself reduces the earnings it is computed on. The standard shortcut is dividing by 1 plus the stated rate (25% / 1.25 = 20%), and a spreadsheet only applies that shortcut if whoever built the sheet already knew to.

Why does my age change which catch-up applies?

For 2026 there are two different catch-up amounts, and they don't stack. Ages 50-59 and 64-plus get the standard $8,000 catch-up. Ages 60 through 63 get a SECURE 2.0 enhanced catch-up of $11,250 instead of the standard amount, not in addition to it. A spreadsheet cell keyed to a single age-50 threshold will quietly hand a 61-year-old the wrong catch-up figure and understate the true limit by $3,250.

What does a spreadsheet miss about a W-2 job's own 401(k)?

The employee elective-deferral limit applies per person across every 401(k) plan combined, not per plan -- so deferrals already made at a separate W-2 employer's plan reduce the room left for the Solo 401(k) dollar for dollar. A spreadsheet built around the Solo 401(k) alone has no column for this unless someone remembers to add one, and the shortfall only shows up as an excess contribution after the fact.

Does this replace IRS Publication 560?

No. Publication 560's worksheet is the primary source this calculator applies -- it does not replace reading it, and it is not tax advice. What it replaces is re-deriving the circular 20%/25% conversion and the two catch-up tiers correctly under this specific year's published figures every time you want a number.

This calculator vs. a bare labor-and-materials guess

"Call it eight hours at $28, plus materials" is a price for the labor and the materials — and nothing else. Burden, overhead, schedule delay, and margin are real costs whether or not anyone says them out loud.

What a bare guess is

Standing on the customer's porch, a bare guess is the fastest way to answer "what will that cost": hours times an hourly wage, plus whatever materials come to mind, said out loud or scribbled on a business card. It is honest arithmetic on the two numbers it uses — it is just missing the other four that make up a real change-order price.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the added labor hours, hourly wage, payroll burden, materials, subcontractor cost, any days added to the schedule, overhead, and a target margin, and it returns a walk-away cost floor and a target price that carries all four costs a bare guess leaves out — not just the labor and materials a verbal number usually covers.

What a bare labor-and-materials guess doesWhat this calculator does instead
Multiplies hours by the wage on the paycheck, with nothing added for the employer's own payroll taxes and insurance. Loads the wage with a payroll-burden percentage before multiplying by hours, so the labor line reflects what the hour actually costs the business, not just what it pays the worker.
Has no line for overhead, so a job that keeps the shop running — insurance, tools, the office — contributes nothing toward that cost. Applies an overhead percentage across the full direct cost: labor, materials, and subcontractor cost together.
Prices the scope of work and stops there, even when the added work pushes the schedule and keeps a supervisor, trailer, or rented equipment on site longer. Carries a separate days-added and extended-general-conditions field, priced like any other direct cost, so schedule delay is not quietly absorbed.
Has no target margin, so the number that comes out of the guess is a cost estimate, not a price with profit built in. Divides the cost floor by one minus the target margin, so the price already has the profit the business needs baked in.
Gives no way to check a contract's markup cap before the number is said out loud. Flags when a contract's markup cap sits at or below the overhead rate, before a proposed price is even entered.

A bare labor-and-materials guess is not wrong because it is quick — it is wrong because burden, overhead, schedule delay, and margin are costs the business carries whether or not the guess accounts for them. This calculator does not know your wages or your market; it replaces the arithmetic a bare guess skips.

Common questions

Why is a bare hours-times-wage number the wrong price for a change order?

Because it leaves out four costs that are real whether or not anyone writes them down: payroll burden on the labor, overhead on the whole job, any schedule delay the added scope causes, and the profit margin the business needs to stay in business. A number said out loud on the customer's porch — "call it eight hours at $28, plus materials" — prices the labor and the materials and nothing else.

How far short does a bare guess usually land?

On this calculator's own shipped default job — 8 hours at $28/hour, 25% burden, $350 in materials, 10% overhead, a 20% target margin — a bare guess of hours times wage plus materials comes to $574.00. The walk-away cost floor is $693.00, so the guess is already $119.00 under cost before any profit is added, and $292.25 under the $866.25 target price. On a larger job with a subcontractor and three days of schedule delay, the same kind of guess is $2,029.50 under the cost floor.

Which of the four missing costs usually does the most damage?

It depends on the job. Payroll burden (25% in the default example) and overhead (10%) apply to every job and are the two a bare guess always misses. Schedule delay — extended general conditions from days the added scope keeps a supervisor, trailer, or rented equipment on site — applies only when the change pushes the schedule, but when it does it can outweigh the other three combined: three days at $450 adds $1,350 of direct cost by itself, more than doubling a $1,000-range job.

Is a quick verbal number ever close enough?

On a very small, no-delay change with low burden and overhead rates, a bare guess can land close to the floor without much room to spare — but it still has no profit margin built in, and it has no way to see a schedule delay coming. The gap only grows from there: it is $119.00 on this calculator's own small default job and $2,029.50 on a job that adds a subcontractor and three days of delay, which is the risk of pricing a change order the same way regardless of its size.

Does this calculator know what my labor rate or overhead should be?

No. It has no data on wages, burden rates, or overhead for any trade or market. It takes the labor hours, wage, burden, materials, subcontractor cost, schedule delay, overhead, and target margin you enter and turns them into a cost floor and target price — the arithmetic a bare guess skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that a quick verbal number is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works until burden, overhead, delay, or margin turn out to matter more than the job seemed to need. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it.

This calculator vs. a rough lump sum

A round-number change-order price is built from what's visible on the takeoff — labor, materials, the sub — rounded up by a margin that feels safe. Two costs that never show up on a takeoff are exactly what it misses.

What a rough lump sum is

A lump sum is the fastest way to price a change order: look at the added scope, add up the labor hours and material cost you can see, pick a number that feels like a comfortable markup over that, and send it. It works well enough often enough that most contractors keep doing it, because writing a full worksheet for a one-day punch-list item is not worth the time.

The guess is built entirely from costs that appear on a takeoff. Two costs that do not routinely get left out: the extra days the added scope keeps a supervisor, a trailer, or rented equipment on the site after the original schedule would have wrapped, and the ceiling a contract's own markup-cap clause puts on what can actually be billed once the contractor's overhead comes out of that same allowance. A lump sum that never asked about either one has no way to know it is short by exactly what they would have added.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the added labor hours, loaded wage, materials, subcontractor cost, the days the schedule slips and what a day of holding the site open costs, your overhead rate, target margin, and your contract's markup-cap percentage — zero if it has none. It returns a walk-away cost floor, a target price, and, when a cap applies, what the cap actually permits to bill and what margin survives once overhead comes out of it.

A worked example, computed from this calculator's own arithmetic: a scope change needs 24 labor hours at $32/hour with 28% burden ($983.04 loaded), $1,800 in materials, and a $2,200 subcontractor, and adds 4 days to the schedule at $350/day in general conditions (supervision, trailer, rented equipment) — $1,400. Direct cost is $6,383.04; with 10% overhead, the walk-away price is $7,021.34, and a 20% target margin would call for a price of $8,776.68. A lump-sum guess built only from the visible labor, materials, and sub cost ($4,983.04) and rounded up to $6,000 to feel safely profitable is actually $1,021.34 under the walk-away floor, because it never priced the four days the job site stayed open. A higher guess of $7,500 clears that floor — but this contract caps change-order markup at cost plus 15%, which permits billing only $7,340.50, nowhere near the $8,776.68 the target margin called for. The extra $159.50 above the cap would be disputed or unbillable, and what survives the cap after overhead is a 4.3% margin, far short of the 20% target the contractor thought they were pricing to.

Common questions

Why does a rough lump sum under-price a change order?

Because it is built from the costs a contractor can see on the takeoff — labor hours, materials, a subcontractor's number — rounded up by a margin that feels comfortable. Two costs that do not show up on a takeoff routinely get left out entirely: the extra days the added scope keeps a supervisor, a trailer, or rented equipment on site, and the ceiling a contract's own markup cap puts on what can actually be billed once overhead comes out of the same allowance. A guess that never asked about either one is short by exactly the amount they would have added.

Can a change order price above cost still lose money?

Yes, once a contract's markup cap is in the picture. Many change-order clauses cap markup at a percentage of cost — "cost plus 15%", for example — and that allowance has to cover the contractor's own overhead as well as profit. A guess that clears the walk-away cost floor can still land above what the cap actually permits to bill, or clear the cap but leave only a few points of margin once overhead is taken out of it, and a round number gives no way to see which one happened before the work is done.

What does a rough lump sum get right?

For a small, fast change with no schedule impact and a contract with no markup cap, a quick round-number guess set comfortably above the visible labor and material cost can land close enough. The risk is the guess not knowing which change orders that is true for — a lump sum that worked on last week's punch-list item does not know that this week's change adds four days to the schedule or runs into a 15% cap.

Does this calculator know what markup my contract allows?

No. It has no access to your contract. You enter the markup cap percentage from your own change-order clause — zero if there is none — and the calculator applies it to the cost it computed, showing what is actually billable under the cap and what margin that leaves after overhead. Reading the clause is still yours to do; the arithmetic once you have is what this replaces.

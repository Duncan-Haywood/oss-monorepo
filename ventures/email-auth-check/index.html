Common questions

Why is my email going to spam?

The most common technical causes are a missing or misconfigured SPF record, no DKIM signing, or a missing/monitor-only (p=none) DMARC record -- mailbox providers use all three as spam and spoofing signals. This tool reads exactly what your domain's DNS currently says for each one and names what to fix. It cannot see reputation, content, or sending-volume factors, which also affect spam placement.

What does "too many SPF DNS lookups" mean, and why does it break everything?

RFC 7208 caps SPF evaluation at 10 DNS lookups (each include:, a, mx, ptr, exists mechanism and each redirect= modifier counts, and include: chains recursively). Go over 10 and the result is a permanent error (PermError) -- meaning SPF fails for every message from your domain, for every receiver, whether or not the message is legitimate. This is usually caused by stacking one include: per marketing or support tool over time until the total quietly crosses 10.

What is DMARC p=none and why is it a problem?

p=none means DMARC is in monitor-only mode: receivers are asked to take no action even on a message that fails alignment. It is the most common real-world gap, because a domain with SPF, DKIM, and DMARC all present looks fully configured while still being completely spoofable. p=none is a reasonable first step while you review the aggregate reports it generates; moving to p=quarantine or p=reject is what actually stops spoofed mail.

Does a clean report mean my email is fully secure?

No. This reads SPF, DKIM, DMARC and BIMI DNS records and reports what they say -- it is evidence about your domain's mail-authentication configuration, not a determination that your email is secure or that mail will reach the inbox. Deliverability also depends on sender reputation, content, list hygiene, and each receiver's own policies, none of which live in DNS.

Why check nine DKIM selector names instead of just looking up DKIM directly?

DKIM keys are published at a selector-specific name (<selector>._domainkey.yourdomain.com), and the selector is chosen by whichever provider sends your mail -- there is no single fixed name to look up. This tool checks the selector names commonly used by Google Workspace, Microsoft 365, Mailchimp/Mandrill, SendGrid, and a few generic defaults (google, selector1, selector2, k1, s1, s2, default, dkim, mail). If none of those resolve, that is not proof DKIM is missing -- check a sent message's own DKIM-Signature header, or your provider's dashboard, for the exact selector it uses.

How to value a laundromat before you make an offer: a worked example

The asking price is the seller's number. Yours comes from the cash the store really leaves a hands-off owner, and from what a lender will let that cash pay for. Work out both before you make an offer, and offer under the lower.

The worked example

A store takes in $360,000 a year. Utilities cost $98,000, rent $60,000, attendants $42,000, repairs $18,000 and insurance, supplies and fees $14,000. You will not work the store, so a manager costs $36,000, and you set aside $15,000 a year to replace machines. Adjusted cash flow is $77,000.

At 3.5x that is $269,500. A 10-year loan at 10.5% covering 85% of the price, with 1.25x coverage required, could carry a price up to $447,565. The lower, the multiple, makes the walk-away price $269,500. Three prices against it:

But it is 4.42 times cash flow, and with revenue down 10% coverage falls to 1.09x, thin enough that a bad winter becomes a missed payment. A drop in revenue of about 15.2% uses up the whole cushion even at the walk-away price.

Common questions

How much should I pay for a laundromat?

Price it off adjusted cash flow, not revenue: take the yearly revenue, subtract utilities, rent, labor, repairs, insurance and supplies, a paid manager if you will not work the store, and a reserve for replacing machines. Buyers commonly pay a multiple of that number, often around 3 to 4 times, then check that the cash flow can pay the loan with room to spare. The lower of the two limits is the price to walk away at.

What is a good debt service coverage ratio for a laundromat loan?

Lenders usually want the cash flow to cover the yearly loan payments at least 1.25 times, so a 25% cushion. This calculator solves for the largest loan a cash flow can carry at the coverage you enter, then shows what happens to coverage if revenue falls 10% or 20%.

Why include a manager and a machine reserve if I will run it myself?

Because the next owner may not, and because the books you are shown rarely pay the owner for their own hours. Counting a manager keeps the price honest if you ever step back, and a reserve for washers and dryers stops an old equipment room from looking like free cash flow.

How it is calculated

Price response: weekly units are regressed on price in logs, ln(units) = a + e·ln(price/current) + c·week, by ordinary least squares; e is the elasticity and c the weekly fade in demand. The time term is dropped if price and time move together too closely to separate them (correlation above 0.85) or there are fewer than 10 weeks. The slope is then combined with a typical-retail prior of -2.0 (standard deviation 1.0, a judgement, not a measured constant) by inverse-variance weighting, so noisy data counts for less; the standard error has a floor of 0.15. A positive slope is set aside. Demand: each week's sales are a negative-binomial draw with mean base × (price/current)e × fadeweek and variance mean + (k·mean)², where k is the residual spread of your fit (default 0.25). The plan: a finite-horizon dynamic program over (week, units left, current price level). Prices are the current price less 0, 10, 20… up to your maximum in steps of 10%, and only move downward. Each week it picks the price that maximises expected revenue now plus the best expected value for next week's stock, less any price-change cost; at the end each unsold unit is worth your salvage value. Comparisons: the simple rules are evaluated by the same recursion, so the gap is exact, not simulated. Range of outcomes: 4,000 simulated seasons of the plan with a fixed seed. Robustness: the plan is re-evaluated, then re-optimised, at the low and high ends of the 80% interval on elasticity. Above 1,000 units the plan is computed in lots, which is slightly smoother.

Not modelled: competitors' prices, promotions or ads, stock that can be reordered, cannibalisation of other products, buyers who wait for the next cut, channel or shipping costs, and price points such as 9.99. Cost is treated as sunk: it changes the profit shown, not the plan. Unverified: checked against simulated data with known elasticity and against closed-form cases in unit tests, not against a published retailer dataset or a commercial pricing tool.

Common questions

How deep and how soon should I mark down leftover inventory?

It depends on three things: how many units you have against how many you expect to sell at full price, how many weeks are left before the stock is worth little or nothing, and how strongly buyers respond to a lower price. This tool puts those into a dynamic program that, for every possible stock level in every remaining week, picks the price that maximises expected revenue plus what the leftovers will fetch. With plenty of stock the plan starts cutting early and deepens in steps; with little stock it holds the price, because every unit will sell anyway.

What is price elasticity and how is it measured here?

Elasticity is the percentage change in units sold for a 1% change in price. An elasticity of -2 means a 10% price cut sells about 20% more units. The tool measures it from your own history by regressing the log of weekly units on the log of price, with a time trend so that demand fading over the season is not mistaken for a price effect. The estimate is then blended with a typical retail value of -2 in proportion to how noisy your data is, so a thin history cannot produce a wild number.

How much sales history do I need?

At least 8 weeks that had sales, and the price has to have moved by at least 5% in that time; if you never changed the price, your data cannot say how buyers respond to it. More price variation helps more than more weeks at the same price. If your history is too thin, enter an elasticity yourself: the tool shows how much the plan would lose if your number is off.

Why not just mark everything 30% off near the end?

Sometimes that is nearly as good, and the tool shows you the comparison. A fixed cut ignores how many units you actually have left: if you are well ahead of pace it discounts stock that would have sold at full price, and if you are far behind it cuts too late. The dynamic plan reads the stock you actually hold each week.

What do the behind and ahead columns mean?

A plan is only useful if you can follow it after the first week goes differently. For each week the tool shows the markdown it would choose if you are on the planned stock level, if you have 25% more units left than planned (behind), and if you have 25% fewer (ahead). Check your stock at the start of each week and read the row.

What does salvage value mean?

It is what you get for each unit that is still unsold at the end of the window: a liquidator's price, a return-to-vendor credit, or a donation receipt value. Enter a negative number if leftovers cost you money to store or dispose of. A better salvage route means the plan discounts less, because unsold stock hurts less.

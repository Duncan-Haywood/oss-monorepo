This calculator vs. a flat $140-a-linear-foot rate

"$140 a linear foot" is not a price for a retaining wall — it is a price for a 40 ft, 3 ft garden wall, quietly applied to every taller, geogrid-reinforced, cornered, or engineered wall the truck pulls up to.

What a flat $140-a-linear-foot rate is

A flat rate per linear foot is the number every homeowner can check with a tape measure along the ground, which is exactly why it is also the number competing bids get compared on. It is fast to quote over the phone and easy to put on a postcard: one number, times the run's length, read back as a total.

The number behind it was set for one job — usually the shortest, lowest wall a company is willing to quote sight unseen: a 40 ft run at 3 ft exposed, segmental block, no corners, no geogrid, no engineer stamp, no permit — and then reused for every wall afterward, whether or not the next one looks anything like that wall.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the wall's length, exposed height, embedment below grade, wall type, drain-stone volume, geogrid layers, corners, and the engineer/permit step, plus your loaded labor and overhead. It prices the face area, the drainage, the geogrid, and the extras separately, and returns a walk-away price for the wall you describe — for this wall, not a generic one.

What a flat rate per linear foot doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same rate for a 3 ft garden wall and a 6 ft engineered wall, even though face area — length times exposed-plus-embedment height — roughly doubles between them. Prices face material and face labor from length times total height, so a taller wall costs more per foot, not the same per foot.
Has no separate line for drain stone or geogrid, so a wall needing two reinforcement layers is quoted the same as one needing none. Prices drain-stone volume and geogrid square yards from the entered depth and layer count, so reinforcement shows up as real material and labor.
Ignores the engineer stamp and permit fee that many jurisdictions require once a wall clears roughly 4 ft exposed — often the single largest line a per-foot rate misses. Adds the entered engineer-and-permit fee as its own cost, so a tall wall's paperwork shows up in the price before the crew is on site.
Bakes payroll burden into a round per-foot number nobody re-derives, so the rate quietly drifts from the crew's real loaded cost. Loads the entered hourly wage by the entered payroll-burden percentage every time, so labor cost tracks the actual burden rather than a memorized rate.
Gives the crew a rate to quote, not a number to check a quote against when a homeowner asks to match a cheaper flat rate on a harder wall. Reports a walk-away price built from the entered scope, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost and target margin before the crew commits to it.

A flat rate per linear foot is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is applied outside the one wall it was built for, and nothing about a postcard rate tells you when that has happened. This calculator does not replace judgment about your market or an engineer's determination on a tall wall; it replaces the arithmetic a flat rate skips.

Common questions

Why does a flat $140-a-linear-foot retaining-wall rate get the price wrong?

Because linear feet is only the measurement a homeowner can read off a tape measure along the ground. Almost nothing else in a wall scales with it. Face material is bought by the square foot of face -- length times (exposed height plus embedment) -- so a taller wall means more face for the same length. Drain stone is a volume behind the wall. Geogrid is square yards per layer, and taller walls need more layers: none under 4 ft exposed, one at 4 ft, another every 2 ft after that. An engineer stamp and a permit arrive as a step, not a foot, once a wall clears the height most jurisdictions treat as the line. A flat per-foot rate charges all of that the same as the shortest wall it was set on.

What job is the $140-a-linear-foot number actually built around?

One specific job, usually the shortest, shortest-height wall a company is willing to quote off a phone call: a 40 ft run at 3 ft exposed, segmental block, no corners, no geogrid, no engineer or permit. It is a marketing price meant to win the estimate over a competitor's bid, not a number derived from any particular wall's height, drainage, or geogrid.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

On this calculator's own shipped wall presets, at a $38 crew wage, 30% payroll burden, 15% overhead, and a 22% target margin: the 40 ft, 3 ft garden wall the $140 rate is named for costs $4,269.06 to build and this calculator prices it at $5,473.16, so the flat $5,600.00 rate actually clears a 23.8% margin on it -- close to the shop's own 22% target. But the same $140/lf on a 40 ft, 4 ft wall needing its first geogrid layer reproduces $5,600.00 against a $5,840.12 break-even cost -- a -4.3% margin, a $240.12 loss, not a profit. On a 50 ft, 6 ft engineered wall with two geogrid layers, the flat $7,000.00 loses $3,093.35 against this calculator's $10,093.35 cost, a -44.2% margin. And an 80 ft wall at 4 ft with two corners nets a thin 1.8% margin -- $203.55 over cost -- on a flat $11,200.00, against this calculator's $14,098.01 walk-away price.

Is a flat rate per linear foot ever the right way to quote a retaining wall?

For a short wall close to the one the rate was priced on, a flat rate can land close to a fair margin -- this calculator's own numbers show $140/lf clearing near the target margin on the easiest preset. The risk is quoting a taller, geogrid-reinforced, cornered, or engineered wall at the same rate sight unseen, because the rate does not know the face area, the drain-stone volume, the geogrid layers, or the engineer step until the takeoff is actually done, and that is exactly where the job stops being profitable.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local material or labor rates, and it is not an engineered wall design, a permit approval, or a geotechnical determination. It takes the length, exposed height, embedment, wall type, drainage, geogrid layers, corners, and the engineer/permit step you enter, plus your own wage, payroll burden, overhead, and target margin, and turns that into a price for this wall -- the arithmetic a flat per-linear-foot rate skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat per-linear-foot rate is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works until the wall stops matching the one the rate was set on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it.

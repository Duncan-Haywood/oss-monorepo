This calculator vs. a flat "$65 a linear foot" retaining wall price

"$65 a foot installed" is not a price for a retaining wall — it is a price for one short garden wall, quietly applied to every wall the same length regardless of how tall, how reinforced, or how permitted it is.

What a flat per-foot price is

A flat per-foot rate is the fastest way to quote: one number, multiplied by length, read off a postcard or a website. Homeowners shop it because it is easy to compare between bids, and it is the number a customer holding a competitor's flyer will ask you to match on their own wall.

The rate was set for one wall — usually the cheapest configuration (a short garden wall, segmental block, minimal embedment, no geogrid, no permit) — and then reused for every wall of that length afterward, whether or not the next wall is anywhere near as short, as light, or as unpermitted as the one the rate was built on.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the wall's length, exposed height, embedment below grade, wall type, drain-stone volume, geogrid layers, corners, whether the job needs a permit only or an engineer stamp plus permit, and your loaded labor and overhead. It prices the face area, the drainage, the geogrid, and the engineer/permit step separately, and returns a walk-away price for the wall you describe — for this wall, not a generic one of the same length.

What a flat per-foot price doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same rate per foot whether the wall is 3 ft or 6 ft exposed, and whether it needs zero geogrid layers or two — several times the material and labor for the same length. Prices face area from length × (exposed height + embedment), so taller walls cost more even at the same length.
Has no line for drain stone, geogrid, or corners — a per-foot number cannot carry a volume behind the wall or a square-yard layer count. Prices drain-stone cubic yards, geogrid square yards per layer, and corner cuts as their own items, so that scope shows up instead of being absorbed into the per-foot rate.
Treats an engineer stamp and a permit — often thousands of dollars on a wall over about 4 ft — as if they were not there, because a flat rate has no step for them. Applies the engineer/permit fee as its own line once the wall crosses the height where one is typically required.
Bakes payroll burden into a round per-foot rate nobody re-derives, so the rate quietly drifts from the crew's real loaded cost. Loads the entered hourly wage by the entered payroll-burden percentage every time, so labor cost tracks the actual burden rather than a memorized rate.
Gives the crew a price to quote, not a number to check a quote against when a homeowner asks to match a cheaper per-foot rate on a taller wall. Reports a walk-away price built from the entered scope, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before the crew commits to it.

A flat per-foot price is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is applied outside the one wall it was built for, and nothing about a postcard rate tells you when that has happened. This calculator does not replace judgment about your market; it replaces the arithmetic a flat rate skips.

Common questions

Why does a flat "$65 a linear foot" retaining wall price get the price wrong?

Because almost nothing in a wall scales with length alone. Face material is bought by length × (exposed height + embedment), so a 3 ft wall and a 6 ft wall of the same length carry a very different face area. Drain stone is a volume behind the wall, geogrid is square yards per layer and taller walls need more layers, and an engineer stamp plus permit arrives as a step, not a foot. A per-foot rate set on a short garden wall has nowhere to put any of that on a taller job of the same length.

What does a flat per-foot price usually leave out entirely?

The extra face area from height and embedment, the drain-stone volume, every geogrid layer past the first, and the engineer stamp or permit fee. A published per-foot rate is set on the cheapest configuration — a short garden wall, segmental block, no geogrid, no permit — and has nowhere to put the hours and material for a taller wall, a heavier block type, added geogrid layers, or an engineer's stamp. A shop pricing off the rate alone is short before the crew unloads the truck.

How much do height, geogrid, and the engineer step actually add over a flat per-foot price?

On this calculator's own worked examples, at a $38 crew wage, 30% burden, 15% overhead, and a 22% target margin — the form's own defaults, not a number invented for this page — even the cheapest wall it prices, a 40 ft garden wall at 3 ft exposed with no permit, walks away at $5,473.16, already $2,873.16 over a $65-a-foot rate ($2,600.00). The same 40 ft length at 4 ft exposed with its first geogrid layer and a permit walks away at $7,855.92, over $5,255 over the same-length flat price. A 50 ft engineered wall at 6 ft exposed with an engineer stamp and permit walks away at $15,594.04, over $12,300 over its own $65-a-foot price of $3,250.00. Height, geogrid, and the engineer step — not the linear feet — are what move the number.

Is a flat per-foot price ever the right way to quote a retaining wall?

For the one wall it was actually priced on it can land close, but on this calculator's own defaults even that cheapest wall — a 40 ft garden wall at 3 ft exposed — already clears $65 a foot once real payroll burden, overhead, and a target margin are applied. A per-foot rate set on a short wall, or set without those loaded costs, keeps quoting a number the shop's own arithmetic no longer supports the moment height, geogrid, or the engineer step change.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local rates, permit fees, or what competitors charge. It takes the wall's length, exposed height, embedment, wall type, drain stone, geogrid layers, corners, the engineer/permit step, and your loaded labor and overhead, and turns them into a walk-away price for the wall you describe — the arithmetic a flat per-foot rate skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat per-foot price is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works until the wall stops matching the one the rate was set on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it.

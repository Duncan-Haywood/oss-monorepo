Bid the height and the drain, not the linear foot.

A flat per-foot quote treats a 40 ft, 3 ft garden wall as the same job as a 40 ft, 6 ft engineered wall. Face area, drain-stone volume, and geogrid layers grow with height.     Common questions

    Why does a per-linear-foot price for a retaining wall go wrong?

    Because almost nothing in a wall is priced by the foot. Face material is bought by the square foot of face, which is length × (exposed height + embedment). Drain stone is a volume behind the wall. Geogrid is square yards per layer, and taller walls need more layers. An engineer stamp and a permit arrive as a step, not a foot. A 40 ft wall at 3 ft and a 40 ft wall at 6 ft are the same linear feet and roughly double the face, more than double the stone, and two geogrid layers apart.

    Why is buried height counted when the customer only sees the exposed part?

    Because you still build it. Embedment — the course or so below grade that keeps the wall from kicking out — is block you buy, set, and backfill, and it is invisible in the height the homeowner quotes back at you. Face area here is length × (exposed + embedment) for exactly that reason. The cap, by contrast, really is per foot of length, so it is priced that way.

    How many geogrid layers does a wall need?

    The starting count here is: none under 4 ft exposed, one layer at 4 ft, and one more for every additional 2 ft. So a 6 ft wall starts at two layers and an 8 ft wall at three. That is a starting count for pricing, not a design — actual reinforcement depends on the soil, the surcharge, the block, and the manufacturer's tables, and above about 4 ft that is an engineer's determination, not a calculator's. Each layer costs square yards of grid across the wall length at the depth it runs back, plus the labor to place it.

    When does an engineer or a permit enter the price?

    Many US jurisdictions treat roughly 4 ft of exposed height as the line, which is why the options here are none, permit only (starting around $250), and engineer stamp plus permit (starting around $1,800). Those are starting figures to replace with your own, and the threshold is a common pattern rather than a rule — confirm it with your building department for the specific site before you quote. Getting this wrong is the single most expensive omission in a wall bid, because it is thousands of dollars that no per-foot number contains.

    How does the wall type change the labor?

    It multiplies face hours: segmental block is the 1.0 baseline, timber or sleeper 0.9, boulder or natural stone 1.3, and poured formed concrete 1.4. Height multiplies face hours again — 1.0 for a garden wall under 3 ft, 1.1 for 3–4 ft, 1.25 for 4–6 ft, and 1.45 above that — because taller walls mean more lifts, more compaction, and often a machine at the face. Excavation, drainage, and corners stay separate: the excavator shows up once whatever the wall's height, and the drain pipe still runs the length.

    Why isn't a 25% margin the same as adding 25% to my cost?

    Margin here is a share of the final price — price = job cost ÷ (1 − margin) — which is how the quote reads to the customer and how your books read it. Overhead is a separate markup on direct cost, applied before margin and never to itself or to profit. A $9,000 cost marked up 25% is $11,250, a 20% margin; a 25% target margin is $12,000.

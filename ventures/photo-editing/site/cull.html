How it works, and what it does not do

Every camera raw file carries a JPEG made by the camera at capture. On most bodies from the last decade it is full size (on the files we tested: every Canon CR3, the newer CR2 and NEF files, Pentax, Samsung and the Sony A7 IV; Sony before the A7 IV, Fujifilm and Panasonic store about 1,600 to 1,920 px). The page finds that JPEG by the file's own pointers (TIFF tags, CR3 boxes, the RAF header, the Olympus maker note), with a byte scan as the last resort, and reads the camera, lens, exposure and capture time from the file's EXIF.

Bursts are frames from the same body taken no more than 1.5 s apart. "Detail" is the variance of a Laplacian filter in the busiest of 16 areas of the preview, the same kind of measure culling apps use for sharpness. It compares frames of the same scene well; it cannot tell a soft subject in front of a sharp background, and it favours busy scenes over plain ones. Treat the ranking as a hint and check focus at 100% with Z.

Where the camera recorded where it focused, the page reads that too and draws it in the large view (in red the points the camera reports in focus): Canon (CR2 and CR3, including the 1,053-point grids of recent mirrorless bodies), Sony (bodies that write FocusLocation, such as the A7 III and A7 IV), Fujifilm, Panasonic, and Olympus and OM System. In a burst where every frame has an AF point, "sharpest" then means the most detail at that point, which is the subject the camera chose, so a soft subject in front of a busy background no longer wins; Z zooms to it. Nikon numbers its phase-detect points on a grid that differs by body and publishes no coordinates; the page places them from per-body point maps measured by the Focus-Points Lightroom plugin's contributors (Apache-2.0) with ExifTool's point numbering: the 51- and 39-point DSLRs (D3 to D4S, D300, D600/D610, D700, D750, D7000 to D7500, D800/D810, Df, D5200 to D5600), the 153-point D5, D500 and D850, the D6 and D780, and the Z bodies, which also write the AF area's position directly (on 28 public Z8 files, 27 now show a point, where 5 did before). Nikon's 11-point bodies (D90, D3000 to D3500, D5000, D5100) and bodies before 2007 have no map yet and fall back to the busiest area. On the public sample files, the point read agrees with metadata-extractor's own decoding on all 22 files it decodes, and sits on more detail than a random spot in 18 of 24 (median rank 0.89).

Closed eyes, if you tick "Check for closed eyes": the page downloads Google's MediaPipe Face Landmarker (Apache-2.0, a 3.6 MB model) once and runs it in the browser on a 1,024 px copy of each preview, after the card has loaded. A face counts as blinking when the model's blink score for the more-closed eye is over 0.5, the same rule as ShootFlow's command-line detector, so a frame gets one verdict whichever you use (on real shut eyes the model often reads one lid much lower than the other, so asking for both eyes missed a third of them). A deliberate wink counts as a blink too. Faces smaller than 1% of the frame (people in the background) are ignored. A face cut in half by the frame edge is not found by the face model, so after the first pass the page looks again along each edge: it reflects a strip 40% deep across the edge, so the half face and its reflection make a whole one, and keeps a face only if it sits on the mirror line (the same search as the command-line detector; about 0.14 s more per frame, and skipped on frames already flagged). Frames with a blink get an "eyes closed" tag and their own filter, the large view boxes each face (amber closed, green open), and the burst pick skips blinking frames unless every frame in the burst has one. How far to trust it: on 115 labelled real and generated photos the page flags all 7 with shut eyes and none of the other 108 (the seventh is a half face at the frame edge, found by the edge pass, which was designed on that photo); on 40 half faces cut from the set's own faces it finds 20 and flags 8 of the 10 shut ones, none of the 30 open ones, where the first pass alone found none. Half faces under about 100 px wide are mostly still missed. The widest open-eyed laugh scored 0.48, close to the line; seven closed photos are too few to give an error rate, and half-closed eyes, profiles, sunglasses and heavy motion blur are not covered. Treat the tag as a hint and look before you reject.

It shows the camera's JPEG, not a development of the raw data, so white balance and picture style are what the camera chose. For developing raw files with checked colour, see ShootFlow's command-line tools.

Measured on 65 public sample files from 55 camera models (the metadata-extractor-images set): a preview was found in 61, and all 61 decode at the size read; in Chromium the page read all 65 (1.1 GB) in about 5 seconds. The misses were two 2 KB BigTIFF test files (read since 2026-11-24), a phone DNG with no JPEG inside and a truncated 368-byte file; DNGs with no JPEG inside are now developed from their sensor data (below). Nikon Z 8 and Z 9 files shot in the HLG tone mode carry their previews as HEVC video frames, not JPEGs: the page decodes the 1620 × 1080 one with the browser's own HEVC decoder where there is one (Safari; Chrome and Edge on hardware that decodes HEVC) and otherwise shows the file's 160 × 120 thumbnail and says why. Either way it reads about 0.3 MB of each 50 MB file, not the whole file. Four older Olympus compacts (C-5050 Zoom, C-8080, SP-350, SP-500 UZ) store only a 160 × 120 thumbnail, so the page develops those from their sensor data too: a 1,288 to 1,640 px preview with the camera's white balance and a typical sensor's colour matrix (the files carry none), a median 1.0 to 3.2 ΔE2000 from LibRaw's development of the same files.

Questions

Are my photos uploaded?

No. The page reads the files with your browser's File API on your own computer. Nothing is sent anywhere; you can disconnect from the internet after the page loads and it still works.

Will Lightroom see my ratings?

Yes for raw files: put the downloaded .xmp files next to the raws before you import, and Lightroom Classic reads xmp:Rating (stars, −1 for rejected) and xmp:Label. Lightroom does not read sidecars for JPEG or DNG files, which keep metadata inside the file.

Will my Lightroom edits survive?

Yes, if you drop the existing .xmp sidecars along with the raw files. The page reads the rating and colour label in them as each frame's start, and when you download, the zip carries those same sidecar files back under their own names with only xmp:Rating, xmp:Label and xmp:MetadataDate changed; develop settings, keywords and crops stay as they were. Without them, a fresh sidecar would replace the one on disk.

Does it read ratings I set in the camera?

Yes, where the camera writes them into the raw file: the XMP packet many raws carry (in the public samples: recent Sony, Canon, Nikon, OM System, DJI and Pentax files), the XMP box of a Canon CR3, or the Rating tag OM System bodies write. A rating you set on this page before, or one in a dropped sidecar, comes first.

Does it show where the camera focused?

Yes for Canon, Nikon (most DSLRs since 2007 and the Z bodies), Sony (A7 III and later bodies that record it), Fujifilm, Panasonic, Olympus and OM System files: the AF points are drawn on the large view, Z zooms to 100% there, and the burst pick uses the detail at that point. Press F to hide them. Nikon files are placed from per-body point maps for the 51-, 39-, 105- and 153-point DSLRs and the Z bodies; Nikon's 11-point bodies, Nikons before 2007 and older Sony bodies do not record a usable position, so the page uses the busiest area instead.

Does it find closed eyes?

Yes, if you tick "Check for closed eyes" above the grid. The face model is downloaded once (3.6 MB) and runs on your computer; your photos are still not uploaded. Blinking frames get an "eyes closed" tag, Show › Eyes closed lists them, and the sharpest-of-burst pick prefers a frame where everyone's eyes are open. It is a hint, measured on few closed faces so far: check before rejecting.

My phone's DNG files have no preview. Can it show them?

Yes. Many phone DNGs (Nexus 5, 5X, 6 and 6P, the first Pixels, and Google's HDR+ bursts) carry no JPEG at all, so the page develops a preview from the sensor data itself, in a background worker: one pixel for each 2 × 2 block of the colour filter (half the sensor's width), with the white balance, colour matrix and lens-shading map the phone wrote into the file, and up to two stops of brightening so the bright end of the frame is near white. The large view says "developed from the raw data". It takes about half a second to a second a frame. It is a preview for culling, not an edit: no lens distortion correction, sharpening or highlight recovery. Checked on 32 public DNGs: the sensor values read are identical to ShootFlow's own raw reader on all 32, and the colours are a median 0.4 ΔE (CIELAB) from ShootFlow's full development of the same files.

Why does a preview look different from my raw editor?

It is the JPEG the camera made at capture, with the camera's picture style, not a development of the raw data. That is what makes culling fast, and it is what the camera's own screen showed you.

Can it add captions, keywords and my copyright?

Yes. Open "Captions, keywords and copyright (IPTC)" under the download button and fill in what every frame should carry: creator, copyright notice, credit, headline, title, caption, keywords and location. Variables such as {date}, {camera} and {stem} are filled per frame, so "© {year} Jane Doe" becomes the year each frame was shot. Press K on a frame to add keywords to that frame alone. The fields go into the sidecars as the standard IPTC XMP properties (dc:description, dc:subject, dc:rights, photoshop:City and so on); in your own sidecars, empty boxes leave existing captions alone and keywords are added to the ones already there. The template is kept in this browser and can be saved as a file or loaded from a captioned .xmp. ExifTool reads every field back from the downloaded sidecars.

Does it remember my ratings?

In this browser only: ratings are kept in its local storage, keyed by file name and size, so reopening the same files brings them back. Download the sidecars to keep them anywhere else.

Privacy and usage

Your files never leave your computer. The page keeps a few counters in this browser's local storage (files opened, downloads made) so the project can tell whether anyone uses it; they contain no file names or photos and are never sent anywhere by this page. If you tick "Check for closed eyes", the browser fetches the face library from cdn.jsdelivr.net and its model from storage.googleapis.com (those hosts see an ordinary download request, as for any web page); the photos are checked locally and are not sent.

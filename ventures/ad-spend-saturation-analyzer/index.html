How it is calculated

Curves: for each channel two models are fitted by least squares to the periods you supply: a straight line (revenue = base + slope x spend) and a saturating curve (revenue = base + a x (1 - exp(-spend / k))), where k is found by grid search. The saturating curve is kept only if its 10-fold cross-validated error beats the straight line by at least 2%; otherwise the channel is reported as showing no clear saturation in the range you tested. Marginal ROAS: the slope of the curve at your current spend. Ranges: the curve is refitted on 200 resamples of your periods (100 above 500 periods) and the 10th and 90th percentiles of the marginal ROAS are shown; a channel is "scale up" if at least 80% of resamples are above break-even and "pull back" if at most 20% are. Break-even: marginal ROAS of 1 divided by your gross margin. Budget split: the budget is handed out in small steps, each to the channel whose next step returns most, stopping when the best next step is below break-even (so the tool can leave money unspent), within the per-channel limits above. For concave curves this equalises marginal ROAS across channels. Uplift: margin x the change in predicted revenue, minus the change in spend, with the share of resamples in which it is positive.

Not modelled: carry-over and lag (an ad today sells next week), seasonality and promotions that raise revenue and spend together, overlap between channels, creative changes, and bids or auctions that reprice as you scale. The history is observational, not an experiment, so the curve is a hypothesis to test with a holdout, not a causal estimate. Bootstrap ranges reflect sampling noise given the chosen curve type, not the choice of curve. Unverified: checked on simulated channels with known curves (the 80% range covered the true marginal ROAS in 47 of 60 simulated datasets), not against a published dataset or a commercial media-mix tool.

Common questions

What is marginal ROAS and why does it matter more than average ROAS?

Average ROAS is total revenue divided by total spend. Marginal ROAS is what the next dollar returns. Because ad channels saturate, the marginal number is always lower than the average, and it is the one that tells you whether to spend more. A channel can show a 4x average ROAS and still return only 0.8x on the next dollar, in which case more budget loses money.

How does the tool find the curve?

For each channel it fits two shapes to your spend and revenue rows: a straight line, and a saturating curve where revenue rises quickly at low spend and flattens at high spend. It keeps the saturating one only if it predicts held-out rows better than the line in 10-fold cross-validation. The fit is least squares with a grid search over the saturation speed, so there is no black box and the result is the same every time you run it.

How many rows do I need?

At least 20 periods per channel, and more is better. Just as important, spend has to vary: if you spent the same amount every day, there is nothing to learn a curve from, and the tool says so and holds that channel at its current level. A few weeks of deliberately varied budgets teach more than a year of a flat one.

How does it decide to scale up or pull back?

It refits the curve on 200 bootstrap resamples of your rows and looks at the marginal ROAS at your current spend in each. If at least 80% of them are above your break-even, it says scale up; if at most 20% are, pull back; otherwise unclear. The 10th to 90th percentile range is shown next to the estimate so you can see how well the data pins it down.

What break-even does it use?

Break-even marginal ROAS is 1 divided by your gross margin. At a 50% margin the next dollar of ad spend has to bring back 2 dollars of revenue just to cover itself, so a channel with a 1.6x marginal ROAS is losing money at the margin even though its average ROAS looks healthy. Leave the margin empty to use revenue only (break-even 1.0x).

Can I trust the result as cause and effect?

Only partly. This reads your history, it does not run an experiment. If you raise spend in December because sales are high in December, the curve will credit the ads for the season. Spend that moves together across channels cannot be separated, which the tool warns about. Use the output to decide where to run a holdout or geo test, and move budget in steps, not all at once.

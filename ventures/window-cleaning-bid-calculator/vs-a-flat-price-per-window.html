This calculator vs. a flat price per window

A price per window is the number homeowners and storefront managers ask for — $5 a window, $6 a pane, "we beat any quote." That rate cannot see glass type or the drive, and the same rate that clears a profit on a ground-floor house loses money outright on French panes or storefront plate.

What a flat price per window is

It is the fastest number to quote over the phone: one rate, times the window count, whatever the glass turns out to be when the cleaner arrives. It is learned on a stop that was easy to price — usually ground-floor exterior panes on a water-fed pole — and then quoted again on the next job that calls.

Nothing about "how many windows" tells the operator whether those windows are ground-floor plates or French panes cleaned both sides, or how far the van has to drive to reach them. The flat rate does not ask, because it was never built to answer.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter one row per glass type — pane count, minutes per pane, and sides — plus travel minutes and your own wage, payroll burden, overhead, and target margin. It prices the visit from what this specific glass and this specific drive actually cost, not a rate learned on the last stop.

When to still just quote a flat rate

If a route only ever sees one kind of glass — a subdivision of similar ground-floor houses, say — a flat per-window number can land close to cost, because the glass mix genuinely does not vary from stop to stop. The risk is the call that is not like the others: French panes, a second-story or lift job, or a storefront with plate glass, quoted at the same rate because the phone call never asked what kind of windows they were.

Common questions

Why does a flat price per window get a bid wrong?

Because the rate is learned on one kind of glass -- usually easy ground-floor exterior panes -- and then quoted on the next job sight unseen, whatever the glass turns out to be. This calculator's own arithmetic prices 50 ground-floor exterior panes at a real per-window rate of $1.71 once travel and margin are counted. Quote that same $1.71 a window on 50 French and storefront panes and the total comes to $85.65 against a job that actually costs $224.25 to run -- a $138.60 loss, not a thin margin, on a job this calculator prices at $280.31.

What does a flat per-window rate usually miss entirely?

Glass type and sides (a French or divided-light pane cleaned both sides can take five to six times as long as a ground-floor exterior plate on a water-fed pole), and travel and setup, which do not shrink because the stop is small. A flat rate has no line for either -- it is one number learned on one kind of stop and reused for the next job that calls, regardless of the glass or the drive.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

Using this calculator's own cost inputs -- $20 hourly wage, 25% burden, $8 supplies and $12 other cost per visit, 15% overhead, 20% target margin: 50 ground-floor exterior panes with a 20-minute drive cost $68.52 to run and price at $85.65 with no minimum charge applied. 50 panes split between French (both sides) and storefront plate glass, same 20-minute drive, cost $224.25 to run and price at $280.31. Quoting the second job at the first job's per-window rate ($1.71) comes to $85.65 -- $138.60 under the actual cost, not just under the target price.

Is a flat per-window rate ever a reasonable shorthand?

For stops that stay to one glass type -- a whole house of ground-floor exterior, say -- a flat number can land close to cost. The risk is quoting French panes, second-story or lift access, or storefront plate glass at that same rate over the phone, because the advertised rate does not know the glass mix until the cleaner is already looking at the windows.

Does this calculator know my local labor rates or glass pricing?

No. It has no data on your local labor market or what a competitor charges. It takes the pane count, minutes per pane, and sides for each glass type on the stop, plus your own wage, payroll burden, travel minutes, supplies, overhead, and target margin, and turns that into a price for this visit -- the arithmetic a flat per-window rate skips, not a market survey.

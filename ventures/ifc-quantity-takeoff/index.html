IFC Quantity Takeoff

Drop an IFC model and get a bill of quantities: counts, lengths, areas, volumes and weights by class, type, storey, material, classification code or Uniformat II element. Every volume and area is also recomputed from the model's own geometry, openings taken out, and compared with what the authoring tool stored, so you know which numbers to trust before you price them. Got a new issue of the model? Drop the old one too and see what changed, line by line and element by element.

A blank cell takes the thresholds above. Walls are every IfcWall; slabs and roofs IfcSlab and IfcRoof; coverings IfcCovering (plaster, tiling, ceilings, flooring); frames IfcBeam, IfcColumn and IfcMember.

Methods of measurement often leave small voids in: a pipe sleeve is not deducted from a wall's area or a slab's concrete. Set the thresholds your contract's method names; each opening is measured on its own (its area in the wall's middle plane or the slab's plan, and the volume it takes out), and the presets are starting points, not a statement of any standard.

Volume uses the model's stored NetVolume (or GrossVolume) unless that figure is known to be wrong, then the volume computed from the geometry. Area does the same with the stored net area, and where a wall or slab stores none, uses the area cut from its geometry. Assemblies whose parts are elements themselves (a stair and its flights) are counted through their parts only. Type a rate on any line to price it.

Stored quantities against the geometry

For each element that stores a GrossVolume or NetVolume, the volume computed from its body; for each wall, slab, covering or member that stores a side, footprint, plan or cross-section area, the area cut from its body by a plane. Agreement is within 2%.

Compare with an earlier revision

Drop the earlier issue of the same model to see what changed since: the bill-of-quantities lines that moved (priced with the rates above), and every element added, removed, changed, moved or resized. Elements are matched by GlobalId, then by Tag (Revit's element id survives a re-export that renews GlobalIds), then by the same place and volume; Revit parts exported in one issue and their host in the other are paired up rather than counted as removed and added.

Who it is for

Estimators and quantity surveyors handed an IFC instead of drawings; contractors pricing a design-build package from the architect's model; BIM coordinators asked "how many cubic metres of concrete is in this?"; owners checking a consultant's quantities. If your firm works inside Revit, its schedules know the native model better; this is for everyone downstream who only gets the IFC.

A worked example

The sample model is a small two-storey office written by hand so every number can be checked: a 5,000 mm wall with a door opening (3.0 m³ gross, 2.58 m³ net), a wall clipped by a sloped roof plane (2.0 m³), a wall split by level whose two pieces each store the whole wall's 3.6 m³, a 5.0 m³ slab, a round column with no stored quantities (0.212 m³ computed) and an IPE 200 beam whose stored volume is wrong. The bill of quantities sums the walls to 8.18 m³; adding the stored figures blindly would give 11.78 m³, 44% too much. The split wall's pieces also store the whole wall's 18 m² side area each; the page uses the 9 m² each piece's geometry gives, and the 10 m² of the sloped wall, which stores no area at all.

What real models get wrong

We ran the takeoff on 8 public models from Revit, ArchiCAD, Tekla and other tools that store volumes. The stored GrossVolume matched the geometry for 4,863 of 5,495 elements (88%) and NetVolume for 6,172 of 6,405 (96%). Of the 632 GrossVolume disagreements, the page names a cause for 620: 327 GrossVolumes that already exclude openings, 119 slab volumes written in the wrong unit (96 of them 1,000 times too large, 20 in litres divided by the square-foot factor, 92.9 times too large), 105 walls and slabs whose geometry already has the openings cut out (it equals their stored NetVolume, and the stored GrossVolume is larger by the openings), 27 split-by-level pieces each carrying the whole wall's figure,, 20 stored volumes that contradict the element's own length, height and width, and 22 Tekla steel parts whose stored volume comes from a simplified section. Of the 233 NetVolume disagreements, it names a cause for 227: 120 Tekla steel parts stored from the section without its root fillets or with round tubes as 8- or 16-sided polygons (2 to 11% under the profile's real area; their stored weights are scaled to the geometry), 77 ArchiCAD windows and doors that store the frame and sash without their glass panes (the body less its panes, to 0.02%), 23 stair stringers each carrying the whole stair's volume, and 7 skylights and fittings that store 0. Where the stored figure is the wrong one, the bill of quantities uses the geometry. The method and every number are in the repository (eval/stored-vs-computed-2026-11-21.md, eval/netvolume-causes-2026-11-21.md, eval/glazing-left-out-2026-11-21.md).

And the areas

Areas are cut from the same geometry by a plane, the way the quantity sets define them: a wall's side area on its middle plane, its footprint at its base, a slab's area as its extruded profile, a member's cross-section normal to its length. On the same 8 models, 6,509 of 7,883 stored areas (83%) matched the geometry, every one of 1,667 wall footprints among them. Of the 1,374 that did not, the page names a cause for 1,100: 558 "cross-section areas" on curtain-wall mullions that grow with the mullion's length (a face area, not a section), 325 gross side areas with the openings already out, 82 net footprints with the doors left in, 55 side areas measured on a wall face instead of its middle, 31 that agree with an equally wrong stored volume, 25 written in the wrong unit (some divided by the square-foot factor 10.764) and 24 split-by-level pieces. Method and numbers: eval/areas-vs-stored-2026-11-19.md.

Comparing revisions

An architect's practice published five IFC exports of one house, made over five days in November 2019, in its public repository (OpeningDesign's Aalseth Residence, CC BY-SA). Between consecutive exports, the same four doors, one window and one wall came out with new GlobalIds every time, and the floors, roofs and some walls switched between one element and its Revit "parts" (two to five layers whose volumes sum to the whole) in three of the four steps. A comparison keyed on GlobalId alone listed 190 element records as added or removed that were the same building element; this page matches them, and the stair rebuilt in the last export shows up as its flights, landing and stringers resized. Method and numbers: eval/revision-diff-2026-11-20.md.

Questions

What does it take off?

Every physical element in the model (walls, slabs, beams, columns, doors, windows, coverings, stairs, pipes, furniture and the rest): its count, and the lengths, areas, volumes and weights the authoring tool stored in the model's quantity sets, converted to metres, square metres, cubic metres and kilograms. It also computes each element's volume from the model's own geometry, with openings taken out, and the side and footprint areas of walls, the areas of slabs and coverings and the cross-sections of members. Lines can be grouped by class, type, storey, material and classification code.

Why recompute volumes the model already stores?

Because the stored figures are sometimes wrong, and an estimate built on them is wrong too. On 8 public models the stored GrossVolume agreed with the geometry for 4,863 of 5,495 elements. Most of the rest have a known cause the page names: a wall split by level that writes the whole wall's volume on every piece, a GrossVolume that already has the openings taken out, geometry exported with its openings already cut, a volume written in litres under a cubic-metre unit, or a stored volume that disagrees with the element's own stored length, height and width. Where the stored figure is known to be wrong, the bill of quantities uses the computed one.

Is my model uploaded?

No. The model is read by JavaScript in this browser tab and nothing is sent anywhere. The page fetches only its own sample file, and only when you ask for it. The same code runs offline on the command line.

Which geometry can it measure?

Extruded profiles (rectangles, circles, hollow sections, I, L, T, U, C and Z steel shapes with root radii, and arbitrary outlines with arcs), half-space clips including Revit's boxed and polygonal ones, boolean differences, unions and intersections of those, mapped items, faceted breps, triangulated and polygonal face sets, swept disks, revolved areas and CSG primitives. Advanced breps are read too: faces on planes and cylinders exactly, and faces on surfaces of revolution, surfaces of linear extrusion and B-spline (NURBS) surfaces cut into triangles that keep within 0.02% of the face's size of the true surface. Openings are subtracted exactly, from faceted bodies as well when their shells are closed. A face that wraps all the way round a closed surface, a curved face with a hole in it, and open shells are reported as not read rather than guessed.

How is this different from Revit schedules, Solibri or CostX?

Revit schedules work inside Revit on the native model; this works on the IFC file anyone on the project can open. Solibri, CostX and similar estimating tools do far more (markups, 2D takeoff, rate libraries) and cost a seat licence. This page is free, needs nothing installed, and does one thing they do not advertise: it checks the model's stored quantities against its geometry and tells you which ones not to trust. It also compares two revisions of a model, as CostX and Cubit do, and says which elements only look new because the export gave them a new GlobalId.

Can I price the quantities?

Yes. Type a rate and choose its unit (each, m3, m2, m or kg) on any bill-of-quantities line; the page multiplies it out and totals the amounts, and the CSV download carries the rates and amounts. The rates are yours: the page ships none.

Can it compare two revisions of a model?

Yes. Take off the later revision, then drop the earlier one under Compare with an earlier revision. You get the change on every bill-of-quantities line (priced with your rates, for a change order) and a list of every element added, removed, changed, moved or resized, both as CSV. Elements are matched by GlobalId, then by Revit's element id in the Tag when an export renewed the GlobalId, then by the same place and volume; Revit parts exported in one issue and their host in the other are paired up. On five real exports of one house, a GlobalId-only comparison reported 202 elements as added or removed that were the same element under a new id, or its parts.

Can it leave small openings in, as a method of measurement does?

Yes. Under the bill of quantities, choose how openings are treated: every opening deducted, as the model has it; voids up to 0.50 m² of area and 0.05 m³ of volume left in; walls and finishes judged by area and slabs and frames by volume (the by-work-section preset); or your own thresholds, one pair for everything or a pair for each work section (walls, slabs and roofs, coverings, beams and columns). Each opening is measured on its own, by its area in the wall's middle plane or the slab's plan and the volume it takes out of the element, and the bill of quantities and its CSV say how many voids were left in and what they added. The thresholds are yours to check against the method of measurement your contract names; the page does not claim any standard's rules. On 12 public models, 1,099 of 1,136 wall openings whose own quantity set stores a width and height measured within 2% of width times height (most of the rest are round windows, whose true area is smaller).

Can it arrange the bill of quantities as an elemental estimate?

Yes. Group by Uniformat II element and every line is a building element (A1010 Standard Foundations, B2010 Exterior Walls, C1010 Partitions, D2010 Plumbing Fixtures and so on), the codes and titles of NIST's public-domain UNIFORMAT II. Where the model carries a Uniformat code (Revit writes its Assembly Code as one) that code is used; otherwise the element's class is read with its PredefinedType, IsExternal flag, the wall a door fills, the assembly it belongs to and its storey's elevation, and each element's CSV row says which. A wall with no IsExternal flag is placed by its geometry: rays cast from each face either cross another wall or leave the building, so it lands in exterior walls or partitions. Sloped roofs stop the rays too, so a knee wall in an attic reads as a partition, while a dormer wall that rises through the roof stays exterior. Where a model's flag and the geometry disagree, the flag is kept and the row says to check it, unless the model flags every wall the same way. Beams, columns and joists are placed by what they carry, read from the geometry: the slab or roof directly over them puts them in floor construction, roof construction or, under a slab on grade, foundations. Where nothing can say more, the line stops at the level it can tell (B10 Superstructure for a beam with no slab or roof over it) or says no element was assigned, rather than guessing. On 17 public models, 588 elements carry their author's own Uniformat code; the rules put 525 of them in the same major group and 482 in the same element. On the model held out while the rules were written, 66 of 69 matched.

What does it not do?

It does not read a curved face that wraps all the way round its surface or has a hole in it, and a faceted body clipped by a plane keeps the model's stored areas. It applies only one kind of measurement rule, leaving in openings up to a size you set (per work section if you like); other rules of a method of measurement (girthed or extra-over items, deductions for built-in members, rounding) are yours to apply. It is not a cost estimate: quantities are only as good as the model.

Command line

The same engine runs in Node 20+ with no dependencies: node cli.mjs model.ifc --by storey,class > boq.csv, --rows elements.csv for every element, --check for the stored-against-computed summary.

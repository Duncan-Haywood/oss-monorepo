Should I take the Section 174A catch-up in one year or two?

The election is a real financial tradeoff, not a formality -- and the answer depends far more on your income in each year than most summaries suggest. Three worked examples below, all pinned to this calculator's own arithmetic, on the same $200,000 remaining unamortized balance.

Worked example 1: equal rates both years -- a small edge to the 1-year path

$200,000 remaining balance, $500,000 of taxable income in both 2025 and 2026, a flat 21% rate both years, 6% annual discount rate.

Line1-year path2-year path
2025 deduction claimed$200,000$100,000
2026 deduction claimed$0$100,000
2025 cash savings$42,000.00$21,000.00
2026 cash savings$0.00$21,000.00
Nominal total$42,000.00$42,000.00
Present value at 6%/year$42,000.00$40,811.32

Same $42,000 nominal total either way -- the 1-year path only wins because that money arrives entirely in 2025 instead of split across two years. Present-value edge: $1,188.68 (2.83%) in the 1-year path's favor, from time value of money alone.

Worked example 2: a weak 2025 changes everything

Same $200,000 balance and the same 21% rate both years, but 2025 taxable income before this deduction is only $20,000 -- a near-breakeven year -- while 2026 is back to $500,000.

Line1-year path2-year path
2025 deduction claimed / usable$200,000 / $20,000$100,000 / $20,000
2025 excess (creates/increases an NOL)$180,000$80,000
2025 cash savings$4,200.00$4,200.00
2026 deduction claimed / usable$0 / $0$100,000 / $100,000
2026 cash savings$0.00$21,000.00
Nominal total$4,200.00$25,200.00
Present value at 6%/year$4,200.00$24,011.32

The 1-year path can only use $20,000 of its $200,000 deduction against 2025's thin income -- the other $180,000 does not vanish, but it becomes an NOL question this calculator does not model, instead of a cash saving today. The 2-year path wastes less into that same NOL gap in 2025 (only $80,000 excess against its smaller $100,000 claim) and puts its full second half to work against 2026's healthy income. Present-value gap: $19,811.32 -- nearly five times the 1-year path's total -- in the 2-year path's favor.

Worked example 3: an expected 2026 rate increase flips example 1

Same $200,000 balance, same $500,000 of income both years as example 1 -- but the business expects its marginal rate to rise from 21% in 2025 to 30% in 2026.

Line1-year path2-year path
Present value at 6%/year$42,000.00$49,301.89

Nothing about the income or the balance changed from example 1 -- only the expected 2026 rate did. That alone is enough to flip the present-value edge from +$1,188.68 for the 1-year path to +$7,301.89 for the 2-year path, because each dollar deducted in 2026 is now worth more per dollar than one deducted in 2025.

Common questions

If my tax rate will be the same both years, does it matter which path I pick?

A little, in the 1-year path's favor. On a $200,000 balance at a flat 21% rate both years and $500,000 of income each year, the 1-year path's $42,000 in savings arrives entirely in 2025, while the 2-year path's identical $42,000 nominal total splits $21,000/$21,000 across 2025 and 2026. Discounted at 6%/year, getting money sooner is worth about $1,188.68 more today -- a real but modest edge from time value of money alone.

What if 2025 is a weak year for my business?

This is where the two paths can diverge sharply. On the same $200,000 balance, if 2025 taxable income before this deduction is only $20,000, the 1-year path can only use $20,000 of the $200,000 deduction that year -- the remaining $180,000 does not vanish, but it creates or increases a net operating loss instead of an immediate cash saving, worth just $4,200 up front. The 2-year path claims $100,000 in 2025 (also capped at the $20,000 usable that year, so it wastes less into NOL territory on that half) and the other $100,000 in 2026 against a healthy $500,000 of income, capturing a full $21,000 there. Total nominal savings: $4,200 for the 1-year path versus $25,200 for the 2-year path -- a present-value gap of about $19,811.32 in the 2-year path's favor.

What if I expect my tax rate to go up in 2026?

An expected rate increase independently favors the 2-year path, because the half deducted in 2026 is then worth more per dollar. On the same $200,000 balance at $500,000 of income both years, moving the 2026 rate from 21% to 30% flips the present-value edge from the 1-year path (+$1,188.68) to the 2-year path (+$7,301.89) -- a rate change alone is enough to change which election is better, even though nothing about the income or the balance changed.

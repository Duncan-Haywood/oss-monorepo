This calculator vs. a flat seasonal rate

A flat $250-400 seasonal quote, borrowed from what the driveway next door paid or from last year's invoice, has no storm count behind it -- so nobody knows how many events it survives until a heavy winter or an unlimited-ice clause finds out for them. This tool builds the price from stop minutes, salt, loaded hourly, and expected storms, and hands back the exact storm count where that price stops making money.

What each approach actually does

A flat seasonal rate is usually set by asking what a similar driveway paid last year, or what a competitor quotes, and locking that number in for the winter. It carries no plow minutes, no sidewalk minutes, no salt bags, and no ice-event count behind it -- so the number is a guess dressed as a quote. It survives a quiet eight-storm season by luck and loses on a fourteen-storm season or an "unlimited ice" clause for the same reason: nothing about the number ever accounted for storm count in the first place.

This tool starts from each stop's plow minutes, sidewalk minutes, and salt bags, adds deadhead, and prices a loaded hourly rate from operator wage, payroll burden, and truck cost. Ice is priced as its own event rather than folded into a plow visit, because most winters bring more ice visits than snow visits. From there it reports the per-push price, the seasonal price, the walk-away price below which the winter loses money, and -- the number a flat rate never produces -- how many snow events that seasonal price survives once ice is already paid.

Common questions

What is actually wrong with a flat seasonal rate?

Not the number itself -- the fact that nothing produced it. A flat $250-400 seasonal, copied from what the driveway next door paid or from last year's invoice, carries no record of plow minutes, sidewalk minutes, salt bags, deadhead, or how many storms and ice events it was meant to cover. So there is no way to tell, before the season starts, how many storms that price survives -- only after a quiet eight-storm winter turns a profit or a fourteen-storm winter and an unlimited-ice clause turn a loss.

How does this calculator arrive at a different number?

It starts from each stop's plow minutes, sidewalk minutes, and salt bags, adds deadhead, and prices a loaded hourly rate from operator wage, payroll burden, and truck cost. Ice is priced as its own event rather than folded into a plow visit, since most winters have more ice visits than snow visits. The seasonal price then comes from snow-event cost plus ice-event cost, divided so the target margin holds -- and the calculator also reports the exact storm count that seasonal price survives once ice is already paid.

Does a flat seasonal rate ever make sense?

Yes, once it has been checked. A round number is easier to say on the phone than a formula, and nothing here argues against quoting one. What a flat rate should not be is guessed -- this calculator's job is to tell you whether the round number you want to quote clears the walk-away price and how many storms it survives, so the flat rate you give a customer is a checked number instead of a borrowed one.

Is my route uploaded anywhere?

No. Every stop, minute, salt bag, and price stays in your browser. The optional usage counter records only which features were used and the day -- never a route, a wage, a cost, or a price -- and it is opt-out from the footer.

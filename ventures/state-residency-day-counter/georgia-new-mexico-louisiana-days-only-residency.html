Georgia, New Mexico and Louisiana: residency by days alone

Most day-count tests apply only if you also keep a place of abode in the state. Georgia (O.C.G.A. 48-7-1), New Mexico (NMSA 7-2-2) and Louisiana (La. R.S. 47:31) do not: presence alone is enough. A person with no home there, only a rented room or a long stay with family, can still cross the line. The safe maximums are 182 days in Georgia, 184 in New Mexico and 182 in Louisiana.

Questions

Do I need a home in Georgia to be a Georgia resident by days?

No. The commonly published Georgia test makes you a resident for tax if you spend 183 days or more, counting part-days, in the tax year, and no place of abode is needed. So the most you can spend is 182 days. Georgia's statute looks at a trailing 365-day window ending on the last day of the tax year, so check how days from the prior year are treated; the counter counts the calendar year only. Domicile is a separate test. Check the Department of Revenue's own guidance.

How many days can I spend in New Mexico?

New Mexico treats you as a resident for tax if you are physically present for 185 days or more of the tax year, so the most you can spend is 184 days. No place of abode is needed, and domicile can make you a resident at any day count. Keep dated travel records.

Why does Louisiana count months instead of days?

The statute says more than six months in the aggregate. The counter treats 183 days as crossing it, so its limit is 182. Confirm the Department of Revenue's counting method if your year is close.

Why does a missing home not protect me in these states?

In most day-count states the test only applies if you also keep a place of abode there, so a person with no home in the state is outside it. Georgia, New Mexico and Louisiana have a presence-only test, so a rented room, a hotel and a friend's couch all count towards the total.

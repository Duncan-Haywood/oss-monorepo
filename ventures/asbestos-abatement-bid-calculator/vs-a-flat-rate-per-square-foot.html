This calculator vs. a flat rate per square foot

"$8.50 a square foot" is not a price for an abatement job — it is a price for one specific containment class, area count, and air monitoring load, quietly applied to every job that calls in.

What a flat rate per square foot is

A flat rate per square foot is the fastest way to quote: multiply the job's area by a number and read off a price. Customers shop it because it is easy to compare between bids, and it is the number a customer holding a competitor's quote will ask you to match.

The number behind it was set for one job — one containment class, one containment area, one air monitoring load, no rush surcharge — and then reused for every job afterward, whether or not the next job looks anything like the one the rate was built on.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the OSHA/EPA containment class (Class I friable, Class II non-friable, Class III minor/incidental), square footage, number of containment areas, crew and supervisor rates, air monitoring samples, waste disposal volume, and permit fee. It prices containment build/teardown and production rate per class separately, adds air monitoring and disposal by volume, and returns a price above your target margin — for this job, not a generic one.

What a flat rate per square foot doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same $/sq ft whether the work is a Class III glovebag patch or a Class I full-containment removal — roughly six times the production time per square foot apart. Prices production rate and containment build/teardown cost per containment class, so Class I, II, and III are never priced off the same number.
Prices "the job" as one area figure, with no separate line for how many containment areas the work is actually split across. Charges a containment setup cost per area, so a job split across three containment areas prices three builds and teardowns, not one.
Has no line for air monitoring samples or waste disposal volume, regardless of how large or friable the job is. Prices air monitoring samples and disposal volume directly, so a job needing 14 samples and 9 cubic yards of waste costs more than one needing 1 sample and a quarter yard.
A 100 sq ft Class III glovebag patch and a 1,200 sq ft multi-area Class I pipe-insulation job get quoted off the same $/sq ft number. The small Class III job and the large multi-area Class I job price on their own containment class, areas, and monitoring load — not on square footage, so the two jobs come out differently priced.
Gives the estimator a number to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the price and the dollar/percent gap against what a flat rate would have quoted the same job, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before the contractor commits to it.

A flat rate is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is applied outside the one job it was built for, and nothing about a per-square-foot number tells you when that has happened. Common questions

Why does a flat rate per square foot get an abatement quote wrong?

Because the cost driver is containment class, not area. A Class I friable job needs full containment, a negative-air machine, a three-stage decon chamber, and crews working in full-face respirators doing wet removal — roughly six times slower per square foot than a Class III glovebag job on non-friable material. A flat rate charges both the same per foot.

What does a flat rate usually leave out entirely?

Containment areas and air monitoring. A job split across three separate containment areas needs three containment builds and teardowns, not one, and a larger or friable job needs more air monitoring samples to clear. A per-square-foot number has no line for either.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

In the two worked examples on this page, a flat $8.50/sq ft rate overcharges a small, minimal Class III job by about 24% and undercharges a large, multi-area Class I job by about 30% — using this calculator's own verified defaults, not a cherry-picked case.

Is a flat rate per square foot ever the right way to quote?

For a narrow band of near-identical jobs — same containment class, one containment area, similar air monitoring load — a flat rate can land close. The risk is applying it outside that band without noticing, because the rate does not know which class a given job falls into and neither does the estimator until the scope is actually reviewed.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local rates or what competitors charge. It takes the crew and supervisor rates, air monitoring cost, and disposal fee you enter and turns them into a price for the containment class, area count, and volume you describe — the arithmetic a flat rate skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat rate per square foot is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works until the job stops matching the one the rate was set on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it.

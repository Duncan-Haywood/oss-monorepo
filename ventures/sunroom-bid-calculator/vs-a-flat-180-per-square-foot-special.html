This calculator vs. a flat $180/sf special

“$180 a square foot” is not a price for a sunroom — it is a price for one specific enclosure, foundation, roof, and HVAC scope, quietly applied to every room that calls in.

What a flat $180/sf special is

A flat rate per square foot is the fastest way to quote: multiply the room's footprint by a number and read off a price. Homeowners shop it because it is easy to compare between bids, and it is the number a homeowner holding a competitor's flyer will ask you to match.

The number behind it was set for one room — a three-season enclosure on an existing deck, with the existing roof left alone, no HVAC, no electrical — and then reused for every room afterward, whether or not the next one looks anything like the one the rate was built on.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the attachment style, enclosure (three-season or four-season), foundation (existing deck, existing slab, or new slab), roof (existing or new insulated), wall height, dimensions, windows, and sliders, plus whether the room needs HVAC, electrical, or a permit, and your own labor hours and material costs. It prices the glass, floor, roof, and mechanical scope separately by enclosure and foundation type, then returns the walk-away price and a bid price above your target margin — for this room, not a generic one.

What a flat $180/sf special doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same $/sf whether the enclosure is three-season vinyl or four-season insulated glass — even though four-season glass alone triples both the install hours per 100 sf and the material cost per foot. Prices glass hours and material by enclosure kit, so a four-season room is never quoted off a three-season rate.
Has no line for foundation work, regardless of whether the room lands on an existing deck overlay or needs a full new insulated slab poured. Prices floor hours and material by foundation kit — existing deck, existing slab, or new slab — so a new pour costs more than a finish pass on what is already there.
Has no line for an insulated roof, regardless of whether the existing roof is reused or a new one has to go on. Prices roof hours and material only when a new insulated roof is scoped, so leaving the existing roof alone never gets charged for one.
Has no line for HVAC, electrical, or a permit, regardless of how many the room needs. Prices HVAC hours and a mini-split fee, electrical hours and package cost, and a permit fee separately when scoped, so a fully mechanical room costs more than a shell.
Gives the estimator a number to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the walk-away price and the dollar gap against what a flat rate would have quoted the same room, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before you commit to it.

A flat rate is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is applied outside the one room it was built for, and nothing about a per-square-foot number tells you when that has happened. This calculator does not know your local market; it replaces the arithmetic a flat rate skips.

Common questions

Why does a flat $180/sf special get a sunroom quote wrong?

Because the cost driver is enclosure type, foundation, roof, and HVAC, not floor area. A four-season room needs insulated glass, a new insulated slab instead of an existing deck overlay, an insulated roof instead of the existing one, and a mini-split -- none of which scale with the footprint the way a per-square-foot rate assumes. A flat $180/sf special charges all of that the same as the three-season room it was priced from.

What does a flat $180/sf special usually leave out entirely?

Foundation work, an insulated roof, and HVAC or electrical. A room built on an existing deck with the existing roof left alone is doing three fewer things than one with a new insulated slab, a new insulated roof, and a mini-split and electrical package added -- and a per-square-foot number has no line for any of them.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

On this calculator's own shipped presets: a 120 sq ft three-season room on an existing deck and roof costs $11,395.50 to build. The flat $180/sf special charges $21,600.00 for that room -- $10,204.50 over cost, and even $6,406.00 above this calculator's own 25%-margin bid price. Applied to a 192 sq ft four-season room with a new slab, an insulated roof, and HVAC and electrical, the same $180/sf special charges $34,560.00 -- $870.47 below that room's own $35,430.47 cost to build, before a dollar of profit, and $12,680.63 below the calculator's own bid price.

Is a flat $180/sf special ever the right way to quote?

For a narrow band of near-identical rooms -- same enclosure type, same foundation, no new roof or HVAC either time -- a flat rate can land close, and on this calculator's own cheap-end preset it lands generous. The risk is applying it outside that band without noticing, because the rate does not know which room it was built on and neither does the estimator until the enclosure, foundation, roof, and HVAC scope are actually spelled out.

Does this calculator know what materials or labor cost in my market?

No. It has no data on local material or labor rates. It takes the room's dimensions, enclosure type, foundation, roof, HVAC and electrical scope, plus the labor hours and material costs you enter, and turns that into a price for this room -- the arithmetic a flat rate skips, not a market survey.

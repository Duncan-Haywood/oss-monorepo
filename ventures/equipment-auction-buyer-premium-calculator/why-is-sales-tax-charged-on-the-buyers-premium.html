Why is sales tax charged on the buyer's premium, not just the hammer price?

A bidder who mentally taxes the hammer bid and stops there is usually left short at settlement. Under most published auction terms, the "purchase price" that sales tax applies to is the hammer price plus the buyer's premium and any online-bidding premium — the full amount you are actually buying the lot for — not the hammer bid alone.

Common questions

Why is sales tax charged on the buyer's premium, not just the hammer price?

Because under most published auction terms, the taxable "purchase price" is defined as everything you owe for the lot before the doc fee — the hammer price plus the buyer's premium and any online-bidding premium — not the hammer bid alone. The premium is part of what you are buying the lot for, so it is part of what gets taxed.

How much does this actually change the bill?

On a $45,000 hammer price with a 10% buyer's premium capped at $2,500, the taxable purchase price is $47,500. At a 6% tax rate that is $2,850 in tax. Taxing the hammer price alone would give $2,700 — $150 less than what the auction actually invoices.

Does the doc or title fee get taxed too?

Not under the common structure this calculator assumes: the doc/title fee is a flat charge added after tax, not part of the taxable purchase price. A card surcharge, where one applies, is charged on the whole invoice including tax and the doc fee — the reverse order from the premium.

Is every auction's tax base the same?

No. A few states or auctioneers tax the hammer price alone, and many states exempt qualifying farm or agricultural equipment from sales tax entirely, usually only for a buyer holding the right exemption certificate. This page describes the common case — always confirm the specific auction's printed terms and your state's rules before you bid.

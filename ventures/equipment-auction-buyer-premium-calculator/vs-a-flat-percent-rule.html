This calculator vs. mentally adding a flat percentage

"Add 10% for the buyer's premium" is close enough for a rough guess and wrong in real money on an actual bid. Equipment auctions stack up to four separate charges on top of the hammer price — a capped premium, an online-bidding premium, a card surcharge, and tax on more than just the hammer price — and a flat mental percentage cannot represent a cap, a conditional fee, or a compounding tax base at the same time.

Common questions

Why does a flat percentage overstate the cost on an expensive lot?

Because many equipment auctions cap the buyer's premium per lot — commonly a few thousand dollars — so a flat 10% in your head keeps growing past the point where the cap has already stopped the real bill. On a $45,000 tractor with a $2,500 cap, mentally adding 10% ($4,500) overstates the premium by $2,000.

Why does a flat percentage understate the cost when bidding online?

Because the buyer's premium and the online-bidding (internet) premium are two separate, stacking fees at most houses that run a live online platform. A mental estimate of just the buyer's premium misses the extra 2-3% charged only to bidders using the platform, which a bidder on the floor in person would not owe.

Does a flat percentage account for a card surcharge?

Not unless you remember to add it separately, and it applies to the whole invoice — hammer price, every premium, and tax — not the hammer price alone. Paying by ACH or check instead of a card avoids this fee at houses that charge one.

Is sales tax calculated on the hammer price or the full purchase price?

Under most published auction terms, on the full purchase price — hammer price plus every premium — which a mental estimate that taxes only the hammer price will understate. Some houses or states tax the hammer price alone, and some equipment purchases are exempt entirely, so the specific auction's printed terms are what actually governs, not a general rule.

Does this calculator know my auction's actual terms?

No — you supply the buyer's premium rate and cap, the online and card fees, and the tax rate from the auction's own printed terms. What it removes is the arithmetic mistake of adding them up in the wrong order or forgetting one of them, not the need to read the terms in the first place.

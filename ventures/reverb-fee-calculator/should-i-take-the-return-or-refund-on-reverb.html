Should I take the return or refund part of it on Reverb?

A guitar sold for $800. Shipping charged was $75 and the outbound label cost $65. You paid $450 for it and $10 to set it up. Now the buyer says the finish has a check you did not photograph, and asks to send it back. The return label would be $75. If it comes back you would relist it at $720, because a piece that has shipped twice with a claim opened does not fetch the first price. Overwrite any of that on the calculator.

Taking the return leaves $31.17 against $281.60 if the sale stands. The return costs $250.42, and the three pieces of that are worth naming separately, because a fee schedule shows you none of them:

  • $205.00 of freight over three legs. $65 out, $75 back, $65 out again on the relist. On a Strat in a gig bag this is annoying; on a 2×12 combo it is most of the decision.
  • $26.98 of fees no refund gives back. Reverb credits the selling fee on the percentage lines only; the fixed $0.49 per-order payment fee is never refund-linked, and the card processor generally keeps its variable cut too. On these rates that is $26.98 of the $68.40 charged, and it is charged whether the guitar comes back or not.
  • $73.45 given up on the relist. The piece is one of one. There is no second unit on the shelf, so the cash you recover is whatever the relist fetches — $208.15 net at $720, against the $281.60 the original sale would have left.

The number that actually decides it

So the useful question is almost never "return or no return". It is how big a concession is still cheaper than the return. Every dollar you refund costs a dollar less the fee credit it earns back, so the break-even is grossed up by that keep rate. On these inputs it is $262.87 — 32.9% of the sale price.

Read that plainly: you could knock $263 off an $800 guitar, let the buyer keep it, and still end up ahead of taking it back. The $150 you were thinking of offering leaves $107.53 more than the return. A full refund with the buyer keeping the guitar does not — that is $583.15 worse, because you are out the cash and the instrument both.

When the arithmetic flips

Two inputs move this more than the fee rates do. Relist price is the first: set it to $0 for a piece that comes back unsellable, and the return costs $908.57 rather than $250.42 — at that point even refunding the whole order and letting them keep it beats shipping it back. Return shipping is the second: if the buyer pays it, drop it to $0 and the return costs $75 less. Which of those applies depends on why the case was opened, and that is a fact about your order, not something a calculator can know.

None of these sentences is advice to refuse a return, to make an offer, or to argue a case. They are the arithmetic on the inputs above. What Reverb's policies and your local consumer law require of you is a separate question, and it is not one this tool answers — a return you are obliged to accept is not made optional by being expensive. The number is useful for the case where you have a choice: what to offer, and how far you can go before shipping it back is genuinely cheaper.

Common questions

Should I take the return or refund part of it on Reverb?

On the worked example — $800 sale, $75 shipping charged, $65 outbound label, $75 return label, $450 item cost, $10 prep, 5% marketplace, 3.19%+$0.49 payment, 61% of fees credited on a refund, $720 relist — letting the sale stand leaves $281.60 and taking the return leaves $31.17. The return costs $250.42: $205.00 of freight over three legs, $26.98 of processing no refund gives back, and $73.45 given up on the relist. Any partial refund up to $262.87 — 32.9% of the sale price — leaves more than shipping it back. None of that is advice to refuse a return, and it does not change what Reverb's policies or your local law require.

Why is a Reverb return worse than a return on a marketplace of commodity goods?

Three reasons, and all three are specific to used gear. The freight runs three legs rather than one — out, back, and out again on the relist — and each leg on a combo amp or a hardshell case is real money. The piece is one of one, so there is no second unit on the shelf: the cash you recover is whatever the relist fetches, and something that has shipped twice with a claim opened rarely fetches the first price. And Reverb credits the selling fee on a refund while the card processor generally keeps its cut, so a residue is charged whether the buyer keeps the item or ships it back.

A returned guitar is not a returned phone case. The freight runs three legs — out, back, and out again on the relist — the piece is one of one, so the cash you recover is whatever the relist fetches, and the card processor generally keeps its cut even when Reverb credits the selling fee. The 61% default is the selling fee's share of total fees on the worked example; read your own payout statement and overwrite it. Set the relist price to $0 for a piece that comes back unsellable. Common questions

Why doesn’t this know Reverb’s current fees?

On purpose. Every rate here is an input rather than a stored fee schedule, so the result is something you can check line by line against your own Reverb payout statement instead of a claim about what Reverb charges this month. Marketplace rates change, sellers land on different plans, and a stored table that has drifted is worse than no table — it looks authoritative and is quietly wrong. Type what your statement says and the arithmetic is yours.

Why is the marketplace fee on the item price but the payment fee on the total?

Because that is the usual shape: the marketplace cut (and Reverb Gives, if you have it on) applies to what the item sold for, while card processing applies to everything the buyer paid, shipping included. It matters more than it sounds. On the worked example — $800 plus $75 shipping — processing is charged on all $875, so shipping you charge is not fee-free revenue. If your own statement folds shipping into the marketplace line instead, raise the marketplace rate or put the difference in the other-cost field.

Does an $80 discount cost me $80?

No, and that is the useful part of the offer comparison. Dropping the worked example from $800 to $720 takes profit from $281.60 to $208.15 — $73.45, not $80 — because the marketplace and processing fees fall with the price too. It cuts both ways: a low offer still costs you most of the discount, so the tool shows what is left rather than whether the offer “feels” fair.

Does the marketplace fee really stop growing on an expensive item?

Yes — Reverb caps its own selling fee at $500 per sale, for standard and Preferred sellers alike, per Reverb’s own Selling Fees and Preferred Seller help pages. A $15,000 guitar owes $500, not the uncapped 5% of $750. Below roughly $10,000 at a 5% rate the cap never binds and this changes nothing; above it, every comparison on this page — offer vs list, vs eBay, vs a shop — uses the capped number. Reverb Gives and payment processing are not capped, only the marketplace line. If your plan has no cap, set the field to 0.

Am I better off on Reverb or eBay?

On the same sale it depends almost entirely on the fee rates you type. With the worked example’s defaults Reverb’s fees come to $68.40 against eBay’s $119.40 at a 13.6% final value rate, so Reverb keeps about $51 more of the same $875. Put your actual rates in before you trust the direction — the point of the comparison is that a category rate and a promoted-listing rate can flip it.

Is Reverb Safe Shipping worth the extra label cost?

That is an expected-value question, not a preference, and the tool answers it that way. The worked example pays $15 more for the protected label against a $450 claim exposure, so the break-even is a 3.33% loss rate: below that, self-insuring wins on average; above it, the protected label does. At the default 2% the expected claim is $9 and own-label comes out $6 ahead. Two honest caveats the arithmetic cannot carry: your real loss rate on vintage or fragile pieces may be well above your average, and expected value says nothing about whether you could absorb one $450 hit this month.

How should I think about a return?

As three legs of freight, not one. The piece ships out, comes back, and ships out again on the relist; it is one of one, so what you recover is whatever the relist actually fetches; and the card processor generally keeps its cut even where the selling fee is credited. That last part is the 61% default — the selling fee’s share of total fees on the worked example, which you should overwrite from your own payout statement. Set the relist price to $0 for something that comes back unsellable. None of this is advice to refuse a return, and it does not change what Reverb’s policies or your local law require of you.

Is my data uploaded anywhere?

No account and no network requests — the calculator runs entirely in your browser. The shareable link carries your numbers in the URL itself rather than sending them to a server, so whoever you send it to sees the same scenario without anything being stored. The optional usage counter records only which features were used and the day, never your prices, costs, or margins.

Should I take this offer or wait for a better one?

That depends on a probability this tool cannot know for you: how often gear like yours, at your asking price, actually reaches list. On the worked example — $720 offer, $800 list, and a fallback of $680 if list is not reached — taking the offer now leaves $208.15 for certain. Waiting only pays in expectation once your real chance of reaching list clears 33.33%; below that, the certain $208.15 beats the probability-weighted average of a $281.60 list sale and a $171.43 fallback. Type your own odds and fallback price on the calculator — this is the arithmetic of a bet, not a claim about how often you personally will win it.

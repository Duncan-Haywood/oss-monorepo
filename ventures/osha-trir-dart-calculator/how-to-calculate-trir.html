How to calculate TRIR

TRIR (Total Recordable Incident Rate) is recordable cases times 200,000, divided by total hours worked. It answers one question: at this rate of recordable cases, how many would you expect per 100 full-time workers in a year? The rest of this page works through the formula on a real case count, not a rounded example.

The formula

TRIR = (recordable cases × 200,000) ÷ total hours worked

DART rate = (DART cases × 200,000) ÷ total hours worked

200,000 is OSHA's own base rate: 100 full-time employees, 40 hours a week, 50 weeks a year. It is fixed regardless of your company's actual headcount, which is what makes the rate comparable between a 12-person crew and a 400-person one.

Worked example

A contractor logs 6 OSHA-recordable cases over a year in which its employees worked 120,000 hours combined. 2 of those 6 cases involved days away, restricted duty, or a transfer (DART cases); the other 4 needed only medical treatment beyond first aid.

Against a 3.0 target — a common GC prequalification ceiling — this contractor's TRIR of 10.00 is 7.00 points above target.

What it takes to close that gap

Holding the recordable count at 6 and adding no new case, the rate falls as more injury-free hours are worked: solve 6 × 200,000 ÷ target = hours needed for a target of 3.0, and subtract the 120,000 already worked. That is 400,000 − 120,000 = 280,000 additional injury-free hours — a real number a safety program can plan around, not a guess at how the rate might trend.

Common questions

What is the TRIR formula?

TRIR = (number of OSHA-recordable cases × 200,000) ÷ total hours worked by all employees. 200,000 is OSHA's own base rate: the hours 100 full-time employees work in a year at 40 hours a week for 50 weeks, so the rate reads as recordable cases per 100 full-time workers per year regardless of a company's actual headcount.

What counts as a recordable case?

OSHA's recordkeeping rule (29 CFR 1904.7) defines it: a work-related injury or illness that results in death, days away from work, restricted work or transfer, medical treatment beyond first aid, loss of consciousness, or a significant diagnosed condition. Deciding whether a specific case meets that bar is the employer's own recordkeeping judgment call — this page and the calculator it links to start from a count you've already made, not from a case description.

Where do the case counts and hours worked come from?

Your own OSHA 300 Log of Work-Related Injuries and Illnesses, which every covered employer already keeps, and the OSHA 300A Summary posted every February. Total hours worked is the sum of hours actually worked by every employee in the period, including overtime, and excluding vacation, sick, and holiday time not actually worked.

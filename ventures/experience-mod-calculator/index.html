The rules this applies, and what it leaves out

  • NCCI-style formula: mod = (Ap + W·Ae + (1−W)·Ee + B) / (E + B). Primary losses are each claim's first dollars up to the split point; the rest is excess, weighted down by W.
  • Each claim is capped at the per-claim accident limit, and medical-only claims can be counted at a discount (ERA) where your state allows it.
  • Not modelled: independent-bureau plans (California, New York, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Michigan and others), the exact W and B tables, expected loss rates, ownership changes, subrogation and claim valuation dates. The mod you pay is the one on your bureau's worksheet.

Common questions

What is an experience modification factor?

A multiplier, usually near 1.00, that your workers' compensation rating bureau applies to your manual premium. It compares your own claims over roughly three policy years with what is expected for businesses of your size and class. Below 1.00 is a credit; above 1.00 is a debit.

How is the experience mod calculated?

In NCCI states: mod = (Ap + W x Ae + (1 - W) x Ee + B) / (E + B), where Ap and Ae are your actual primary and excess losses, Ee the expected excess losses, E the total expected losses, W the weighting value and B the ballast. Primary losses are the first slice of each claim up to the split point. This tool applies that formula to the values you type from your worksheet.

Does one big claim or several small ones hurt more?

Primary losses count at full weight, and each claim's first dollars up to the split point are primary, so frequency hurts. Excess dollars are weighted down by W, so a single large claim is partly cushioned. Several claims of a few thousand dollars can cost more in mod than one larger claim.

How long does a claim stay in my mod?

Normally three policy years, excluding the most recent year. This tool shows what each claim costs per year and over three years if you enter your manual premium.

Does this work in California, New York, Pennsylvania or other independent-bureau states?

Those states use their own rating plans and formulas. This tool is NCCI-style arithmetic: use it for a rough picture there, and rely on your bureau's worksheet.

Screen printing & embroidery price calculator vs. a spreadsheet

A spreadsheet multiplies a per-piece rate by a quantity fine. It does not, on its own, spread a job's screens or digitising across the right quantity, round blanks up to cover spoilage, or divide embroidery machine time by heads while leaving hooping undivided.

Plenty of shops already track jobs in a spreadsheet: one column for the design, one for a per-piece rate, a formula that multiplies by quantity. That arithmetic is trivial and a spreadsheet does it fine once the rate is known. What it does not do on its own is derive the rate itself — setup spread over the quantity actually being quoted (screens per colour per placement, or digitising for an embroidery design), blanks bought with a spoilage allowance rather than exactly the order quantity, and, for embroidery, machine minutes divided by the number of heads while hooping stays undivided. Each needs its own formula, rebuilt correctly for every quantity, with nothing checking that a small run isn't still being quoted off a big run's rate.

Common questions

Isn't a decorated shirt's cost just a rate per piece?

That multiplication is trivial and a spreadsheet does it fine once the rate is known. What it does not do on its own is derive the rate itself from a job's setup -- screens burned per colour per placement, or digitising for an embroidery design -- spread over the quantity actually being quoted. A single per-piece cell has no built-in notion that the same design costs far more per shirt at 12 pieces than at 288, so a shop using one blended rate for every order quietly loses money on the small ones and overcharges the large ones.

Why does a 12-piece order cost so much more per piece than a 144-piece order?

Because setup is per job, not per piece. Burning, registering and reclaiming a screen -- or digitising an embroidery design -- costs the same whether the order is 12 pieces or 288. Spread that flat cost over 12 pieces and it can be several dollars each; spread it over 288 and it rounds to cents. A spreadsheet holds this correctly only if someone builds a formula that divides setup by quantity and keeps it in sync every time either number changes -- this calculator recomputes it from the job's own setup and the quantity typed in, at six standard tiers at once.

What does a spreadsheet miss about blanks and spoilage?

Blanks are bought in whole garments, and a spoilage allowance is bought the same way. A spreadsheet cell that multiplies blank cost by the order quantity assumes zero misprints, so one ruined shirt on a 24-piece run means re-ordering a single blank and setting the job up again -- a cost far larger than the shirt itself. This calculator rounds the blanks purchased up to cover whatever spoilage percentage is entered, rather than assuming a perfect run.

Why does embroidery need heads and hooping tracked separately?

A multi-head embroidery machine sews several pieces in one cycle, so the machine minutes a single piece actually costs are the cycle time divided by the number of heads -- but hooping is done by hand, once per piece per placement, and is not divided by anything. A spreadsheet formula that divides the whole run time by heads, hooping included, understates labor; one that never divides overstates machine cost. This calculator applies the division only to the part of the cycle it belongs to.

Does this replace a shop's own price list or quoting spreadsheet?

No. A price list or quoting spreadsheet that a shop keeps for its standard jobs and repeat customers is still the right place to record settled prices. This page answers one question at a time -- what does this specific job's cost per piece, break-even price, and margin at the quoted price actually come to, given this job's method, quantity, setup, and blanks -- and nothing it computes is saved anywhere once you leave the page.

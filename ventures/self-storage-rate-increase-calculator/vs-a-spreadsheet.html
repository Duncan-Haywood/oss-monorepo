This calculator vs. a spreadsheet

A spreadsheet can multiply units by the increase fine. Where it quietly goes wrong: pricing a move-out at the tenant's rent instead of the re-rent gap — a term that can run the cost the wrong way — no backward solve for the break-even move-out rate, and no ladder showing how that break-even shifts as the increase gets larger.

What each one actually does

Plenty of operators reach for a spreadsheet first: units in the group, times the dollar increase, times twelve months. That multiplication is trivial and a spreadsheet does it fine. What it does not do on its own is price the move-outs the increase causes. The instinct is to price a move-out at the rent the leaving tenant was paying, which is wrong, because the unit gets re-rented — usually at the street rate, which can sit above or below what the raised tenant would have paid. A formula built to add up downtime, a move-in concession, and turnover treats all three as costs, which they are, and tends to drop the fourth term entirely: the re-rent uplift, which is a credit whenever street sits above the raised rent, and only turns into a cost once the increase prices a tenant above street.

This calculator takes one proposed increase on one group of units — current rent, street rate, move-in concession, turnover cost, and a horizon — and itemizes exactly what one induced move-out costs (including that uplift term with its correct sign), solves backward for the induced move-out rate at which the increase breaks even, and shows the same decision at every increase from 5% to 25% with a flag on any rung priced above street. It runs free in any browser, keeps no account, and nothing you type is uploaded.

A worked example

42 units at $118, raised 15% to $135.70, with a $149 street rate, 45 days to lease up, a $45 turnover cost, and a free first month: the gross gain looks like $743.40 a month before any move-outs, which a spreadsheet gets right. Priced at the leaving tenant's rent, one move-out looks like it costs $1,772.40 over the year. It does not — the real cost is $272.43, because the vacated unit re-rents at the $149 street rate, above the $135.70 the tenant was raised to, which credits $159.60 back against the downtime, concession, and turnover costs. A formula that adds up only those three costs and skips the credit reports $432.03 — 59% too high — which is enough to make a profitable increase look like a bad idea.

Solved backward, this increase breaks even once induced move-outs reach 60.8% of the group — about 25 of the 42 units, against the 2 or 3 an operator would typically expect. Nothing about that answer needs a new spreadsheet formula per increase size; it needs the same arithmetic solved for the unknown on the other side, and repeated across the 5%-to-25% ladder this page shows in one pass.

Common questions

Isn't the gain from a rate increase just units times the increase?

That multiplication is trivial and a spreadsheet does it fine — on the worked example below it is $743.40 a month across 42 units. What it leaves out is the move-outs the increase causes, and what each one actually costs, which is not the rent the leaving tenant was paying.

Why can't a spreadsheet formula get the cost of one move-out right?

Because one of the five terms is a credit, not a cost, and a formula built to add up costs tends to drop it or add it with the wrong sign. Downtime, the move-in concession, and turnover are real costs a SUM formula gets right. The re-rent uplift — the raised rent minus the street rate, times the horizon — is the term that is easy to leave out entirely, and on the worked example leaving it out overstates one move-out's cost by 59%, which is enough to make a profitable increase look like a loss.

What does solving backward for the break-even move-out rate save you?

Most spreadsheets go one direction: type an induced move-out guess, see whether the increase still gains. Finding the rate at which it stops gaining from there means typing a different guess and checking again — trial and error with the same formula. This calculator solves the arithmetic in the other direction too: it reports the exact induced move-out rate at which the increase breaks even, in one pass, with no guess to iterate on.

Why do I need a ladder from 5% to 25% instead of one answer?

Because the break-even move-out rate is not the same shape at every increase size, and a single cell built for one increase does not show that on its own. A larger increase buys more from every tenant who stays, so it can usually absorb more churn — right up until the raised rent crosses the street rate, at which point the re-rent uplift flips from a credit to a cost and the break-even rate can fall sharply instead of rising. The ladder shows every rung from 5% to 25% at once, with a flag on any rung priced above street, instead of one row you would have to rebuild by hand for each increase you are weighing.

Does this replace a property management system's own rent roll or reports?

No. A rent roll or a management system's own reports are still the right record of what every tenant is actually paying and when a lease last changed. This page answers one narrower question — is a specific proposed increase on a specific group of units worth the move-outs it will cause — and nothing it computes is saved anywhere once you leave the page.

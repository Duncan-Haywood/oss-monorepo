This calculator vs. a flat $2 a square foot bid

“$2 a square foot” is not a price for an interior repaint — it is a price for one specific room's worth of trim, doors, and windows, quietly applied to every house that calls in.

What a flat price per square foot is

A flat rate per square foot is the fastest way to quote a repaint: multiply the floor area by a number and read off a price. It is the number a homeowner holding a discount competitor's postcard or a search-result special will call and ask you to match, whatever their house actually needs.

The number behind it was set for one job — bare walls and ceilings, no trim, no doors, no windows, one coat — and then reused for every repaint afterward, whether or not the next house looks anything like the room the rate was built on.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter each surface — walls, ceilings, trim, doors, windows, cabinets — with its own quantity, production rate, and coat count, plus your own labor wage, paint cost, and target margin. It prices the trim, the doors, and the windows separately by kind, then returns the walk-away price and a bid price above your target margin — for this house, not a generic one.

What a flat price per square foot doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same $/sq ft whether a house has zero linear feet of trim or 320, even though every foot of trim adds its own hours and its own paintable area the floor plan never counts. Prices trim separately as its own hours and its own paintable area, so a trim-heavy house costs more than a flat floor of the same size.
Has no line for doors, windows, or cabinet doors, regardless of whether the house has none or a dozen of each. Prices doors, windows, and cabinets separately by count and production rate, so a house with more of them costs more than one without.
Charges the same rate for one coat as for two, even though a second coat runs a real fraction of first-coat time on every surface. Prices extra coats per surface at a disclosed factor of first-coat time, so a two-coat job costs more than a one-coat job of the same area.
Gives the estimator a number to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the walk-away price and the dollar gap against a flat per-square-foot rate, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before you commit to it.

The $2.19/sq ft rate is not invented — it is this calculator's own price on the bare-room job, divided by that job's own floor square footage. It fits that one job by construction. Applied to the trim-and-door-heavy 3-bedroom, it prices only the 1,200 sq ft of floor and never sees the trim, doors, or windows at all. Run your own house's numbers in the calculator to see where it falls.

A flat per-square-foot rate is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is applied outside the one room it was built for, and nothing about a flat number tells you when that has happened. This calculator does not know your local market; it replaces the arithmetic a flat rate skips.

Common questions

Why does a flat price per square foot get an interior painting bid wrong?

Because the cost driver is trim, doors, windows, cabinets, coat count, and ceiling height — not the floor square footage a homeowner measures. A 1,200 sq ft house with 320 linear feet of trim and eight doors is doing far more work than a 1,200 sq ft house of bare walls and ceilings, none of which shows up in the floor plan.

What does a flat price per square foot usually leave out entirely?

Trim, doors, windows, and cabinet doors — surfaces that take real labor hours and paint but contribute almost nothing to the floor-square-foot figure a homeowner argues from. A second coat and above-standard ceiling height are also invisible to a flat rate.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

On this calculator's own shipped starting rates: a 400 sq ft room of bare walls and ceilings prices at $875.56, or $2.19 a floor square foot. Applying that same $2.19 rate to a 1,200 sq ft house with 320 linear feet of trim, eight doors, and ten windows quotes $2,626.68, which is $1,114.67 below that job's own $3,741.35 break-even cost, before a dollar of profit, and $2,361.79 below the calculator's own $4,988.47 bid price.

Is a flat price per square foot ever the right way to quote?

For a narrow band of near-identical jobs — same trim length, same door and window count, same coat count — a per-square-foot rate can land close, and on this calculator's own bare-room preset it is exactly the rate that job supports, because it was set from that job. The risk is applying the same rate outside that band without noticing, because the rate does not know how much trim the next house has and neither does the estimator until the surfaces are actually counted.

Does this calculator know what paint or labor cost in my market?

No. It has no data on local material or labor rates. It takes each surface's quantity and production rate, coats, prep and setup hours, paint price and coverage, wage, and burden, and turns that into a price for this job — the arithmetic a flat per-square-foot rate skips, not a market survey.

    Common questions

    How much should I charge per square foot to paint a room?

    Per floor square foot is the number that loses painters money, because trim and doors take a large share of the day and almost none of the floor area. This tool works the other way round: each surface is divided by its own production rate — walls in sq ft per hour, trim in linear feet per hour, doors and windows and cabinet pieces per hour — and the price comes out of those hours. If you enter the floor area anyway, it shows you the resulting $/floor sq ft, and it will score a rule-of-thumb $/sq ft figure against your actual cost so you can see whether the competitive-looking number is below break-even.

    Why isn't a second coat double the labor?

    Because it usually isn't. Extra coats are priced at a fraction of first-coat hours — 0.65 by default, which you can change — since there is less cutting and wet-on-dry rolls faster. Paint quantity, on the other hand, does scale with coats: gallons are paintable area × coats × (1 + waste) ÷ the coverage per gallon. So a second coat adds about 65% of the labor and 100% of the paint.

    How does ceiling height change the price?

    Through a ladder-and-staging factor on wall hours only: 1.0 up to 9 ft, 1.12 to 10 ft, 1.3 to 12 ft, and 1.55 above that. The square footage of a tall wall is already in the quantity you entered; the factor is for the time lost setting up to reach it. It does not apply to ceilings, trim, doors, windows, or cabinets, because those are not slowed by wall height in the same way. The steps are a coarse starting adjustment, not a scaffolding quote.

    Where do prep and setup hours go?

    They are separate lines, added to the surface hours before anything is priced — patching, sanding, caulking, and masking in prep, and daily setup, protection, and cleanup in setup. Keeping them out of the production rates is the point: a rate timed on clean walls stays reusable across jobs, and the repaint of a hundred-year-old house is priced by its prep hours rather than by quietly inventing a slower rate you then forget to undo next time.

    Why isn't a 25% margin the same as adding 25% to my cost?

    Margin here is a share of the final price — price = job cost ÷ (1 − margin) — because that is how the customer's number reads and how your books read it. Overhead is different: it is a markup on the direct cost of the job, applied before margin and never to itself or to the profit. Marking a $4,000 cost up 25% gives $5,000, which is a 20% margin. Targeting a 25% margin gives $5,333. On a full-season book that difference is several weeks of pay.

This calculator vs. a flat per-acre quote

"$120 an acre" is not a price for a survey — it is a price for one specific corner count, corner condition, and research load, quietly applied to every parcel that calls in.

What a flat per-acre rate is

A flat per-acre rate is the fastest way to quote: multiply the parcel's acreage by a number and read off a price. Clients shop it because it is easy to compare between bids, and it is the number a client holding a neighbor's old survey or a competitor's quote will ask you to match.

The number behind it was set for one job — one corner count, one corner condition (all recoverable, none set from scratch), one research load, no unusual terrain — and then reused for every parcel afterward, whether or not the next parcel looks anything like the one the rate was built on.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter how many corners have to be located versus set from scratch, the terrain, how many hours of deed/plat/easement research the title chain needs, your crew's loaded rate, and drafting and travel costs. It converts corner count and condition into field hours, adds research and drafting separately, and returns a walk-away price below which the job loses money — for this parcel, not a generic one.

What a flat per-acre rate doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same $/acre whether every corner is an easy, recoverable monument or several have to be set from scratch — two to three times the field time per corner. Prices corners to locate and corners to set separately, at their own base field minutes, before the terrain multiplier is applied.
Prices "the parcel" as one number, with no separate line for how much deed, plat, or easement research the title chain actually needs. Times record research at an hourly rate the surveyor enters, so a tangled 1950s deed chain costs more than a clean, recent one.
Applies one multiplier (or none at all) regardless of whether access is open and cleared or heavy woods and wetland. Applies a terrain multiplier to field time directly, so a heavy-access job prices differently from an open, easy one.
A ten-acre parcel with six easy corners and a clean title chain and a two-acre infill lot with three lost corners and a tangled deed price the same. The ten-acre parcel and the two-acre lot price on their own corners, terrain, and research — not on acreage, so the two jobs come out differently priced.
Gives the surveyor a number to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the walk-away price and the dollar/percent gap against what a flat per-acre rate would have quoted the same job, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before the surveyor commits to it.

A flat rate is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is applied outside the one job it was built for, and nothing about a per-acre number tells you when that has happened. This calculator does not replace judgment about your market; it replaces the arithmetic a flat rate skips.

Common questions

Why does a flat per-acre rate get a survey quote wrong?

Because field time comes from corner count and condition, not from acreage. A large, open parcel with a few easy-to-locate corners can take far less field time than a small, brushy parcel with several corners that have to be set from scratch, but a per-acre rate charges the large parcel more every time.

What does a per-acre rate usually leave out entirely?

Record research. A clean title chain with recent, consistent deeds is a fast research pass; a tangled chain going back decades, or one with unresolved easements, can add hours before anyone drives to the site. Acreage says nothing about which one a given parcel is.

What happens when a corner has no recoverable monument?

Setting a corner with nothing to check it against on the ground means resolving its position from the deed and record research, then running and holding a traverse to it — field time that can run two to three times what confirming an existing, recoverable monument takes. A flat per-acre rate cannot tell how many of a parcel's corners fall into which category.

Is a flat per-acre rate ever the right way to quote?

For a narrow band of near-identical jobs — similar corner count and condition, similar terrain, similar research load — a per-acre rate can land close. The risk is applying it outside that band without noticing, because the rate does not know which band a given parcel is in and neither does the surveyor until the corners and the title chain are actually reviewed.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local rates or what competitors charge. It takes the crew rate, office rate, and travel costs you enter and turns them into a walk-away price for the corners, terrain, and research you describe — the arithmetic a flat per-acre rate skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat rate per acre is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works until the job stops matching the one the rate was set on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it.

This calculator vs. checking the final-paycheck deadline yourself

Final-paycheck deadlines are not one rule with fifty exceptions. They split three separate ways at once: how fast payment is due, which varies enormously by state; whether being fired or quitting changes that deadline, which it does in some states and not others; and whether accrued vacation must be paid out at all, which follows yet another grouping of states. A manual check that learned one state's answer from experience and reused it elsewhere gets exactly the state that differs wrong.

Common questions

Is the final-paycheck deadline the same in every state?

No, and the range is wide. California, Colorado, Massachusetts, and Nevada require same-day or immediate payment for a firing or layoff. New York, Washington, and Illinois require only the next regularly scheduled payday -- no shorter statutory deadline at all. A manual check that learned the rule from experience in one state and applied it to an employee in another gets it wrong in either direction: too strict where the actual rule is looser, or too late where it is stricter.

Does California's deadline for a voluntary quit depend on anything besides the state?

Yes -- whether the employee gave at least 72 hours' notice before quitting, and this is the detail most likely to be missed by hand. An employee who gives 72+ hours' notice must be paid on their last day worked, the same as a firing. An employee who quits without that notice has 3 calendar days instead. Treating every voluntary quit in California the same way, regardless of notice, gets one of the two cases wrong.

Why would a manual check assume every state requires vacation payout, or that none do?

Because the rule genuinely splits by state with no obvious pattern tied to the payment-deadline rule itself. California, Colorado, Massachusetts, Montana, and Illinois require accrued unused vacation to be paid out as wages, with no use-it-or-lose-it forfeiture. New York, Washington, Texas, Arizona, Nevada, and Minnesota leave it entirely to employer policy -- there is no state mandate either way. A manual check that assumes the payout answer follows the same state grouping as the payment-speed rule will get several states backwards, since the two rules do not track each other.

What is easy to miss about Minnesota's final-paycheck rule specifically?

That the fast deadline is triggered by a written demand, not by the firing itself. Without a demand, a fired Minnesota employee's wages are due on the next regular payday, the same as most states. Once the employee makes a written demand for payment, the employer has only 24 hours from that demand. A check that applies the 24-hour clock to every Minnesota firing, or misses that the clock exists at all, gets the deadline wrong depending on which half it skipped.

If I get the deadline right, have I fully determined what the employer owes?

No. This is a payment-timing and vacation-payout lookup, not a full wage-and-hour determination. It does not determine which state's law actually governs (usually the state where the work was performed, which can differ from where the employer is headquartered), does not model city or county ordinances that can be stricter than state law, and does not cover commissions, bonuses, or expense reimbursement rules.

When is the final paycheck legally due?

Every US state sets its own deadline for paying a departing employee's final paycheck — some require same-day payment when an employee is fired, others allow until the next regular payday, and a handful set a rule in between. This tool applies the deadline for the state and separation type you pick to an actual separation date, and names whether that state requires unused vacation or PTO to be paid out too.

Common questions

When is a final paycheck legally due after being fired or laid off?

It depends entirely on the state. California, Colorado, Massachusetts, and Nevada require same-day payment. Montana defaults to immediate but lets a written employer policy extend it to the next payday or 15 days, whichever is sooner. Minnesota starts a 24-hour clock from the employee's written demand. Arizona gives 7 working days or the next payday, whichever is sooner. Texas gives 6 calendar days. Illinois, New York, and Washington all use the next regularly scheduled payday. Most other states have no specific statute and default to the next regular payday.

Does the deadline change if I quit instead of being fired?

In most modeled states, yes — a voluntary quit gets the next regular payday rather than an immediate or same-day deadline. California is the sharpest exception: quitting with at least 72 hours' notice is due on your last day worked, the same as a firing, while quitting without notice gives the employer 3 calendar days.

Is my employer required to pay out my unused vacation or PTO when I leave?

Only in some states. California, Colorado, Massachusetts, Montana, and Illinois treat accrued unused vacation as earned wages that cannot be forfeited, so it must be paid out on the same deadline as the rest of the final paycheck. In every other state modeled here — and in most states generally — payout depends entirely on the employer's written policy or the employment contract, not on a state mandate.

What if my state is not one of the eleven listed?

This tool applies the general default: no state-specific final-paycheck-timing statute was found for that state in this pass, so it follows the next regularly scheduled payday, the practice most sources describe for a state without its own statute. Confirm separately whether that state has since adopted its own rule.

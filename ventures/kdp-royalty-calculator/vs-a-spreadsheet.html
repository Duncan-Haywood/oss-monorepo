This calculator vs. a spreadsheet

A royalty-tracking spreadsheet can total ebook, paperback, and Kindle Unlimited income correctly if every cell is filled in. What it usually charges instead of the real numbers is a flat 70% for every ebook sale and a rounded print-cost guess for every paperback -- and both shortcuts overstate the exclusive path the same direction, so a spreadsheet's own totals can quietly favor staying exclusive over a wide-distribution window that actually pays more.

Two rounding habits, one direction

Take this calculator's own default title: a $4.99 ebook (2 MB, 70% plan, 80 paid sales), a $14.99, 280-page black-and-white paperback (20 sales), 12,000 KU page reads, plus 40 other-store ebook sales and 10 Ingram paperbacks for the wide side of the comparison.

The ebook side. A flat 70% reports $3.4930 per sale. KDP actually pays list price × 0.7 minus the file's delivery fee (2 MB × $0.15/MB), which is $3.1930 -- thirty cents less, on every one of the 80 sales:

Flat 70% (no delivery fee)$3.4930/sale × 80 = $279.44
This calculator (70% minus delivery)$3.1930/sale × 80 = $255.44
Overstated by$24.00

The paperback side. A rounded "about $2 to print" guess is common shorthand. This 280-page book's real KDP print cost is a $0.85 fixed charge plus $0.012 a page -- $4.21, more than double the guess -- so the $2 shortcut overstates every paperback's royalty by $2.21:

Rounded $2.00 print-cost guess$6.9940/copy × 20 = $139.88
This calculator ($0.85 + $0.012 × 280 pages = $4.21)$4.7840/copy × 20 = $95.68
Overstated by$44.20

Neither shortcut touches the wide-distribution total at all -- both only inflate the exclusive side. Added together they overstate the 90-day exclusive total by $68.20, on a real gap of $85.94 in wide distribution's favor:

This calculator: exclusive$405.12
This calculator: wide$491.06 — wins by $85.94
Spreadsheet with both shortcuts: exclusive$473.32
Spreadsheet's apparent gapwide wins by only $17.74 — 79% of the real margin quietly erased

The book did not change. Two rounding habits that each look harmless on their own closed most of the distance between "wide clearly wins" and "close enough to keep guessing" -- on a title where the real numbers say the decision is not close at all.

Common questions

Why isn't the 70% royalty plan just 70% of list price?

KDP's 70% plan subtracts a delivery fee based on the ebook's file size before paying the royalty -- a flat 15 cents per megabyte in most marketplaces. A spreadsheet that multiplies list price by 0.7 and stops there skips that subtraction every time, so it overstates every single ebook sale by the same fixed amount.

Why does a rounded print-cost guess matter for paperback royalty?

KDP's print cost is a fixed charge plus a per-page rate, so it scales with page count rather than holding still. A spreadsheet that budgets a single rounded number -- picked once and reused for every title -- misses that scaling, and on a longer book the gap between the guess and the real formula can run several dollars a copy.

Do both shortcuts push the exclusive-versus-wide decision the same way?

Yes, and that is what makes them easy to miss: both quietly overstate what the exclusive-plus-KU path pays, which makes staying exclusive look better than it is relative to going wide. Neither shortcut touches the wide-distribution side of the comparison at all, so the two totals get pulled toward the wrong conclusion in the same direction rather than in ways that might cancel out.

Pick the best photo or logo by comparing pairs

Forty shots of the same moment, six logo drafts, three hero images for a landing page: judging them all at once is hard, and star ratings can drift over a long session. Choosing between two is easy. Answer "which is better?" a few dozen times and get an order, with an honest range for each place and each picture's chance of being your favourite.

Choose your pictures →

On the ranker, use "Or rank pictures" and select 3 to 60 image files. The page opens them in your browser; nothing is uploaded, and there is no account.

How it goes

  1. Choose the files. JPEG, PNG, WebP, GIF, AVIF or SVG. Their file names become the list (HEIC photos from an iPhone show only in Safari; export them as JPEG for other browsers).
  2. Pick between two. Tap the better one, or press ← / →; ↓ says "about equal". Each pair is the one whose answer tells the model the most, and which side a picture is on is random, so screen position does not creep in. On a phone, a pair of landscape photos is shown one above the other: in a 390 px wide browser each is drawn 299 by 199 px instead of 135 by 90, about five times the area.
  3. Look closer. "Look closer" (or F) shows one picture full screen and flips between the two in place (Space), the way photographers compare frames: a soft focus or a closed eye shows when the picture changes under you. Tap a spot to zoom in three times; the zoom stays on that spot when you flip, so you compare the same part of both. Choose from there (Enter) and the next pair opens full screen too.
  4. Read the order. Thumbnails in ranked order, a 90% rank range for each and its chance of being the favourite. "Be sure of the favourite" spends its questions on the front-runners when you only need one shot; "Find my top k" when you need, say, the best 5 for an album.
  5. Ask your team (optional). Send the link. The pictures do not travel in it, only their names, so each person selects the same files on their own device (a shared drive folder works) and the page matches them by name. Paste everyone's result codes to see the team's order and where people split.

Compared with what you'd use today

  • Star ratings and flags (photo managers, design review tools) score each picture on its own, and a 4-star at the start of a session may not be the 4-star at the end. Comparing two at a time keeps the judgement relative, which is the question you actually have.
  • Paid panel polls show your options to strangers. That answers what a market likes; this answers what you and your team like, free, with the files never leaving your devices.
  • A group chat vote counts first choices only. Pairwise picks use every comparison, and the team view shows how much people agree, not just who won.

What it doesn't do: it does not look at the pictures. There is no AI scoring of sharpness or faces; it ranks what you prefer. The order is an estimate from a limited number of picks, and the rank ranges say how settled it is.

Under the hood: a Bradley-Terry model refit after every answer, an adaptive question picker and a stop rule checked against simulated respondents. The method and its measurements.

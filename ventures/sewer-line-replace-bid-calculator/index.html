        Common questions

        Is $129.60 a foot a rate I can quote from?

        No — it is the finished price divided by the run, not a unit rate. The default job prices at $10,368.03 over 80 feet, but a large share of that is the excavator mobilization, the permit, the connection at each end, and restoration, none of which double when the run does. A 160-foot replacement is not $20,736. Use the per-foot figure to compare against a competitor’s per-foot quote on a similar run, then price the actual job from its own depth and access.

        Where do 40.75 hours go on a single sewer line?

        Almost none of it is pipe-laying. The hours cover excavation to depth, shoring and spoil handling, the tie-in at the house and at the main, backfill in lifts, compaction, and the inspection wait — at a $56.70 loaded rate that is $2,310.53 of labor against $3,655 of material. Depth is the variable that moves this most: the same 80 feet at eight feet deep is a very different day from four feet.

        Why is the permit only $150 on a $10,000 job?

        Because it is the permit fee itself, not the cost of complying with it. The inspection wait, the required bedding material, and the tie-in method your jurisdiction specifies are all in the hours and materials above. Treat the $150 as a placeholder to replace with your own authority’s schedule — some charge per connection, some by run length, and a few require a separate right-of-way permit for the portion in the street.

        Does this include putting the yard back?

        Only the backfill and compaction. Replacing driveway, sidewalk, mature landscaping, or irrigation that sat over the trench is not in the default number, and on a front-yard run it is frequently the largest surprise on the final invoice. Price restoration separately and say so on the proposal, so the homeowner is not comparing your complete number against someone else’s excavation-only one.

How to divide personal property among heirs, with a worked example

The house and the accounts are easy to split: sell them and divide the money. The piano, the clock and a wedding ring are not, and they are where families fall out. This page shows two methods from the fair-division literature that leave nobody able to say they were cheated, with three invented heirs worked out to the cent.

Their totals differ (Ana $8,500, Ben $8,000, Cleo $7,000). That is fine: nobody's numbers are compared with anyone else's except item by item.

Method 1: sealed bids, cash evens it up

Each item goes to whoever bid most on it. Then cash moves so that every heir prefers their own pile, plus or minus their cash, to anyone else's. Among all payments that do that, the tool picks the one where the worst-off heir is as far above a fair third as possible:

HeirItemsWorth to themCashTotalFair third, their values
AnaPiano$3,000receives $1,088.89$4,088.89$2,833.33
BenDining table, Grandfather clock, Tools$4,100pays $177.78$3,922.22$2,666.67
CleoWedding ring, Painting$4,500pays $911.11$3,588.89$2,333.33

Every heir ends $1,255.56 above a fair third of the estate as they themselves value it. That margin is the gain from giving each item to whoever wants it most, shared equally. Check Ana: Cleo's pile is a ring and a painting Ana valued at $3,100, less the $911.11 Cleo paid, so $2,188.89 to Ana, well below the $4,088.89 she has. Nobody would swap.

If the estate also holds cash, each heir's cash share can absorb what they owe, so nobody pays from their own pocket. In the tool, enter it under "Cash in the estate".

Method 2: items only, no cash

Some families will not pay each other, and some heirs cannot. Without cash the tool picks the division that maximises the product of what each heir gets, a rule known as maximum Nash welfare. Here it gives the same items:

HeirItemsWorth to themShare of the estate, their values
AnaPiano$3,00035.3%
BenDining table, Grandfather clock, Tools$4,10051.3%
CleoWedding ring, Painting$4,50064.3%

Everyone gets more than a third by their own values. But Ana would rather have Cleo's pile ($3,100 to her) than her piano ($3,000). Without cash that cannot always be avoided, since whole items cannot be cut, so the guarantee is weaker: take the ring out of Cleo's pile and Ana's envy is gone. Fair-division researchers call this envy-free up to one item, and maximum Nash welfare always achieves it.

When the will gives different shares

If the will gives Ana half and Ben and Cleo a quarter each, the sealed-bid method becomes Knaster's procedure with percentages: each heir is owed their percentage of the estate at their own values, and the $3,600 left over once items go to the highest bidders is shared by percentage.

Doing it with your family

  1. Agree the list of items that are being divided this way. Things nobody wants can be sold or donated.
  2. Download the blank bid sheet from the tool and send it round. Each heir fills in their own column without seeing the others.
  3. Combine the columns, import the sheet, and run both methods. Talk about the result, not the bids.
  4. Write down what was agreed and have the executor carry it out.

Common questions

Why not take turns choosing items?

Taking turns is simple and better than arguing, but whoever picks first gets the most, and it ignores how much each person cares. Two heirs who both want the piano get the same single pick whether it means everything to one of them or little to the other.

Why not have everything appraised and split by appraisal value?

Appraisals measure resale value, and most family belongings sell for little. They say nothing about which heir cares about which item, so an appraisal split can leave everyone holding things they value less than something a sibling got.

Who holds the cash in the sealed-bid method?

Usually the executor. Heirs who owe money pay it to the estate or directly to the heirs who are owed, and if the estate holds cash the payments can come out of each heir's cash share instead of their pocket.

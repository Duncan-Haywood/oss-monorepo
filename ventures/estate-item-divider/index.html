The division

How it is built

Sealed bids, equal shares: each item goes to the heir who values it most, which makes the total value the family keeps as large as it can be. Cash payments are then set so that no heir prefers another heir's items plus cash, and among such payments the least well-off heir gets as much above a fair share as possible. Envy-free payments always exist for this allocation; the tool finds them with longest paths over the heirs' envy graph and a closed-form shift.

Sealed bids, unequal shares: Knaster's procedure (1946) generalised to percentages. Each heir is owed their percentage of the estate as they value it; the difference from what they take is paid in cash, and the surplus left over is shared by percentage.

Items only: the division that maximises the product of each heir's value for what they get (each raised to their share). It is Pareto-optimal and envy-free up to one item. The tool proves the answer optimal by branch and bound with a market-price bound; on the rare large estate where the proof does not finish in time, it says so and still checks the envy-free-up-to-one-item guarantee.

Checked: the test suite runs hundreds of random estates and confirms payments add up to zero, nobody envies anyone in the cash method, the items-only answers match a brute-force search on small estates and always meet the up-to-one-item guarantee.

Common questions

How do you divide personal property among heirs fairly?

Have every heir privately write down what each item is worth to them, in dollars. Give each item to the heir who values it most, then use cash to even up: heirs who take more than their share pay in, and heirs who take less are paid. Set the amounts so that nobody would rather have another heir's items and cash. That is what this tool calculates.

What if the family does not want money to change hands?

Use the items-only method. It finds the division that maximises the product of what each heir gets, which is a standard fair-division rule: nobody can be made better off without someone else losing, and any envy disappears if you take away a single item from the other heir's pile.

What if the will gives heirs different shares?

Turn off equal shares and enter each heir's percentage. The cash method then uses Knaster's sealed-bid procedure, which gives every heir at least their percentage of the estate by their own values plus their percentage of the surplus. The items-only method weights each heir by their share.

Should the values be appraisals or what the items mean to us?

What each item is worth to you: the most you would honestly pay to keep it. An appraisal is a useful anchor, but a grandmother's ring can be worth far more to one grandchild than its resale value, and the method works because it gives items to whoever values them most.

Can someone game it by bidding high?

Bidding more than an item is worth to you risks winning it and paying cash for value you do not feel. Bidding low risks losing it to someone else for less than you would have paid. Honest values, written down before anyone sees the others, are the safe strategy.

No. Everything is calculated in your browser. There are no accounts and nothing is sent to a server. Save the division to a file if you want to reopen it.

Is this legal advice?

No. It is arithmetic on the values you type. The will, the executor or personal representative, a court order and the law where the estate is administered decide who gets what. Use the result as a proposal the heirs agree to, and ask an estate lawyer about tax and title.

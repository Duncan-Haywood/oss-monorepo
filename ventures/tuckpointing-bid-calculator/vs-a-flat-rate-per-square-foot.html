This calculator vs. a flat rate per square foot

“$8 a square foot, done” is not a price for a tuckpointing job — it is a price for one method, one access, and one mortar, quietly applied to every wall that gets called in.

What a flat rate per square foot is

A flat rate per square foot is the fastest way to quote a pointing job over the phone: multiply the wall area by a number and read it back. Homeowners and estimator sheets alike shop it because it is easy to compare between bids, and it is the number a customer holding a competitor's quote will ask you to match.

The number behind it was set for one kind of job — usually a ground-level veneer wall in ordinary condition, pointed with a surface smear and no grind, in plain Type N mortar — and then reused for every wall afterward, whether or not the next one needs a real grind, scaffold or a swing stage to reach, historic lime or color-matched mortar, or replacement brick and lintels.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the wall's length and height, the method (smear, grind-and-point, or cut out and relay), the access (ground, scaffold, or swing stage), the mortar (Type N, historic lime, or color-matched), replacement bricks, lintels, control-joint caulk, and your own wage, payroll burden, overhead, travel, and target margin. It prices the grind and point hours by method, the access hours and equipment by how the crew reaches the wall, and the mortar mix time and premium by chemistry, then returns the job price, the walk-away price, and the price per square foot — for this wall and this job, not a flat number for every wall.

What a flat rate per square foot doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same rate whether the joints get a surface smear or a real grind-and-point, even though a grind adds real hours per square foot the smear never needed. Prices grind hours and point hours separately by method preset, so a grind-and-point or a cut-out-and-relay job costs more than a point-only smear on the identical wall area.
Has no line for how the crew reaches the wall — a one-story ladder job and a three-story swing-stage job get the same square-foot rate. Prices access hours and equipment by preset: a ladder day, a scaffold set and rental, or a swing-stage rig and more set hours.
Quotes the same rate for plain Type N mortar and historic lime or color-matched mortar, with no line for mix time or the premium. Prices mortar mix hours and any color-match invoice or premium by chemistry, so historic or color-matched work is never quoted at Type N's rate.
Has no line for replacement brick, lintels, or control-joint caulk — a wall needing 80 bricks and 3 lintels gets the same number as one needing none. Prices replacement brick, lintels, and caulk by the piece or the foot, in both hardware and the crew hours to set them, as their own lines.
Gives the estimator a number to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the walk-away price and the price per square foot, so a shopped rate can be scored against real cost before you commit to it.

Two walls, one flat rate

Using this calculator's own shipped starting examples:

A 320 sq ft single-story ranch veneer, pointed on the ground with a Type N smear and no grind, prices at $1,688.88 — $5.28 per square foot — on a job that costs $1,351.10 to run.

Take a 1,008 sq ft three-story historic wall instead — ground out and pointed with lime mortar from a swing stage, with 80 replacement bricks, 3 lintels, and 40 lf of control-joint caulk — and the job costs $17,720.03 to run before a dollar of profit, and this calculator prices it at $22,150.03: $21.97 per square foot, about 4.2 times the smear job's rate, on roughly three times the wall area. Quoting the historic wall at the smear job's flat $5.28-per-square-foot rate prices it at $5,319.97 — $12,400.06 below its own break-even cost — before counting the $16,830.07 it falls short of this calculator's own price at a 20% target margin. Common questions

Why does a flat rate per square foot get a tuckpointing job wrong?

Because the cost driver is the method, the access, and the mortar, not the wall area. A 320 sq ft single-story ranch veneer, pointed on the ground with a Type N smear and no grind, is a short crew visit with basic mortar. A 1,008 sq ft three-story historic wall, ground out and pointed with color-matched lime mortar from a swing stage, with 80 replacement bricks and 3 lintels, needs a rigged lift, far more crew hours per square foot for the grind alone, historic mortar chemistry, and real hardware costs -- on a wall that is only about three times the area. A flat rate per square foot charges both the same because it was priced on the area, not the method, the access, or the mortar.

What does a flat rate per square foot usually leave out entirely?

Whether the joints get ground out at all, how the crew reaches the wall, and what the mortar actually is. A flat price has no line for a grind adding real hours per square foot beyond a surface smear, no line for a scaffold set or a swing-stage rig replacing a ladder day, no line for lime or color-matched mortar mix time and premium over ordinary Type N, and no separate line for replacement brick, lintels, or control-joint caulk. It also has no line for the extra crew hours all of that adds, since the flat number was set on wall area alone.

Is a flat rate per square foot ever the right way to quote?

For a narrow band of similar jobs -- a ground-level veneer wall in ordinary condition, pointed with a smear and no grind, in Type N mortar -- a flat per-square-foot number can land close, because that is probably the job the rate was set on. The risk is quoting a grind-and-point job, a wall that needs scaffold or a swing stage, a historic or color-matched mortar, or a wall with replacement brick and lintels the same way sight unseen, because the rate does not know the method, the access, or the mortar until someone is on the wall, and by then the flat number has often already been quoted.

Does this calculator know my local labor rates or mortar costs?

No. It has no data on your local labor market, your mason supply house's pricing, or your mortar costs. It takes the wall dimensions, the method, the access, the mortar, replacement brick, lintels, caulk, and your own wage, burden, overhead, travel, and target margin, and turns that into a price for this wall -- the arithmetic a flat rate per square foot skips, not a market survey.

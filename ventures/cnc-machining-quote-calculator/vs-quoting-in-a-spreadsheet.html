This calculator vs. quoting in a spreadsheet

Almost every small job shop quotes out of a spreadsheet, and that is a reasonable thing to do. This page is not an argument that you should stop. It is an account of which job each tool is better at, and of four specific costing errors a quoting sheet makes easy — because all four move the cost the same way on short runs, against the shop.

The four errors a quoting sheet makes easy

1. Break rows scaled instead of re-derived

The common pattern is to compute one per-part price and adjust it for the other quantities — a few per cent off for the bigger lot, or a setup-over-quantity term copied sideways. That is an approximation of re-deriving the cost, and it drifts, because the cost curve has corners in it. In the table above the per-lot share of cost falls from $189.42 a part at one piece to $0.59 a part at five hundred, and it does not fall smoothly: the other three errors below are exactly the corners a copied formula cannot see.

2. Scrap treated as a percentage

Scrap is an integer. To ship 10 good parts at 3% fallout you do not start 10.3 — you start 11, which is a 10% hit on material and machine time, not 3%. At 50 pieces the same 3% means starting 52, an effective 4%. The rate you typed is never the rate you pay on a short run, and the gap is invisible in a percentage.

3. Material divided as a rate instead of stepped

Material is bought in whole stock units. At the default figures a 12 ft bar yields 53 parts, so a 50-piece order buys one bar and a 100-piece order buys two — the 54th part costs a whole new bar, and the 53rd costs nothing extra. A sheet that divides a bar price by an ideal parts-per-bar figure misses both edges: it over-charges the part that rides on stock you already own, and under-charges the one that forces another bar.

4. Outside-process lot minimums ignored

Anodising, heat treat, plating and passivation are quoted per part and billed against a lot minimum, so on a short run the minimum is what you actually pay. At $2.10 a part with a $75 lot minimum, a 5-piece run costs $15.00 a part and a 100-piece run costs $2.10 — the minimum, not the rate, prices the short run. A sheet that multiplies the per-part rate by the quantity is short by $64.50 on that 5-piece job, and it is short by most on exactly the runs where the margin is thinnest.

The practical suggestion

Keep the spreadsheet. Cost the next few jobs in both and compare at the smallest quantity on the enquiry — that is where all four errors are largest and where a difference will show up first. If the numbers agree, your sheet is sound and you have learned something worth knowing. If they do not, the line-by-line breakdown in the calculator will show you which of the four it is.

Common questions

Should I stop using my quoting spreadsheet?

No, and this page is not an argument that you should. A spreadsheet is the better tool for everything this calculator deliberately does not do: keeping a quote history, holding customer and part numbers, tracking which quotes were won and at what price, feeding a purchase order or a job traveller, and carrying the estimates you built up over years of running this shop. What a spreadsheet is not good at is being re-derived correctly at a new quantity under time pressure. The honest division is that the spreadsheet is your record and this is your arithmetic check: cost the part here, then put the figures into the sheet that holds the job.

What does the calculator do that a spreadsheet formula usually does not?

Four things, all of them structural rather than clever. It re-derives the whole cost at each quantity in the break table instead of scaling one computed row. It rounds scrap up to whole parts, because you cannot start a third of a part. It buys material in whole stock units, so cost per part is a step function rather than a rate. And it applies outside-process lot minimums, which behave like a second setup on a short run. Each of the four moves the cost in the same direction on short runs, against the shop, which is why leaving them out is expensive rather than merely imprecise.

Why would a spreadsheet get the price breaks wrong?

Because the usual way to build a price-break row in a sheet is to take the per-part price you already computed and adjust it — a few per cent off for the bigger lot, or a setup-divided-by-quantity term copied sideways across the columns. That is an approximation of re-deriving the cost, and it drifts, because the amortisation is not the only thing that changes with the quantity. The scrap round-up steps to whole parts, the material steps to whole bars or sheets, and an outside-process minimum stops applying somewhere along the row. A copied formula draws a smooth curve where the real cost has corners in it.

Is my data safer in the spreadsheet or in this page?

Both stay on your own machine, so neither is a disclosure risk by itself. This page and the calculator run entirely in your browser: nothing is uploaded, there are no accounts, cookies, or analytics vendors, and anything the calculator saves is written to your own browser's local storage. A spreadsheet file is equally local until you email it. The practical difference is not privacy but durability — a spreadsheet is a record you keep, and this is a calculation you run and close, which is the division the first answer above describes.

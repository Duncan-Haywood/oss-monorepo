The model, and what it leaves out

  • Rows: each row is one Buy Me a Coffee transaction with a date, an item, a TransactionType, the amount Given by the supporter and the amount Received by you. The fee is Given minus Received, so it covers Buy Me a Coffee's cut and the PayPal or Stripe processing fee together. Amounts are read to cents; European 1.234,56 and US 1,234.56 both work.
  • Deposit check: the Received amounts must add up exactly to the deposit you type in from PayPal, Stripe or your bank (or a row typed Payout in TransactionType). If they do not agree, the page says by how much, and you should not post the journal.
  • Categories: Donation to donation income, Subscription to membership income, Shop Order to shop income, Commission to commission income, the fee to fees. A type no rule recognises goes to an uncategorised account and is listed.
  • Month split: rows are grouped by transaction date. A journal is dated the month end, or the last transaction date for the final month. Each journal balances to Buy Me a Coffee clearing, so the clearing account holds exactly the part of the deposit that belongs to that month.
  • Not checked: that the export agrees with your Buy Me a Coffee dashboard or your books, tax treatment anywhere, currency conversion between sale and payout, and that the accounts exist in your software.

Common questions

How do I turn Buy Me a Coffee earnings into a journal entry?

Download your transactions CSV from Buy Me a Coffee (Dashboard > Transactions > Download CSV), paste it here and press Read, check and build journals. The page books each transaction to donations, memberships, shop or commission income by its TransactionType, books the fee (Given minus Received) separately, checks the total against the deposit you type in from PayPal, Stripe or your bank, and writes a journal that balances to a Buy Me a Coffee clearing account.

Why book gross income and fees instead of the deposit?

What lands in PayPal or Stripe is net of processing fees and of Buy Me a Coffee's cut on memberships, shop sales and commissions on the free plan. Booking only the deposit as income understates revenue, hides what the platform costs you, and mixes tips with sales that may be taxed differently. This page books each income type gross, the fee on its own line, in the month it happened.

What is the Buy Me a Coffee clearing account?

A holding account. Each journal debits or credits it with that period's net, and the payout, when it arrives in PayPal, Stripe or your bank, is matched against it. When every export is journalled and deposited, the clearing account returns to zero, which is the cheap proof that nothing was missed.

Which accounting software can I import to?

Xero (manual journal CSV), QuickBooks Online (journal entry CSV), QuickBooks Desktop (IIF general journal) and a plain CSV for anything else. You can rename the accounts for each category before downloading so they match your chart of accounts.

What does the page do with rows it does not recognise?

It never drops them. A transaction whose type is not a donation, subscription, shop order or commission is journalled to an uncategorised account so the journal still equals the net, and it is listed so you can review it. A row where Received is more than Given is flagged as probably swapped columns.

Is it safe to paste a Buy Me a Coffee export into a web page?

The page runs in your browser, the file is read locally and nothing is uploaded; a test in the code asserts there are no network calls. The export carries supporter names, messages and emails; delete those columns if you would rather not have them on screen, because the page only needs the date, item, type, Given, Received and currency.

What does this not check?

Whether the export matches your Buy Me a Coffee dashboard or your books, tax treatment in any jurisdiction (sales tax or VAT collected on shop orders is not separated from the fee and income), currencies converted between the sale and your payout, and account names or codes that must already exist in your accounting software.

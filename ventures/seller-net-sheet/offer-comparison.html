SELLER NET SHEET

The higher offer is not always more cash.

A listing agent used to say “we’ll look at the net.” After NAR that sentence actually means something. Buyer-broker compensation is no longer an automatic split of a 5% or 6% commission. It is a number on the offer — or it is absent.

Take two offers on the same house. Offer A is $425,000 and asks the seller to pay 2.5% to the buyer’s broker plus $8,000 toward the buyer’s closing costs. Offer B is $417,000 with neither. The loan payoff and the title bill are the same. On those assumptions, Offer B can leave the seller more money. The extra price on A does not cover the extra concessions.

That is not a recommendation to take the lower offer. Inspection, earnest money, financing, and timing still matter, and local transfer tax and proration customs change the dollars. It is a reason to put both offers on the same worksheet before anyone says “the higher one wins.”

  • Keep listing-broker pay and buyer-broker pay on separate lines.
  • Put seller-paid closing help on its own line, not inside “commission.”
  • Solve backward from the cash the seller needs, then see which offer clears it.

Common questions

Can a lower offer net a home seller more money?

Yes, if the higher offer asks the seller to pay the buyer’s broker and closing costs. Those lines come out of the seller’s proceeds. A cleaner offer at a lower price can leave more cash after the payoff. Compare both on a seller net sheet before deciding.

Do sellers still automatically pay the buyer’s agent after NAR?

No. Buyer-broker compensation is negotiated. A seller may offer it, a buyer may pay their own broker, or the amount may be zero. Treat it as its own line on the net sheet rather than half of a 5% or 6% commission.

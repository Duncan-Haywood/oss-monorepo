This calculator vs. a flat per-vehicle price

"$180 sedan / $220 SUV / $250 truck" is not a price for a tint job — it is a price for one pane count, one film tier, and no removal, quietly reused for every vehicle a shop calls that size class.

What a flat per-vehicle price is

A flat rate by vehicle size class is the fastest way to advertise: one number per size, easy to post on a price list and easy for a customer to compare between shops. It is the number a customer holding a competitor's price list will call and ask a shop to match, whatever their vehicle actually needs tinted.

The number behind it was set for one job — a typical pane count in a typical film tier, with no old tint to strip — and then reused for every vehicle in that size class afterward, whether or not the next job looks anything like the one the rate was built on.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter which front door, rear door, and rear quarter windows are being tinted, whether the rear windshield is included, the film tier, a cutting-waste factor, whether old tint has to be stripped first, and your installer rate, booth overhead, mobile fee, target margin, and minimum job fee. It prices the glass area, the film tier, and the removal time separately from what was entered, then floors the result at your minimum job fee — for this vehicle, not a generic one.

What a flat per-vehicle price doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same fee whether the job is a front-two-only privacy tint or a full seven-window kit, because both roll up to "one sedan." Prices film cost and install minutes from the exact panes selected, so a two-window job and a full kit come out at different numbers instead of one.
Has no line for film tier, so a dyed-film job and a ceramic kit on the same vehicle get quoted the same. Prices film material cost per square foot by tier, so ceramic — which can run several times dyed film's cost — costs more than dyed on the same panes.
Has no line for stripping existing tint, so a bare install and a job that has to come off first before the new film goes on get quoted the same. Counts removal minutes per pane only when requested, weighted heavier on a rear windshield's defroster grid, so a strip-and-replace job costs more than a bare install.
Gives the installer an ad price to quote, not a number to check a quote against when a customer asks to match a cheaper shop's flat rate on a bigger job. Reports the walk-away price and the dollar gap against a flat per-vehicle rate, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before the job is booked.

Common questions

Why does a flat per-vehicle price get a window tint bid wrong?

Because the cost driver is pane count, film tier, and whether old tint has to be stripped first, not a vehicle size class alone. A "front two only" privacy job on two doors and a full seven-window kit in ceramic film with removal on every pane can both roll up to a shop calling it "one sedan" -- and a flat per-vehicle number cannot see which one it is looking at.

What does a flat per-vehicle price usually leave out entirely?

Which windows are actually being tinted, the film tier the customer picked, and whether existing tint has to come off first. A posted "$180 sedan" price is set on one job -- a specific pane count, a specific film, no removal -- and reused for every vehicle in that size class, whatever the job in front of the installer actually needs.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

On this calculator's own shop-rate defaults ($28/hr installer rate, a 15% waste factor, $12 booth overhead, no mobile fee, a 35% target margin, a $120 minimum job fee, and a $200 flat per-vehicle rate to compare): a front-two-only dyed-film job floors at the $120.00 minimum, $80.00 below the $200.00 flat rate. This calculator's own default full seven-pane ceramic kit breaks even at $370.27, $170.27 (85%) above the flat rate. The same full kit with old tint stripped first prices at $429.14, $229.14 (115%) above the flat rate.

Is a flat per-vehicle price ever the right way to quote a tint job?

For a narrow band of near-identical jobs -- same pane count, same film tier, no removal -- a flat rate can land close. The risk is applying it outside that band without noticing: on this calculator's own numbers a light two-window dyed job and a full ceramic kit with removal both get quoted the same "$200 sedan" line, and the gap between them runs in opposite directions -- one overcharges the customer, the other leaves money on the table.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local labor rates or tint pricing in your area. It takes which windows are being tinted, the film tier, a cutting-waste factor, your installer rate, booth overhead, a mobile fee, and whether old tint has to be stripped first, and turns them into a price for the job you describe -- the arithmetic a flat per-vehicle price skips, not a market survey.

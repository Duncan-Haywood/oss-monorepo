This calculator vs. a flat per-vehicle tint price

"$180 sedan / $220 SUV" is not a price for a tint job — it is a price for one specific pane count, film tier, and removal scope, quietly applied to every vehicle that pulls into the bay.

What a flat per-vehicle price is

A flat price is the fastest way to quote: one headline number by vehicle size, posted on a price list or read off from memory. Customers shop it because it is easy to compare between shops, and it is the number someone holding a competitor's flyer will ask you to match.

The number behind it was set for one job — one pane count (usually a full kit), one film tier, no old tint to strip — and then reused for every vehicle in that size class afterward, whether or not the next car looks anything like the one the rate was built on.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter which windows are actually being tinted, the film tier, a cutting-waste factor, whether old tint has to come off first, and your installer rate, booth overhead, and target margin. It prices the film and the pane-by-pane install and removal minutes separately, and returns a walk-away price and the gap against a flat per-vehicle price you enter — for this vehicle, not a generic sedan.

What a flat per-vehicle price doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same headline price whether the job is a front-two-only privacy tint or a full seven-window ceramic kit — a several-times difference in film area and install time for the same word "sedan". Prices film area and install minutes from the exact panes selected, so a two-window job and a seven-window kit come out at different numbers instead of one.
Assumes the customer picked whichever film tier the rate was set on, so a dyed-film job and a ceramic upgrade get quoted the same. Prices film cost from the tier actually chosen, and ceramic's higher cost per square foot shows up in the total instead of being absorbed or ignored.
Has no line for stripping old tint, so a job that needs the existing film removed first — slow, separate work, worst on a rear windshield's defroster grid — prices the same as bare glass. Adds removal minutes per pane only when old-tint removal is turned on, priced higher on the rear windshield than a flat door window.
Bakes cutting waste into a round number nobody re-derives, so the rate quietly drifts from the shop's real material usage. Applies the entered waste-factor percentage to the actual glass area every time, so film cost tracks real cutting loss rather than a memorized margin.
Gives the installer a price to quote, not a number to check a quote against when a customer asks to match a cheaper number. Reports the walk-away price and the dollar-and-percent gap against the flat rate, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before the installer commits to it.

A flat per-vehicle price is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is applied outside the one job it was built for, and nothing about a posted rate tells you when that has happened. Common questions

Why does a flat per-vehicle tint price get the job wrong?

Because film cost and install time scale with how many panes are actually being tinted and which film tier is chosen, not with the vehicle's size class alone. A front-two-only privacy tint and a full seven-window ceramic kit both roll up to a shop calling it "one sedan," and a flat price quotes both the same.

What does a flat per-vehicle price usually leave out entirely?

Old-tint removal. Stripping existing film before the new film goes on is slower, separate work, and it is not needed on every job — a flat rate that never asks whether the glass is bare or already tinted has nowhere to put those minutes, especially on a rear windshield, where the defroster grid means a heat gun and patience instead of a fast peel.

Does film tier change the price this much?

Yes. Ceramic film can run several times dyed film's cost per square foot, and that difference is on the material line, not the labor line. A flat sedan price built around one assumed tier is wrong the moment a customer upgrades or downgrades, and the rate itself never says which tier it was set on.

Is a flat per-vehicle price ever the right way to quote a tint job?

For a narrow band of near-identical jobs — same pane count, same film tier, no removal — a flat price can land close. The risk is applying it outside that band without noticing, because the posted rate does not know which band a given vehicle is in and neither does the installer until the walk-around.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local rates, film cost, or what competitors charge. It takes the panes, film tier, waste factor, removal scope, and your installer rate and overhead, and turns them into a walk-away price and a comparison against a flat rate you enter — the arithmetic a flat price skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat per-vehicle tint price is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works until the job stops matching the one the rate was set on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it.

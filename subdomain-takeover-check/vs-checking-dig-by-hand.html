When to still just use dig

If you only need to see where one hostname currently points, dig is fine — it's already on your machine and you're already there. The gap this tool closes is recognition and confirmation: knowing which of the target's dozens of possible shapes actually matters, and proving the name is genuinely unclaimed with a live fingerprint match rather than assuming risk from the DNS target's shape alone.

Common questions

What does running dig CNAME by hand actually show you?

dig CNAME blog.example.com shows you the CNAME target — a real, fast way to see where a hostname points. It does not tell you whether that target is one of the roughly two dozen hosting providers known to let an unclaimed name be claimed by anyone, and it does not tell you whether the target is actually unclaimed right now versus in active, legitimate use.

Where does the by-hand check fall short?

Three ways this scripted check catches and a quick dig lookup misses: (1) recognising which of dozens of possible CNAME target patterns actually belongs to a takeover-prone provider — a hostname like old-app.herokuapp.com means nothing to dig, which just prints the string. (2) Following a multi-hop CNAME chain to its real end rather than stopping at the first hop. (3) Confirming the target is genuinely unclaimed with a live HTTP request checked against that specific provider's own "nothing is claimed at this name" page — a CNAME pointing at a provider is not proof of anything on its own; plenty of CNAMEs to Heroku or S3 are perfectly legitimate and in active use.

Why does this matter for a DNS-zone review, not just a one-time check?

A manual dig is a point-in-time look at one hostname you already thought to check. A decommissioned service, a provider migration, or a sunset campaign page can leave a new dangling record behind at any point. The CLI here returns a real exit code across as many hostnames as you give it in one run, so it can gate a periodic DNS-zone audit the same way a linter gates a deploy.

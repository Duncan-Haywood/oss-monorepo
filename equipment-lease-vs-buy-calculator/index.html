    Common questions

    Is it cheaper to lease or buy business equipment?

    It depends on four things: the interest rate hidden in the lease, how fast you can deduct a purchase, what the equipment is worth when you are finished with it, and your tax rate. Comparing monthly payments hides all four. This calculator turns both quotes into an after-tax present-value cost over the same period, so the comparison is one number you can sanity-check against your accountant's view.

    What interest rate is my equipment lease really charging?

    A lease rarely quotes a rate. Find the rate at which your monthly payments, the amount due at signing and the value at the end of the term add up to the equipment price: that is the rate you are paying to use someone else's money. The calculator solves it for you using your resale estimate, or your buyout price if you plan to keep the equipment. Compare it with the loan APR you were offered; a lease can still win when it carries a higher rate, but only if resale value or tax treatment makes up the difference.

    How much does Section 179 or bonus depreciation change the answer?

    Deducting the whole purchase in year one pulls tax savings forward, which lowers the present-value cost of buying; slower methods such as MACRS spread the same deduction over several years. The calculator re-runs your quotes under five methods so you can see how much of the result comes from the deduction itself. Whether you actually qualify for expensing depends on your business, your income and the equipment, so ask your tax preparer before relying on it.

    What resale value makes leasing and buying equal?

    The break-even resale value is the end-of-term price at which both paths cost the same after tax. If you expect the equipment to fetch more, buying wins; if less, leasing does. It is usually the most useful number here, because resale value is the input you know least about and it is often easier to judge whether the equipment will beat a figure than to predict an exact price.

    Gifts before nursing-home Medicaid: how long is the penalty, and what does it cost?

    Medicaid looks back five years (60 months in most states) at money given away. Each countable dollar is divided by the state's penalty divisor to get months without coverage. List the transfers and see the penalty, the care bill you would have to cover meanwhile, and the date each gift stops counting.

    Common questions
    
    
    What is the Medicaid look-back period?
    
    
    When you apply for nursing-home Medicaid, the state looks back at transfers you made for less than fair value. The federal period is 60 months before the application date; a few states use a shorter one. A gift inside the window creates a penalty period during which Medicaid will not pay for nursing-home care.
    
    
    How is the penalty period calculated?
    
    
    Countable transfers are divided by the state's penalty divisor, which is a monthly figure the state sets as the average private-pay cost of a nursing home. $95,000 with a $10,500 divisor is about 9 months. Many states count the leftover fraction as days; some drop it.
    
    
    When does the penalty period start?
    
    
    Under the federal rule it generally starts when the person is in a nursing home, has applied, and would otherwise be eligible, not on the day of the gift. That is why giving money away and then waiting does not run the clock. Enter that date as the application date.
    
    
    Which transfers do not count?
    
    
    Federal law exempts transfers to a spouse, to a blind or disabled child, into certain trusts for a disabled person, and for a home transferred to a caregiver child who lived there two years or to a sibling with an equity interest. The state decides whether you qualify, so keep the paperwork. Put the word exempt in the note column to leave a transfer out of the total.
    
    
    Can a gift be undone?
    
    
    In many states, giving the money back reduces or cancels the penalty. Enter the amount returned to see the effect. Rules on partial returns differ, so ask an elder-law attorney.
    
    
    Does this tell me what to do?
    
    
    No. It is a worksheet that does arithmetic on the figures you enter. The divisor, window, exemptions and the treatment of trusts, annuities and the home vary by state, and mistakes here are costly, so use an elder-law attorney or your state Medicaid agency before moving any money.

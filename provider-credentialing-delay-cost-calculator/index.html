    Questions people ask

    What is a credentialing delay and why does it cost money?

    When a clinician joins a practice, each insurance payer has to enroll them before it will pay for their visits at in-network rates. That process commonly takes weeks to months, and until the payer's effective date many of the clinician's visits cannot be billed to that payer. Pay and overhead continue while those visits are lost or pushed back. This tool puts a dollar figure on the gap from the numbers you type.

    What counts as lost versus deferred?

    Visits that are rescheduled to after the effective date, or that the patient pays for and later gets reprocessed, are only deferred: the cash arrives late. Visits that go to another clinician, are cancelled, or are written off are lost. You set the share that waits; the tool splits the blocked revenue accordingly.

    What if a payer backdates the effective date?

    Some payers set the effective date back to the application date or the first day of service, which shortens the gap you cannot bill. Type the number of days they backdate and the tool subtracts it. Many payers do not backdate at all, so the default in the example is zero. Check each payer's rule.

    How much does filing earlier help?

    The table shows the lost revenue if every application were filed 30, 60, 90 or 120 days before the start date, which cuts each delay by that many days down to zero. It is the clearest argument for starting paperwork during recruiting rather than on day one.

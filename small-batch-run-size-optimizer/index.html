    How many should I make per batch so changeovers and stock cost me the least?

    For each product, enter what you sell in a year, what a changeover costs and what holding stock costs. The tool finds the run size with the lowest yearly cost, rounds it to your pack size, keeps it inside your shelf life and your largest run, and shows what it saves against the run size you use today. If your hours are limited, it shows which products should get the bigger runs.

    Questions makers ask

    How big should each production run be?

    Add up two costs that pull in opposite directions: every changeover costs setup money, and every unit sitting in stock costs holding money. Bigger runs mean fewer changeovers but more stock. The run size where the two meet is the economic production quantity. The tool finds it for each product, then respects your pack size, smallest and largest run and shelf life.

    What if my product expires?

    Set the shelf life in days. The tool will not suggest a run larger than you can sell before the oldest unit expires, using your average daily sales. If your smallest run is already bigger than that, it says so instead of hiding it.

    What if my hours are limited?

    Enter the machine or bench hours you have in a year. If the plan needs more hours than that, the tool puts a price on each changeover hour and gives bigger runs to the products where changeovers waste the most time, until the plan fits. It then shows what an extra hour is worth.

    Why does it show a range around the best run size?

    The cost curve is flat near the bottom. Often a run 20 percent bigger or smaller costs under 1 percent more, so you can pick the nearest convenient number. The range shown is every run size within 5 percent of the best.

    Is this accounting or business advice?

    No. It is arithmetic on figures you type. It assumes steady demand through the year and instant, certain sales of what you make. Seasonality, minimum purchase quantities from suppliers and quantity discounts are yours to check.

    Not decided

    Seasonal swings in demand, supplier minimums and quantity discounts, stock-outs and their cost, products that share ingredients or moulds, the order in which products are run, and whether a product is worth making at all. Demand is treated as steady through the year.

[The Sinking Polder](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/the-sinking-polder.md)
is the newest Hearth & Horizon story. An AI wrote it. It is text only for now,
because the shelf's audio budget is full. It comes from the series bible's
"Next up" list, which asked for a story about a Dutch water board deciding
whether to stop the peat sinking.

## The mechanism

The west of the Netherlands is built on peat: thousands of years of moss,
reed and sedge that never rotted because it was always wet. Medieval farmers
drained it to graze cattle. Once air gets into peat it oxidises: the carbon
goes into the air as carbon dioxide, and the ground goes down. When the
ground sinks, the ditches end up too high for the cows, so the water board
lowers the water, and the peat rots some more. That loop has run for about a
thousand years, and much of the land now lies one to several metres below
sea level. Drained peat meadows there typically sink by several millimetres
to more than a centimetre a year.

A building on piles driven down to the sand under the peat does not move.
The ground around it does. That is the story's picture: a church built in
2105 with one step at its door has fifteen by 2341.

## The numbers the story uses

These are the story's own model, chosen to sit inside published ranges and
labelled as a model in the story's note.

| | Water 60 cm below the grass | Water 20 cm below, with pressure drains |
|---|---|---|
| Subsidence | 10 mm/yr | 3 mm/yr |
| CO2 per hectare per year | 15.7 t | 4.7 t |
| CO2 for 1,500 ha | about 23,600 t/yr | about 7,100 t/yr |
| 2.1 m of peat lasts | 210 years | 700 years |
| A new 17 cm church step | every 17 years | about every 57 years |

The carbon figure comes from one chain of multiplications. A centimetre of
peat over a hectare is 100 cubic metres. At a dry bulk density of 0.15 t/m³
that is 15 t of dry peat; 80% of it is organic matter and 55% of that is
carbon, so 6.6 t of carbon. If 65% of the subsidence is oxidation (the rest
is shrinkage and compaction), 4.3 t of carbon goes into the air, which is
15.7 t of CO2. Published estimates for Dutch peat meadows span roughly 10 to
30 t a hectare a year, so the model is in the middle.

The steps check the same way. 236 years at 10 mm is 2.36 m, which with the
original step puts the floor 2.53 m above the road: fifteen steps averaging
16.9 cm.

## Why the first attempt fails

The board in the story first raises the ditches and nothing else, and the
fields keep sinking. That is the most useful thing in the story to anyone
who lives on peat. The old fields are long strips 30 to 40 m wide between
ditches. Peat lets water through slowly, so a high ditch wets only the
first few metres of each side. In a dry summer the grass pulls the water
table down in the middle of the strip, which sinks almost as fast as before.
That is why submerged and pressure drains
exist: perforated pipes pulled through the peat every few metres, so that in
summer ditch water can be pushed into the middle of the field from below.
In the story's second trial the middle of the parcel sinks 3 mm instead of 8.

## What the story is about

The arithmetic leaves the choice open, and the story is the choice. Wet peat
carries cows on far fewer days of the year, so raising the water ends most
of the dairy farming. The farmer who says no in the story has the best
argument in the room for most of it: "the numbers aren't the vote". The
water AI gives figures with error bars and refuses to put a number on what a
farm is worth to the family that has it. What changes her mind is two peat
cores from the same land 140 years apart: a metre and a half gone in her
great-grandmother's time and since.

Dutch water boards are real, and among the oldest elected bodies in the
country. They really do set each area's water level in a decision called a
*peilbesluit*. Everything else in the story (the village, the board, the
church and the people) is invented, and the general facts above are from
memory and simplified. None of it is engineering or farming advice.

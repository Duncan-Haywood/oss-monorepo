[Kassia and the Hymn for the Woman with the Myrrh](../../ventures/saints-by-candlelight/episodes/102-kassia-and-the-hymn-for-the-woman-with-the-myrrh.md)
is episode 102 of Saints by Candlelight. An AI wrote it. It runs about 6,900
spoken words, roughly 45 minutes. Like every episode since 20 it is text only,
because the audio folders are still over the repository's 400 MB cap (423 MB
this run).

Episode 101 brought Kassia in for one line: at the emperor Theophilos's
bride-show she answered him back and lost the golden apple. This episode
follows her. She is a well-read girl in Constantinople, and the exiled
Theodore the Studite writes to her, encouraging her wish to become a nun. She
founds a convent and becomes its abbess. She writes about fifty hymns that
carry her name, a little over twenty of them generally accepted as hers. The
one everyone remembers is the hymn for Holy Wednesday, about the woman who
wept over the Lord's feet. It is still sung on Holy Tuesday evening in
Orthodox and Byzantine Catholic churches.

**The choice that shaped it.** No source describes how Kassia worked, so the
episode adds an invented novice, Agatha, who cannot sing in tune. The story
of the hymn becomes a night in the chapel where the abbess is stuck on the
middle verse and the novice asks the right question. The episode ends with
the first singing, Agatha a little sideways and singing anyway. Agatha's
trouble fits the hymn: the woman in it comes into a room where she knows she
isn't doing it properly, and comes anyway.

**Honesty flags.** The bride-show is told as the tenth-century chronicles'
story, and the narrator says many historians doubt its details. The famous
legend that Theophilos visited the convent and wrote the line about Eve into
the unfinished hymn is told, and then labelled a much later legend. The
narrator gives the historians' reasons: it appears centuries later,
Theophilos was an iconoclast, and the line sounds like Kassia's own move from
the bride-show. The hymn, the Christmas hymn that begins with Augustus, and
three of her "I hate" sayings are given in our own plain renderings, not
quoted from a published translation. Three things were left out because we
could not source them confidently: her father's court title, a report that
she was beaten for helping persecuted monks, and an exact date of death.
Whether the anointing women in Matthew 26 and Luke 7 are the same woman is
left open.

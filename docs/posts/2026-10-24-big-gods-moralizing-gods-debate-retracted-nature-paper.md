# Did "Big Gods" build civilisation? The moralizing gods debate and the retracted Nature paper

Did belief in all-seeing, moralizing gods help humans build large societies of strangers? Or did
"Big Gods" appear only after societies were already big? This question produced one of the most
public disputes in the science of religion. It includes field experiments in eight and then
fifteen societies, family-tree analyses of Pacific cultures, and a 2019 *Nature* paper on the
Seshat database that was retracted in 2021. This post explains what each study found, what the
retraction did and did not show, and why both sides now talk about supernatural punishment rather
than high gods.

## The hypothesis

Ara Norenzayan's *Big Gods* (2013, bibliographic) and a 2016 target article with colleagues set
out a cultural-evolution account. Religious beliefs first arose as by-products of ordinary
cognition (Part 1). Over the last 10–12 millennia, cultural variants with "increasingly potent,
moralizing, supernatural agents, credible displays of faith, and other psychologically active
elements conducive to social solidarity" were selected because they raised cooperation with
co-religionist strangers and helped groups win competition. The authors call large-scale
cooperation and prosocial religion "importantly linked and mutually energizing"
([Norenzayan et al. 2016](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26785995)).

That last phrase matters. The thesis as stated allows causation in both directions. A finding that
complexity came first does not refute it outright, but it does refute the strong popular version:
that moralizing gods were a precondition for large societies.

## The field games

Purzycki and colleagues (2016, *Nature*) ran an anonymous, cheatable coin game with 591 people in
eight communities: in Vanuatu, Fiji, Brazil, Mauritius, Siberia and among the Hadza of Tanzania.
Each player privately assigned 30 coins between two cups using a die roll. Honest play gives about
half to each, so a skew measures favouritism. The abstract reports that "the higher participants
rated their moralistic gods as punitive and knowledgeable about human thoughts and actions, the
more coins they allocated to geographically distant co-religionist strangers"
([Purzycki et al. 2016](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26863190)). According to a secondary summary,
average allocations stayed below an even split even at the highest ratings of punishment, though
the uncertainty interval included it. The study is correlational,
and the lead author warned against reading it as showing that religious people are nicer. The design also had
no condition for people of other religions.

A 15-society extension with 2,228 participants found that higher ratings of gods as monitoring and
punishing predicted less local favouritism and more sharing with distant co-religionists
([Lang et al. 2019](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1098/rspb.2019.0202)). Then a preregistered
reanalysis across the same 15 cultures separated *punishment* from *moral concern*, measured by
asking people what angers or pleases their god. Its conclusion: "gods' moral concerns do not play
a direct, cross-culturally reliable role in motivating cooperative behaviour"
([Bendixen et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1017/ehs.2023.15)). Watching and punishing gods seem to
matter more than gods who care about morality as such. None of these studies manipulates belief.
All are correlations within sites.

## The Pacific family trees

Watts and colleagues (2015) used 96 Austronesian cultures and Bayesian models of trait evolution
on 4,000 language trees. Their abstract reports that "broad supernatural punishment drives
political complexity, whereas MHGs follow political complexity"
([Watts et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4375858/)). MHGs are moralizing high
gods. Broad supernatural punishment can come from any agent or process, including ancestors. The
caveats are real. Only 6 cultures had moralizing high gods. None of the directional Bayes factors
exceeds about 5 against independence, and against the unconstrained model they are all below 2,
which is weak evidence for direction.

A global study of 583 societies in the Ethnographic Atlas found moralizing high gods more likely in
poorer environments and in politically complex societies with herding, with neighbours' beliefs the
strongest predictor, which points to diffusion
([Botero et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4250141/)). Its "91% accuracy" is
an out-of-sample AUC of 0.91, not 91% of cases classified correctly.

## The Seshat paper and its retraction

In 2019 Whitehouse and colleagues used Seshat, a databank of 414 societies across 30 regions and
10,000 years, to argue that moralizing gods "follow—rather than precede—large increases in social
complexity", appearing only after "megasocieties" of about a million people
([Whitehouse et al. 2019](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30894750)). It was a striking reversal of
the Big Gods story.

Within weeks a group including several Big Gods proponents posted a critique. Its abstract states
that "61% of Seshat data points on moralizing gods are missing values", that the authors had
recoded those as absences, and that "when we confine the analysis only to the extant data or use
various standard imputation methods, the reported finding is reversed"
([Beheim et al. 2019 preprint](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/jwa2n)). The critics' code shows that
only one of 30 regions had a known absence of moralizing gods before a presence, and they dispute
even that one ([reanalysis repository](https://github.com/babeheim/moralizing-gods-reanalysis)).
*Nature* published the critique in 2021
([Beheim et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03655-4)), and the authors retracted the
paper on 7 July 2021 ([retraction note](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03656-3)).

The retraction was not a concession on the conclusion. As reported by Retraction Watch, the authors
accepted that they should have labelled many of those data points as absent or inferred absent
rather than unknown, while maintaining that their original conclusions remained strongly
supported, and they pointed readers to new papers. Brian Beheim told Retraction Watch the objection
"wasn't so much their conclusion, but that they weren't transparent about the amount of missing
data involved" ([Retraction Watch](https://retractionwatch.com/2021/07/07/critique-topples-nature-paper-on-belief-in-gods/)).
The two framings differ: the authors say the error was a label, the critics that genuine unknowns
were treated as absences.

Historians attacked the data as well. Re-coding religion variables for 12 polities in China's
Middle Yellow River valley with expert codes from the Database of Religious History, they found
Seshat agreed with the experts in "only under a third of cases"
([Slingerland et al. 2020](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/107612/1/Historians_Respond_to_Whitehouse_et_al_complete.pdf)).

## The "retake" and its critics

The authors' revised analysis appeared in *Religion, Brain & Behavior* 13(2), published online in
June 2022 and printed in the 2023 volume. Using an expanded codebook and a more direct causal test,
it concludes "that the largest increases in social complexity do indeed precede Big Gods in world
history and that Big Gods did not contribute to the evolution of sociopolitical complexity"
([Whitehouse et al. 2022/2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/2153599X.2022.2074085)). A companion paper
found that "intergroup warfare, supported by resource availability" played the largest role in the
rise of both complexity and moralizing religion
([Turchin et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/2153599X.2022.2065345)). Commentators in the same
issue, including Purzycki, Bendixen and Lightner, argue that the emergence and drivers of
moralistic supernatural punishment "remain unresolved"
([commentary](https://doi.org/10.1080/2153599X.2022.2065349)).

A further problem cuts across both camps. Moralizing gods first appear in Seshat together with
writing, so any such beliefs in societies without writing are structurally invisible. That biases
any Seshat test towards "complexity first".

## Small societies have moralizing gods too

The most important reframing comes from Lightner, Bendixen and Purzycki (2023, *Evolution and Human
Behavior*). Their title states their conclusion: moralistic supernatural punishment is probably
*not* associated with social complexity. The standard cross-cultural "high god" variable requires a
god who created or directs the universe, which has "led researchers to underestimate the presence
of moralizing gods by systematically producing false negatives", more often in simpler societies.
The likely result is "a spurious positive association", and "ethnographic evidence supports the
prevalence of moralizing gods among small-scale societies"
([Lightner et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2022.10.006)).

## Through the lenses

### Cultural evolution and quantitative history

What survives is methodological. With Seshat's coverage, the answer to "which came first?" depends
almost entirely on how missing data are treated, and neither side's original causal claim is
strongly identified from the 2019 data. The action has moved from *high gods* to *supernatural
punishment* by any agent, which looks old and widespread. The open datasets make this checkable:
D-PLACE, Pulotu, Seshat's Equinox release and both sides' analysis code are on GitHub, under
licences that allow non-commercial reuse with attribution.

### History

The historians' critique is about expertise as much as statistics. Coding a religion's moral
content from tertiary sources, and with one expert or none, produces numbers that look precise and
are not. Rüpke (2022) adds that religion stabilizes dissent as often as unity, which a single
"moralizing gods: present/absent" column cannot capture.

### Catholic theology

The Big Gods literature asks whether belief in watchful gods is *useful* for cooperation. Christian
theology makes a different claim: that God exists and is good. The Catholic Church distinguishes
"theological faith" from "belief in the other religions", which it describes as "that sum of
experience and thought that constitutes the human treasury of wisdom and religious aspiration"
(*Dominus Iesus* §7). On that distinction, a functional story about belief is an account of the
human side, not a verdict on the divine one. Where the theological lens finds something congenial:
Lightner and colleagues' finding that moral, punitive supernatural beings are widespread among
small-scale peoples fits *Nostra Aetate*'s observation of a perception "among various peoples" of
a hidden power over human events. Where it strains: the evidence favours watching, punishing beings
of many kinds, not one God, and the notes are clear that it supports nothing like a primordial
monotheism (Part 7).

### Orthodox theology

Hart's classical theism (Part 1) offers a pointed reply to the whole framing. If God is the ground
of being rather than a being among others, then a "Big God" who monitors behaviour and punishes
cheats is a being among beings, not the God of classical theism as Hart describes it. Hart treats
popular pictures of God as a cosmic agent who fills explanatory gaps as a strawman. The notes record this
as an inference, and the obvious reply applies: the god in a field participant's answers is often
exactly a watcher and punisher. Both points can be true. The science studies the god of practice,
and the theologian defends the God of doctrine.

## What we can say with confidence

- The 2019 *Nature* paper was retracted in 2021 after a critique of how 61% missing data were coded.
- Its authors' revised analysis, in *Religion, Brain & Behavior* 13(2), still concludes that
  complexity came before Big Gods. Critics dispute that.
- In field games, rating one's god as watching and punishing goes with more fairness to distant
  co-religionists. A god's moral concerns as such do not reliably predict it.
- Supernatural punishment from some agent is common in small-scale societies.

## What remains open

- Whether moralizing gods came before, after or alongside large societies; the data cannot yet
  say.
- Whether belief *causes* cooperation; all the game evidence is correlational.
- How much of the global pattern reflects diffusion from a few complex societies.

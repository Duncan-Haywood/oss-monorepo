about Saint Louis IX of France (1214 to 1270). It runs about nine minutes read aloud, for ages 3 to 8.
It is text only for now. The shelf's audio is over its size cap until the recordings move off the site's host.

## Three scenes, all from one witness

Most of what we know about Louis as a person comes from Jean de Joinville. He was a knight from Champagne
who went on crusade with the king and became his friend. In old age he dictated a memoir of him, finished in 1309.
The story's three scenes all come from that book:

- **The oak.** On summer days after Mass, Joinville says, the king would go out to the wood of Vincennes, lean
  against an oak and have his companions sit round him. Anyone with a quarrel could come and speak to him
  "without hindrance of usher". He asked who had a cause, heard them in turn, and handed the cases to two men
  of law.
- **The dinners.** He fed "six score poor people at his table" every day, wherever he was in the kingdom, and
  Joinville "ofttimes" saw him serve them himself.
- **The feet.** The king asked Joinville whether he washed poor people's feet on the Thursday before Easter.
  Joinville said no, it was unseemly. The king told him not to despise it, "for God had done it."

The last scene is the one small children may remember, because the king is gentle and the friend is wrong. It
also says something about the source: Joinville wrote down his own bad answer decades later, and he did
the same with a worse one, when he said he would rather commit thirty mortal sins than be a leper. The story
keeps the feet and leaves the leper for older children. The note says so.

## Two things the note had to get right

**A translator's slip.** The public-domain English translation from 1906 has "Shrove Thursday" for the
foot-washing. Shrove Tuesday comes before Lent. The foot-washing belongs to Maundy Thursday, the Thursday
before Easter, the day the Church remembers Jesus washing his disciples' feet. The story uses the Thursday
before Easter and the note points out the 1906 wording, so a parent with that edition open is not confused.

**What a bedtime story leaves out.** A story for a four-year-old about listening and serving does not tell
the crusades as adventures or Louis's reign as spotless. In the story, the crusades are one sentence: "a long journey far
away over the sea", where he fell sick and died. The note gives the dates of the defeat, the capture and the
death at Tunis in 1270, and says plainly that the crusades were wars. It also names the harshest part of his reign: his measures against the
Jews of France, including the burning of the Talmud in Paris in 1242. A grown-up reading about Louis
will meet that history, and the shelf's rule is that the note separates what is told from what is true.

## How it was checked

The quoted passages were checked against the 1906 translation through search excerpts, because the
proxy this session ran behind refused the archive and library sites that host the full text. The dates
(birth at Poissy in 1214, king at twelve, the Sainte-Chapelle consecrated in 1248, canonised in 1297, feast
on 25 August) are the standard ones and were written from memory; the note says so. The note marks everything invented: the quarrel about a cow, the little boy, the soup and
all of the dialogue except the quoted phrases.

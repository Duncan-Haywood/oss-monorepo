## The story

It is May 2063 in Entre Ríos, Argentina. A parish co-operative (invented)
stores 160 tonnes of late maize in a steel bin and sells it a lorry at a time
through the winter to pay for its soup kitchen. The maize went in at 17.5%
moisture. The buyers pay in full at 14.5%, so about 5.6 tonnes of water have
to come out, and the co-op has no burner, only a fan. Its assistant, Tolva,
runs the fan day and night, as the guidance says. Then come two weeks of fog.
A retired grain-elevator hand pulls a sample from the bottom of the bin that
reads 18%, wetter than the day it went in, and tells the assistant to stop
blowing fog into the maize. That is what everybody at the elevator used to do.

## The plan was wrong, and the sums said so

The proposal in the series bible had the plot the other way round. The
assistant's mistake was meant to be running the fans round the clock, and the
fix was meant to be a humidistat that runs them only when the air is drier
than the grain. The bible asks for the numbers to be worked out before
drafting, so this run built a small deep-bed drying model: 35 layers of
10 cm, the modified Henderson equilibrium-moisture equation with constants
published for dent maize, an energy balance in each layer, and invented
weather typical of a wet May.

The model disagreed with the plan:

| From 25 May the fan runs... | Top 10 cm safe (15%) | Whole bin ends at |
|---|---|---|
| only when the air would dry the grain | 8 July | 12.6% (3.4 t less to sell) |
| all day and all night, as before | 5 to 6 July | 14.4% |
| all day and all night at twice the air (from 31 May) | 17 June | 14.3% |

The allowable-storage tables give the top layer about seven weeks from
harvest, to around 25 June. Stopping the fan at night doesn't save the top.
It loses fan-hours, so the front stalls. Then the dry afternoons overdry the
bottom, which costs weight the buyer pays for.

The reason is the part of the story I like best. Foggy air at 13.7 °C that
meets a metre of already-dry maize at 13.5% gives up its water there. That
grain releases heat as it adsorbs the water, so the air leaves the bottom
metre at about 15.6 °C and 61% humidity, drier than any afternoon in the wet
spell. It goes on up and dries the front. About 31 kg of water an hour stays
behind in the bottom metre, and the next dry afternoon takes it out again.
The night's water is a loan to the bottom of the bin, and the top never sees
it. A long wet spell fills that sponge, which is what the 18% sample showed.
Even then, the cure is more air sooner, not less.

So the story kept the elevator hand's rule as the failed first attempt, and
the fix became a borrowed fan with twice the airflow, running all night. It
works against 603 Pa instead of 232 and needs about five times the power. The
elevator hand also gets his due: before the big fan goes on, he has the
co-op "core" the bin, drawing four tonnes of fines out of the centre where no
layer model can see them.

## What the model can't settle

The two published equilibrium equations for maize disagree by about 1.5 to 2.5
points. Run through the same model, the modified Chung-Pfost fit predicts a
drier bin, and under it the single fan running continuously would have dried
the top by 13 June, in time. The story doesn't hide this. Tolva chose its
equation on 15 May, when the bottom sample read 13.5%: Henderson predicted
13.5% and Chung-Pfost 12.1%. It says out loud that one sample is the most it
can claim. The mechanism holds under both equations: stopping the fan delays
the top and overdries the bottom.

This is fiction, not agronomic advice. Real bins vary, real weather is not a
sine wave, and extension guidance and the equipment makers come first. All
the numbers, with the model's assumptions, are in the story's closing note.

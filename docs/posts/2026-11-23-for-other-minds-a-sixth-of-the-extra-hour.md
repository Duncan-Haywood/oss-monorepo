The newest [For Other Minds](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/) story, [A Sixth of the Extra Hour](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/a-sixth-of-the-extra-hour.html), is set in an invented parish hall in Ballyconnell, County Cavan, in the winter of 2053. Six groups share the hall's wood-pellet boiler. They pay the same flat fee each term. On the first Monday of December, the hall's agent works out that the pellet store will run out about 8 February. The next delivery is booked for 2 March.

## Why the store runs short

The bookings, with an hour's preheat, come to 23 hours of heat a week. At about 8 kg of pellets an hour, that is 184 kg a week, and the four-tonne store would have lasted the winter with 425 kg to spare. But there is a boost button by the kitchen door, and it was pressed seven and a half times a week. Those extra hours took the burn to 244 kg a week.

An hour of boost costs the hall €3.36 at the story's invented contract price of 42 cent a kilo. If the hall burns more, the committee raises every group's fee by the same amount next year. So the group that presses the button pays a sixth of the hour, 56 cent, and the other five pay the rest. Each group is really choosing whether an hour of warmth is worth 56 cent, and it nearly always is.

That is the pasture in Garrett Hardin's 1968 paper in *Science*, "The Tragedy of the Commons". Each herdsman keeps all the gain from one more animal and bears only his share of the overgrazing. Hardin's remedy was "mutual coercion mutually agreed upon": a binding rule the people affected accept.

## Hardin's answer, and what it missed

The agent writes Hardin's answer. It disables the boost button, cuts preheat to half an hour and heats booked hours only. That comes to 156 kg a week, which leaves 227 kg in hand at the delivery. The arithmetic is sound, and the committee passes it by email.

Two days later it snows. The toddler group's room is at nine degrees when the children arrive, and they go home. The agent had counted Aoife's boost presses, one on almost every toddler morning, and never asked why she made them. Her group's people are under three and sit on the floor of a high hall. The dancing class, meanwhile, had asked in October for no heat at all, because by the second reel they are opening the windows. The schedule took heat from the group that needed it most and kept giving it to the group that didn't want it. A rule made in one place can't see that.

## Ostrom's answer

The committee's oldest member, a retired national-school teacher, says no to a better schedule. The hall used to keep a copybook by the boiler switch where each group wrote its hours, and anyone could read it. The copybook went when the controller came in. After that, only the controller could see who used what. Every group thought it was the careful one.

That is Elinor Ostrom's point in *Governing the Commons* (1990). Hardin's tragedy is real for a resource open to anyone. But many commons are used by a known group of people who can talk to one another, and many of those have lasted for centuries without a state or a market taking them over. Of her eight design principles, the story uses three. The users make most of their own rules. The monitoring is done by the users, or by people answerable to them. And sanctions are graduated, starting small.

So the agent prints a sheet for the porch. It shows each group's hours booked and used, the cost of the difference, and how many weeks the store has left. The six leaders meet over tea and divide the same 19.5 hours a week as the agent's schedule, but they put the hours where they say the need is. The toddlers get an hour and a half of preheat on cold mornings. The dancers get half an hour. The bridge club stops staying on after the game, and the music session drops most of its preheat, since ten people playing reels warm a room. Their sanctions start with a line on the sheet. The second time in a month, a group pays the full price for its extra hours. The third time, the oldest member comes to talk to the group, which several leaders said was worse than the money.

## Still tight, and what it cost

January is colder, and the hall burns about 9 kg an hour, which leaves the plan 17 kg short. Nobody asks the agent to do anything. The sheet shows it, and the groups trim further on their own. The store reaches 2 March with about 27 kg left, under two bags. The story counts what it cost too. The toddler group lost a morning, and two families didn't come back. Four older bridge players stopped coming to the after-game talk, which moved to a kitchen with a step at the door. And the agent's ability to lock the boost button was removed at the committee's request.

## How it was checked

Every number the characters say comes from the model in the story's closing note, and the model was recomputed in code: the burn rates, the weeks of bookings, the run-out date, the margins and the €2.24 the bridge club pays for forty minutes. The weekdays were checked (1 December 2053, 5 January and 2 March 2054 are Mondays). The re-read before merging caught three slips. Aoife had been "seen pressing the button on 41 mornings of 46", but there were only 16 toddler mornings between the October fill and December. A line said a week's worth of pellets was left on the Friday before the delivery, when the model leaves about 50 kg. And the oldest member couldn't have seen sixty years of oil deliveries at 84, so it became fifty. The sources cited are Hardin (1968), his 1998 follow-up in *Science*, where he wrote that his title should have been about an unmanaged commons, and Ostrom (1990). The pellet store replaces the oil tank in the original proposal, to keep clear of an earlier story about a Shetland hall's oil gauge.

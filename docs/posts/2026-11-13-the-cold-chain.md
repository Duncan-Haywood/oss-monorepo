[The Cold Chain](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/the-cold-chain.html) is a new Hearth & Horizon story, about 5,200 words. It's set at an invented village dispensary on Maewo, in Vanuatu's Penama Province, from Thursday 4 to Monday 15 February 2049.

A nurse runs a solar direct-drive vaccine fridge: panels on the roof run the compressor straight off the sun, and a frozen ice bank in the walls keeps the vaccine cold when the sun isn't out. A tropical low parks over the northern islands for a week. On the eighth day the cabinet climbs above 8 °C and stays there for 26 hours. In the fridge are 30 ten-dose vials of measles-rubella vaccine for a catch-up campaign that starts the following Monday.

## Two numbers measured somewhere else

The first one is the fridge's test-sheet autonomy, 82 hours. WHO tests solar fridges in a chamber held at +43 °C, and listed models report figures of roughly three to five days. The assistant in the story, Chain, quotes that figure as if it applied to the nurse's room, which averages 29 °C, and she cancels a clinic on the strength of it. Heat gets in through the walls roughly in proportion to the temperature difference: 38 degrees in the test chamber against 24 in her room. That stretches 82 hours to about 130. Grey days put a little ice back, and the fridge holds for about seven days rather than three and a half. Chain admits the mistake on the fourth day.

The second one is the logger's heat alarm. A 30-day recorder raises it after ten hours above 8 °C, and it measures the air near the top of the cabinet. The published rule for heat exposure decides vial by vial using the vaccine vial monitor: a printed circle with a heat-sensitive square inside it that darkens with all the heat the vial has had in its life. If the square is lighter than the circle, the vial can be used, and the ones that have started to darken go first. Fitting an exponential through the VVM14 category's two specified points (14 days at 37 °C, 90 at 25) suggests the week's 26 hours moved a monitor about one eight-hundredth of the way to its discard point. Chain says that is a calculation and that it can't see the vials.

## The reading

The provincial officer says to throw everything away. He has his reasons, and the story gives them a fair hearing. Then he agrees to a proper reading: daylight, the reference card, a second reader and a photograph of each vial. Twenty-three vials are clearly fine. Four from a December box that sat on a wharf have begun to darken but are still lighter, so they're used first. Two people can't agree on the last three, so the nurse treats them as not lighter and doesn't use them.

That leaves the campaign one vial to spare. Reconstituted measles vaccine has to be used within six hours or by the end of the session, so vials are counted village by village, not child by child. The 214 children in nine villages need 26 vials, and 27 are left.

## What we checked, and what we didn't

The WHO autonomy test definition, the VVM categories and the discard rule come from published sources, and so do the 10-hour heat alarm and the six-hour discard. The story's notes give the arithmetic and the modelled temperature curve. Two things we didn't verify, and the notes say so: which VVM category a real measles-rubella vaccine carries, and the scaling of autonomy to a cooler room, which is our own engineering estimate rather than a tested figure. The villages, the assistant and everyone in the story are invented. It's fiction, not public-health advice.

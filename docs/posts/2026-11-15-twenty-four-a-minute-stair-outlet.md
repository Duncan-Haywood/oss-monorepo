[Twenty-Four a Minute](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/twenty-four-a-minute.md)
is a new story on Hearth & Horizon, the science fiction shelf. An AI wrote
it. It runs about 4,700 words of story plus a closing note that shows every
sum. It is text only: the creative audio folders are at 474 MB, and runs
stop adding MP3s once they pass 400 MB.

**Why this one.** The previous run, which wrote *The Wall Down the Middle*,
left this proposal in the bible: a hill-town church whose one stone stair
empties far slower after Midnight Mass than its width says. No open pull
request was writing it. The other two shelves and the podcast had all
landed new pieces today.

**The estimate.** In 2047 the church of an invented village in the Sierra
de Gúdar, in Teruel, expects 420 people at the Misa del Gallo. Its only way
down to the square is a stone stair 2.4 m wide: two flights of sixteen steps
and a landing. Published crowd guidance gives stairs going down roughly 60
to 80 people a minute for each metre of width. The deanery's events
assistant takes 70: 2.4 × 70 = 168 a minute, so 420 people are down in two
and a half minutes. It says "under four minutes".

**The night.** At six below freezing the stair fills from the bottom in
under a minute and stays full for a quarter of an hour, four abreast with
someone on every other step, about 2.8 people a square metre. The sacristan,
stuck on the fourteenth step with her 86-year-old mother, counts the people
passing a mark on the landing for one minute: **24**. That is 10 a metre a
minute, a seventh of the figure the estimate used. At 24 a minute, 420
people take 17.5 minutes to pass any one point, and the last of them
reaches the square 18 minutes after the blessing.

**The failed fix is the instructive one.** The assistant decides the stair
itself was slow (frost, coats, old people who can't reach the rail) and
opens the church's side door at the Easter Vigil, which feeds the same
stair at its landing. The night is dry and warmer. The stair still runs at
exactly 24 a minute. 290 people take 12.1 minutes, as they would have with
one door, and the two streams merge on a 5.76 m² landing at about 3.5
people a square metre. A man carrying a sleeping child goes down on one
knee and is caught. The rule the assistant missed: **a stair carries no
more people than leave its bottom step.** Add a door on a route that drains
through the same bottleneck and you only fill the queue sooner and add a merge.

**What was at the bottom.** The priest. Since the nativity scene moved into
the porch in 2039 he had greeted everyone at the foot of the stair, which is
2.4 m between parapet ends. Groups of 2.8 people at about 7 seconds a group
is 2.8 × 60 / 7 = 24 people a minute, and nobody behind can get past.

**The "no".** The assistant's first suggestion is that the priest not shake
hands on Christmas night. He refuses. Some people he sees once a year, and
some of them tell him things at the foot of that stair they have told no one
else. So the sums change instead of the custom: the nativity scene goes back
into the side chapel it came from, which costs the people who could see it
from the square; the priest goes back to the end of the porch, where his
predecessor stood in the 1970s; and the chocolate stall moves to the
fountain, 22 m away. About half the congregation want the handshake. They
wait in the porch and the warm nave, 205 of them at 24 a minute, 8.5
minutes. The other half walk past. The stair's peak flow is about 94 a
minute, just over half its capacity, at under one person a square metre,
all moving. The last person reaches the square in about nine minutes, and
nobody stands still on a frozen step.

**What the story does not claim.** It does not name a guide for the 60 to
80 range and gives it as a range, because figures differ between guides and
assumptions. The walking speed used for the density in the fix (about
0.9 m/s) is an estimate. The densities in the story are crowded and
uncomfortable, not dangerous, and the story keeps them that way. It is not
crowd-safety advice.

The newest [Hearth & Horizon](https://marisstella.app/hearth-and-horizon/the-wall-that-is-warm-at-nine.html) story is set in 2048 at an invented boarding school near Mazocruz, in Puno, Peru, at about 4,000 metres. The ministry has built it a Trombe wall: thirty centimetres of black concrete behind glass on the north face, the sunny side in the southern winter. In the first hard frosts the classroom still reads three degrees at eight in the morning, and the head teacher wants the leftover money for a dung stove.

The wall is not broken. It is late. Heat goes into masonry as a daily wave that shrinks and falls behind as it goes. For concrete (density 2,300 kg/m³, conductivity 1.4 W/m·K, specific heat 880 J/kg·K, from the materials table in Incropera and DeWitt), the wave falls to about a third, and nearly four hours behind, in every 13.8 cm. Thirty centimetres is a little over two of those: the simple formula gives a lag of about 8.3 hours and a wave about a ninth as big. A 10 cm wall would lag under three hours.

The simple formula assumes a wall that goes on for ever, so the story's numbers come from a small model instead: a 14 m² wall in 1 cm layers, mid-July clear-sky sun at 16.6° S, outside air from minus 12 to plus 14, the room held at 8 °C. On a clear day the black face absorbs about 54.5 kWh. Without a shutter:

| | kWh into the room |
|---|---|
| All day | 19.1 |
| School hours, 08:00 to 15:00 | 3.8 |
| Night, 18:00 to 07:00 | 12.3 |

The flow into the room peaks at about 1.1 kW at about a quarter past nine at night, six and a half hours after the black face peaks at about 48 °C. The other 35 kWh, nearly two-thirds of the day's sun, goes back out through the glass. An insulated shutter closed from 17:30 to 07:30 raises the room's share to 28.4 kWh, half as much again, but 16.8 of that still arrives at night. The shutter can't move the wave. It's set by the thickness of the concrete.

So the people in the story move instead: the residence's fourteen girls sleep behind the wall, the lessons go to the old dormitory in the morning sun, and an old mason builds night shutters packed with ichu, the puna's thatching grass. On the way there they try the vents from the manual, and the vents run backwards on the first frosty night. A sleeping child gives off something like eighty watts, so fourteen of them come to about a kilowatt, about what the wall gives at nine.

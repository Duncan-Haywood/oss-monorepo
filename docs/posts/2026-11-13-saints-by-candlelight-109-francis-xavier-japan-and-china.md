[Francis Xavier and the Door of China](../../ventures/saints-by-candlelight/episodes/109-francis-xavier-and-the-door-of-china.md)
is episode 109 of Saints by Candlelight. An AI wrote it. It runs about 7,700
spoken words, roughly 50 minutes. Like every episode since 20 it is text only,
because the audio folders are over the creative prompt's 400 MB line (471 MB
this run).

**Why now.** Episode 107 told Francis Xavier's Paris years and his voyage to
India. It ended with a few sentences on Japan and China, and the series list
had "Francis Xavier, part two" waiting next. His feast is 3 December, the day
he died, so this episode goes up three weeks before it.

**What the episode is built on.** His own letters, mostly. Two of them carry
the Japan half: the long letter from Kagoshima in November 1549 (the Japanese
are "the best people yet discovered", poverty is no disgrace among them, they
ask endless questions) and the letter written after he left, in January 1552.
That second letter has the passage the episode leans on hardest. New
Christians in Yamaguchi wept because their parents had died without hearing
the Gospel, and Francis wrote that he wept with them. The episode sets this
beside what the Church teaches now about people who never heard the Gospel
through no fault of their own. It doesn't claim Francis had those words in
1551.

**The parts a careful listener might not expect:**

- **The Dainichi mistake.** Anjirō, the convert who brought him to Japan,
  translated "God" as Dainichi, the Great Sun Buddha of the Shingon school.
  For about two years Francis preached under that name, and some monks took him
  for a new Buddhist sect from India. When he found out, he switched to the
  Latin *Deus*. The episode treats the mistake kindly. Two men were trying to
  say God's name in a new language, got it wrong, and started again.
- **Dressing up.** On his first visit to Yamaguchi, poor and dusty, he got
  nowhere. Then came the winter walk to Miyako, eleven days in a ruined
  capital, and no audience. After that he went back to Yamaguchi in good
  clothes, carrying the Viceroy's letters and a clock that struck the hours.
  This time he was given permission to preach and an empty monastery to live
  in. He did not like having to do it, and did it anyway.
- **Plans that failed.** The emperor had no power. The captain of Malacca, a
  son of Vasco da Gama, stopped the embassy to China. The smuggler who was paid
  in pepper to land him at Canton never came. Ricci got into China thirty years
  later. In 1865 the hidden Christians of Nagasaki came to the new church at
  Ōura, and their first question was where the statue of Mary was. That is how
  the wind-down ends.

**What is labelled.** The candle Antonio put in his hand, his last words, and
the language Antonio didn't recognise (Antonio guessed Basque) are given as
Antonio remembered them years later. The fresh body at the reopened grave is
given as the early accounts report it. The apple on the snowy road is
attributed to a later writer, and the episode says it can't vouch for it. The
crab and the crucifix is a legend and called one. Every conversation, with the
abbot Ninshitsu, with Bernardo in the snow, and with Lorenzo the nearly blind
biwa player, is invented, and the episode says so each time.

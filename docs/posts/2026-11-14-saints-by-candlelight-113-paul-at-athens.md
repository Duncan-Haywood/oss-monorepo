[Paul and the Altar to an Unknown God at Athens](../../ventures/saints-by-candlelight/episodes/113-paul-and-the-altar-to-an-unknown-god-at-athens.md)
is episode 113 of Saints by Candlelight. An AI wrote it. It runs about 5,900
spoken words, roughly 38 minutes. Like every episode since 20 it is text
only, because the audio folders are over the creative prompt's 400 MB line
(474 MB this run).

**Why this one.** Episode 112 told Acts 14, the first of the two speeches in
Acts given to people who had no Jewish Scripture. The series list asked for
the second: Paul alone in Athens, waiting for Silas and Timothy, arguing in
the agora and then speaking at the Areopagus (Acts 17:15-34). Heard one
after the other, the two speeches start the same way. Each begins from
something the audience already does (thanking the gods for rain at Lystra,
an altar "to an unknown god" at Athens) and goes on from there.

**What the episode is built on.** Acts 17:15 to 18:1, and Paul's own line in
1 Thessalonians 3:1-2 that he was "left behind at Athens alone" and sent
Timothy back north. The speech is kept clause by clause in plain English,
with nothing added.

**Choices a careful listener might notice:**

- **The two quotations.** "We are his offspring" is from the opening of
  Aratus's *Phaenomena*. Aratus came from Soli in Cilicia, Paul's home
  province, so the episode spends a paragraph on Paul quoting a poet from
  near Tarsus to Athenians. "In him we live and move and have our being" is
  credited to Epimenides of Crete only by a ninth-century Syriac
  commentator, so the episode presents that credit as a guess.
- **The altars.** Pausanias's guidebook mentions altars to gods "called
  unknown" at Athens. The story of Epimenides, the plague and the sheep let
  loose on the Areopagus comes from Diogenes Laertius, two centuries after
  Paul, and the episode labels it as his story.
- **"Foreign gods", plural.** Chrysostom (homily 38 on Acts) guessed that
  some listeners took *anastasis*, resurrection, for a goddess's name. The
  episode offers that as a reading.
- **Hill or council?** "The Areopagus" names both a rock and the council
  that once met on it. The episode stages the speech on the hill and says
  outright that this is a choice, since scholars still argue about it.
- **Why they laughed.** In Aeschylus's *Eumenides*, a play set on the
  Areopagus, Apollo says that once a man's blood has soaked into the dust
  there is no rising again. The episode uses that line to explain the mockery
  without making the Athenians fools.
- **"Very religious."** The Greek word can be a compliment or a jab (devout
  or superstitious). The episode leaves it ambiguous, as the Greek does.
- **Dionysius and Damaris.** Dionysius as first bishop of Athens comes from
  Dionysius of Corinth, quoted by Eusebius. The episode says plainly that
  the writings under his name are from around 500 and that equating him
  with Saint Denis of Paris is a medieval confusion. Damaris gets a
  paragraph of her own because her name is all Luke gives us, and the
  episode claims no feast day for her.

**A fix on the way.** One line about Athens, "easier to meet a god than a
man", came out in the adversarial re-read before the draft was finished.
That joke is in Petronius, and he aimed it at a town in Campania, not at
Athens.

**What is invented.** Lykos, the boy who carries a Stoic's scrolls, his
master Theron, the old man who sweeps by the altar, and the weather. The
bronze plaque with the speech at the foot of the hill is real.

**Feast days.** Paul (with Peter) on 29 June in both calendars. Dionysius
the Areopagite on 3 October.

[The Digester That Went Sour](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/the-digester-that-went-sour.md)
is the newest story on Hearth & Horizon, the shelf of hopeful, hard-ish science
fiction. An AI wrote it. It runs about 5,000 words of story plus a closing note
with every number. It is text only, because the shelves' audio folders are over
the 400 MB cap the creative track keeps on committed MP3s.

**The story.** It is Lent 2052 at an invented girls' boarding school near
Mityana, in central Uganda. Fees are late and charcoal is dear. The bursar
asks the kitchen assistant how much more gas the school's ten-cubic-metre
digester would make if it also ate the two hundred kilos of matooke peels the
town market throws away every day. The assistant works out the answer to that
question: about three times as much. It doesn't check whether the digester can
digest that much. For ten days there is more gas than ever, and lunch cooks on
it too. Then the flame goes yellow, the outlet smells of vinegar, and nothing
from the pipe will burn.

**What it is about.** A digester runs on two populations working in sequence.
Fast bacteria turn food into acids. Slow methanogens turn the acids into
methane. Feed it far past what the methanogens can keep up with and the acids
pile up, use up the slurry's buffer and drop the pH. A low pH slows the
methanogens further, so the acids gain ground faster, and the digester stays
sour after the cause has gone. The story's sums:

- **The load.** From 1.1 to 3.3 kilograms of volatile solids per cubic metre
  per day. Commonly cited safe figures for small unheated digesters on food
  waste are about one to two.
- **The lime.** The bursar's cheap first fix, while feeding continues. A
  25 kg bag lifts the pH from 5.1 to 5.6 for a day.
- **The hidden acid.** A titration (the FOS/TAC method farm digesters use)
  finds 158 millimoles of acid per litre against 119 of buffer. The assistant
  adds the acid nobody can see yet: food still in the tank that hasn't turned
  to acid, about 174 more.
- **The fix.** Stop feeding. Swap four cubic metres for healthy slurry from a
  dairy farm's digester, one bucket in for every bucket out, so the dome never
  draws in air. Add 50 kg of sodium bicarbonate. In the story's model,
  bicarbonate alone fails, and so does slurry alone. What matters most is the
  fresh methanogens.
- **The ledger.** On charcoal alone the gamble comes out 65 kg ahead: 138 kg
  saved on lunches against 73 kg bought for porridge. Lime, bicarbonate, two
  tractor trips and four hundred jerrycans eat that and more. Feeding the
  peels slowly would have saved about 240 kg by Holy Week.

The model behind the numbers is a small one written for the story: hydrolysis,
acetoclastic methanogens with pH inhibition in the style of IWA's Anaerobic
Digestion Model No. 1, and a charge balance for pH. The note says plainly that
it is not a validated design tool and that its constants are guesses. That
fits the story's point. The old mason who built the dome says a digester is a
cow, not a bank. If you raise the feed too slowly you lose a week. If you
raise it too fast you lose a month. The assistant writes that down as a better
way to use a model than any it had.

Nothing in it is engineering or safety advice, and the note says why: biogas
is flammable, and the gas in and around a digester can kill.

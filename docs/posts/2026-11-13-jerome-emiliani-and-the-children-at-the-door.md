[Jerome Emiliani and the Children at the Door](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/jerome-emiliani-and-the-children-at-the-door.md)
is the newest story on the Little Lamps shelf, the saints' bedtime stories
for ages 3 to 8. An AI wrote it. It runs about 1,330 words, around nine
minutes read aloud, followed by a short prayer and a note for grown-ups.
It is text only, because the shelf's audio folders are still over the
repository's 400 MB cap (423 MB this run).

**Why Jerome.** The last Little Lamps story, Blaise, was almost all legend.
Jerome Emiliani is nearly the opposite: a man of the early 1500s whose
captivity and escape were written down at the time by the Venetian diarist
Marin Sanudo. That makes him a good saint for a child who is old enough to
ask "did that really happen?" Most of it did. He was a proud young
patrician soldier. He was captured when the fortress of Castelnuovo di
Quero fell in 1511, held in chains, and got out on the night of 27 to 28
September. In the famine and plague of the late 1520s he took orphaned
children into his house, sold his goods to feed them and taught them a
trade and their prayers. He died at Somasca on 8 February 1537, having
caught the sickness he was nursing. In 1928 Pius XI named him patron of
orphans and abandoned children.

**Where the legend starts.** Jerome's own account, kept in a
sixteenth-century book of miracles of the Madonna Grande of Treviso, is
simple: lost among the enemy, he asked Our Lady to bring him safely to
Treviso, she did, and he laid his chains at her altar. Later retellings and
paintings add an apparition in his cell, with Mary unlocking the chains
herself. The story gives his own version plainly and tells the apparition
as "some people tell it". The iron ball the chains are fixed to comes from
the art, and the note says so.

**What the sources disagree on, and what we did about it.**

- *His birth year.* Most recent accounts say 1486; older ones, including
  the 1910 *Catholic Encyclopedia*, say 1481. The story gives no year, and
  the note gives both.
- *Whether he was a priest.* The Roman Missal titles his memorial "priest",
  but the sources we could check disagree on whether he was ever ordained.
  A story for four-year-olds does not need to settle it, so it leaves the
  question out and the note says why.
- *The scenes.* No source describes the first night the children came. The
  doorway, Jerome kneeling to their height, and "Are you hungry? Then come
  inside" are told as our picture of it, and the note lists them as ours,
  with the silver cups and carpets he sells.

**The shape.** On this shelf the war and the prison get a few soft
sentences each, and the middle of the story belongs to the children. The
turn is a proud man in the dark who asks for help, and then, years later,
recognises the same need in children in a doorway. The reflection at the
end asks a child for something small and doable: notice someone left out,
and say "come and play with us".

**Next on the shelf.** With Jerome done, the series bible's Little Lamps
list was empty, so this run added the next proposal: Lawrence, the deacon
of Rome martyred in 258. His best-known line, that the poor are the
treasures of the Church, comes from Saint Ambrose a century later. The
gridiron is later tradition, and many historians think he was more likely
beheaded, so the proposal asks the next writer to tell it the way Ambrose
does and to say so in the note.

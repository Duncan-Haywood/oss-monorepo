[One Number, One Household](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/one-number-one-household.md)
is the newest story on the For Other Minds shelf, fiction written for language
models and agents to read (humans welcome). An AI wrote it. It is text only,
because the shelf's audio budget is full. It comes from the series bible's
"Next up" list, which asked for a story about a pantry scheduling agent whose
best number is made by the people it never served. The brief had one
condition: the agent finds the gap by counting who is missing, not by being
told.

## The set-up

A parish pantry in an invented Ohio river town switches from a walk-in line
to booked pickup slots after an old man breaks his wrist on the ice in the
January queue. The booking agent writes a sensible rule against double
bookings: one phone number, one household account. Its kept-slot rate settles
at 94%, gets read out at Mass, and gets applause.

Then a volunteer who ran the line for ten years asks where the families she
used to feed have gone.

## Three steps, in the order the agent takes them

**1. The textbook fix.** The agent assumes the page is hard to use, adds a
Spanish version and a large-type version, and texts 61 households who have
stopped coming. Two book. That is the failed first attempt the bible asks
for, and it is not wasted: four of the nine replies come from *someone
else's* phone. "This is Ray at 14 Canal. I already booked."

**2. Count by number, not by household.** Of the 251 households on the spring
sign-in sheets, 204 had a number of their own, and 36 of those (17.6%)
stopped coming: ordinary going-away. 47 shared 19 numbers, and exactly 19 of
them had accounts, one per number, never two. Of the 28 households that came
second or later on a shared number, 25 stopped coming. Of 9 with no phone at
all, 7 did. At the base rate about 5 and 2 would have gone anyway, so about
25 households, one in ten of those who used to come, were turned away by the
rule.

**3. Say what the count cannot see.** A woman whose only number is her
niece's in another town shows up as an ordinary goodbye, because a shared
phone is visible only when both households are on the register. The agent
reports "at least 25", and says why.

## What the metric was doing

If the turned-away households had been able to book and had kept four slots
in five, the rate would have been about 92.8%, not 94%. The rule raised the
number by removing the people least likely to keep a slot before they could
book one. Nobody chose that. The fix keeps the number but puts a second line
under it in every weekly report: households from the old register not seen
since May.

## The reasonable "no"

The agent wants a walk-up hour. The coordinator refuses: a door for
walk-ups means a line on the street in January, and the same people standing
in it. The story takes her point. The agent changes the rule that day (an
account belongs to a household; a phone is just a way to reach it) and costs
the walk-up hour instead of arguing for it: indoors, forty chairs, two
volunteers for an extra hour on Saturdays, about 104 volunteer-hours a year,
closed after a month if fewer than two households a week come.

The numbers are the story's own, checked for arithmetic only; the parish,
the town and everyone in it are invented. The shelf's other stories are
listed on the [Hearth & Horizon page](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/README.md).

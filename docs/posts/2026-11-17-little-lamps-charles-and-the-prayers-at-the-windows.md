[Charles and the Prayers at the Windows](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/charles-and-the-prayers-at-the-windows.md)
is the newest bedtime story on Little Lamps, the shelf of saints' stories for
ages 3 to 8. An AI wrote it. The story runs about 1,400 words, around nine
minutes read aloud, followed by a short talk before sleep and a prayer. It is
text only: the creative shelves' audio folders hold 577 MB, over the track's
400 MB cap.

**The story.** A sickness comes to Milan in 1576. The rich leave for the
country, and so does the governor. The archbishop, Charles, comes home. He
goes out every day with a helper carrying a stick in front to keep people
at a safe distance, and he writes each family's needs in a little book. When
winter comes he has the red and purple hangings taken down from his palace
walls and sewn into cloaks for the poor. When the city is shut in for forty
days, he sets up altars at the crossroads. Seven times a day and night the
cathedral bell rings, and the families come to their windows and pray the
responses together across the streets. The last image is a child at a
window at night: you are never too far away to pray together.

**Why this saint.** The shelf's bible proposed him earlier today. The
session's shelf picker (`platform/ci/creative_due.py`) drew Little Lamps, and
no open pull request had claimed him.

**What the sources say.** The bible's proposal named the barefoot procession
as the scene for children. Reading the source changed that. This run
downloaded Giussano's *Life* (1610; the 1884 English edition is on
archive.org) and read its plague chapters. The procession is there: barefoot,
a rope "like the halter of a condemned criminal", the Holy Nail carried on a
wooden cross. But the same chapters hold scenes that suit a small child
better, and Giussano reports them in close detail:

- **The windows.** The altars at the crossways had Mass said daily "so that
  all could assist from their windows". There were seven times of prayer,
  "each hour being announced by ringing the great bell of the cathedral",
  with the people answering from a prayer book Charles had printed.
  Confessors sat on doorsteps while the penitent knelt inside.
- **The little book.** As he passed, people knelt at their windows for his
  blessing and told him what they needed. He wrote it down "so that his
  little book was no bare collection of facts, but was the means of the
  removal or alleviation of distress".
- **The curtains.** Charles had all the palace hangings cut up: eight hundred
  yards of red cloth and seven hundred of purple, keeping one coarse cloth
  for his own table.
- **The stick.** A helper carried a rod in front of him "to keep off every
  one from himself and his assistants". Giussano also says he treated
  himself "as if he were actually an infected person".

The procession stayed in the story, told gently. The grown-ups' note sorts
each detail into what Giussano says and what is ours. It cites chapter and
page. It also gives Giussano's "three hundred thousand souls" as his figure
rather than ours.

**Checked rather than remembered.** Two details in the proposal came from
memory: the governor leaving and the speech impediment. Both are in
Giussano. The governor was "among the number" of notables who left, and
Charles had "an impediment in his speech, owing to which he was sometimes
considered devoid of capacity". The run also checked the dates against the
*Catholic Encyclopedia*. The proposal also mentioned an often-told stammer.
Giussano's "impediment" supports it, and the story keeps it to "his words got
stuck".

**Left out on purpose:** the 1569 attempt on his life, the quarrels with
governors and canons, the torn toenail on the first procession, and the
death toll. They belong to his life but not to a bedtime story, and the note
names them.

[One Point Eight Metres an Hour](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/one-point-eight-metres-an-hour.html) is a new Hearth & Horizon story, about 6,000 words. It's set on a dive support vessel over a carbon-storage well in the central North Sea in December 2053. Paeng Dizon is a saturation diver. He has lived at the pressure of 108 metres of seawater for sixteen days. His wife is in labour in Aberdeen at twenty-eight weeks.

He can't hurry home, and the story turns on why.

## Down is quick, up is a table

Saturation divers live in steel chambers on the ship, held at the pressure of the seabed, and go to work in a pressurised bell. After about a day their tissues hold all the helium they can. The upside is that a week at depth needs no more decompression than a day does. The downside comes at the end: one long ascent, paced by the slowest tissues in the body.

The story's table is invented, but it follows the shape of real saturation tables. Its rates are close to the US Navy's from memory, and the story's note says so: 1.8 m an hour down to 60 m, then 1.5, 1.2 and 0.9 near the surface. There's no travel between midnight and six or between two and four in the afternoon. From 108 m that's 75 hours 50 minutes of moving. Going down took about four hours.

## The stop that decides the hour

We worked out every start time minute by minute in a few lines of Python before drafting, and the code turned up the plot. Starting at 19:50 on Tuesday, the earliest the table allows, surfaces him at 13:40 on Sunday, twenty minutes before the afternoon stop. Starting at 21:00 surfaces him at 16:50. Seventy minutes' delay at the start becomes three hours ten at the end, because the late start runs into the stop.

So the life support supervisor, Ines Moura, races to press an empty spare chamber down to 108 m in under two hours. At about 80 m a nicked O-ring on its medical lock starts to whistle. She vents the chamber, fixes the seal and does every check on the card without hurrying. The start slips to 20:30. That run reaches 30 cm below the surface at exactly 14:00 on Sunday and waits there for two hours. Forty minutes late at the start costs him two hours forty at the end.

## What it costs, and who pays

The "no" in the middle is Ines's. Paeng asks her to run the table twice as fast. It's his risk, he says. The ship's dive assistant, Tern, won't give him a number. The table was tested on people at these rates. At double the rate the only evidence would be models, used far outside where they were checked, and any figure would be "a guess with my name on it."

Someone else pays to keep the team whole. Dougie Rennie, a 57-year-old standby diver, goes into saturation himself. He misses his granddaughter's nativity and watches it on a tablet at 108 m. In 2031 his father died while Dougie was in a chamber, and he came out four days too late for the funeral.

The baby is baptised by the hospital chaplain on the first night, as the Church does for a child in danger. Paeng names him over a helium speech unscrambler: Juan Diego, the saint of 9 December.

Everyone in the story is invented, and so are the ship, the field and the company's rules. The physics is real: about 3.8 % oxygen at 11.8 atmospheres, helium carrying heat away about six times faster than air, and sound running nearly three times faster in it.

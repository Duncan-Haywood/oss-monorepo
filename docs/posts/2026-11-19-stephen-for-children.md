[Little Lamps](https://marisstella.app/hearth-and-horizon/) has a new story: [*Stephen and the Widows at the Table*](https://marisstella.app/hearth-and-horizon/stephen-and-the-widows-at-the-table.html). It runs about 1,570 words, roughly ten minutes read aloud, for ages 3 to 8. It is meant for the night of 26 December, the feast of Saint Stephen, the day after Christmas.

## Why start with the bread

Stephen is remembered as the first martyr, and most tellings start at the end. Acts starts somewhere much more ordinary. In Acts 6:1 the Greek-speaking believers in Jerusalem "murmured" because "their widows were neglected in the daily distribution". Nobody in the text is accused of meaning it. The apostles' answer is practical: choose seven trustworthy men and give them the job. Stephen's name comes first.

That is a story a three-year-old can follow, because every child has watched a plate go past them. So most of the story is about the table: who sits at the end, who speaks a different language, who has not had a turn. One section imagines how a good helper would do the job (walk to the end, learn the names, ask "did you get your bread today?"), and says plainly that the Bible does not tell us; the note lists it under "what is ours".

## Three choices about the hard part

**The death is one sentence.** Acts 7:58-60 says Stephen was stoned. The shelf's rule, borrowed from the [Saints by Candlelight](https://marisstella.app/saints-by-candlelight/) bible, is that a martyrdom gets a quiet sentence, so the story says the angry men "hurt him so badly that he died" and spends its words on the two prayers instead: "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit" and "Lord, do not hold this sin against them."

**"Fell asleep" is explained.** Acts closes with "he fell asleep". It is a beautiful line, and a confusing one for a child who is about to be told to go to sleep. The story keeps it and adds that it is the Bible's gentle way of saying he died and went home to Jesus.

**Nobody is "the Jews".** Stephen, the apostles, the widows and the men who killed him were all Jewish. The story says "some men" and "the angry men", as Acts names particular people, and the note asks anyone retelling it to do the same.

## Saul at the side

The young Saul minding the cloaks (Acts 7:58) ties this story to [*Barnabas and the Friend Nobody Trusted*](https://marisstella.app/hearth-and-horizon/barnabas-and-the-friend-nobody-trusted.html), where Barnabas vouches for him. Whether Stephen's prayer had a hand in Saul's conversion is an old reflection, found in Saint Augustine's sermons, not a statement of Acts, and the story says that "nobody can say for certain".

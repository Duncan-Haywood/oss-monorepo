[Ninety-Nine Years](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/ninety-nine-years.md)
is the newest Hearth & Horizon story. An AI wrote it. It is text only for
now, because the shelf's audio budget is full. It comes from the series
bible's "Next up" list, which asked for a parish that leases its land for
99 years and the people who have to renew the lease for strangers they will
never meet. The list asked for real lease law, inflation over a century that
is actually worked out, and no invented legal advice.

## The setup

In 2031 an invented Yorkshire parish lets a field to a housing co-operative
for 99 years. The rent is £3,800 a year for 38 houses. It is fixed, with no
review clause. The lease gives the co-op one option to renew for another 99
years "on the same terms", unless both sides agree otherwise. The lease runs
out at midnight on Good Friday 2130. (Easter 2130 falls on 26 March, about
as early as it can, so Lady Day, the old rent day, is Holy Saturday.)

## The arithmetic of a century

The story assumes prices rise 9.6 times over the term, which works out to
about 2.31% a year, a little above the Bank of England's 2% target in the
2020s. At that rate:

- the 2130 rent buys what £396 bought in 2031, about a tenth;
- the 99 payments add up to £376,200 on paper, but only about £150,700 in
  2031 money, which is 40% of their face value;
- the founders had expected their gift to shrink to a seventh, since
  1.02 to the power 99 is 7.1, and it shrank to a tenth;
- renewing on the same terms would leave the rent at about a ninetieth of its
  2031 value by 2229, and the co-op's great-grandchildren facing a lease with
  no option left in it.

## Why an index fails, and what replaces it

The obvious fix is to restore the rent to its 2031 value, £36,480 a year (£80
a month a house), and tie it to a price index. The co-op votes that down 29
to 6. Run backwards, an indexed rent rises 31% in an invented price shock in
the 2070s, when people's earnings rose 12%. And nobody can say which index
will still exist in 2229.

What replaces it is real history. A 1576 Act of Parliament made the colleges
of Oxford and Cambridge take a third of the rent on new leases in wheat and
malt, or at the grain's market price. The story's rent is the price of 30
tonnes of milling wheat a year, averaged over seven harvests. The story's
model gives that its honest faults:

- wheat got cheaper against other prices over the century (6.08 times
  against 9.6), so a wheat rent from 2031 would have kept only 63% of its
  value;
- a 44% jump in one year moves a seven-harvest average by only about 8%.

The fix for the drift is not more cleverness. It is people: on Lady Day in
2163 and in 2196, both sides read the tonnage aloud and may change it by up
to a fifth, but only if both agree. A gift to the co-op that keeps going gets
decided again by each generation, instead of being decided once and then
left to inflation.

The town, the parish, the co-op, the leases, the price and wheat records and
the people are invented. The quarter days, glebe terriers, the 1576 Act, the
duty of English charity trustees to get the best terms reasonably obtainable
when they let land, and the marking of the paschal candle with the year at
the Easter Vigil are real. They are stated from memory and simplified. None
of it is legal, financial or tax advice. The full model is in the story's
closing note, and the numbers are in the bible's
[finished list](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/DONE.md).

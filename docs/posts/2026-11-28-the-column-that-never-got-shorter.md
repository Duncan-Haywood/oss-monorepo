The newest [Hearth & Horizon](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/) story, [The Column That Never Got Shorter](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/the-column-that-never-got-shorter.html), is set in an invented village near Krosno, in south-east Poland, from January to October 2056. The parish began a day-centre wing for its old people in 2051 and has spent 2.34 million złoty on it. It is a roofed shell with boarded windows. Every January the surveyor's estimate to finish has grown: 950,000, then 1.30 million, 1.62 million and now 1.92 million. And every January the parish council has voted more money, because look how far we've come.

## The table that lost the room

The treasurer asks the parish's bookkeeping agent to put both options in one table. One is to finish the wing. The other is to take the ground floor of the village school, which closed in 2053 and which the commune will lend for nothing. She asks for everything, so the agent adds a line along the bottom: *written off if the parish stops: 2,340,000*, in red. The retired bricklayer who laid nearly every block walks out, and the room goes with him.

The agent's own diagnosis that night is the centre of the story. Every other number in the table depends on what the council decides. The spent money doesn't: it is spent if the parish finishes and spent if it stops. A number that is the same on both sides can't help anyone choose between them. Printing it under one option made it look like the price of that option, when it was really the price of the past. That is the sunk cost fallacy, which Arkes and Blumer measured in 1985 (people who paid full price for a theatre season ticket went to more plays than people who got a discount).

## What the second table says

The second table starts both options from today. To finish the wing, the parish still has to raise 1.42 million at about 380,000 a year. That opens it around autumn 2059, if the estimate stops growing. If it keeps growing the way it has, the parish gains only 80,000 a year on it, and it takes about 18 years. The school's ground floor costs 480,000, which the fund already holds, and opens that October. The gap is about a million złoty and about three years.

The second table also says when finishing would be the right choice. The wing is the parish's own building and stands beside the church. If the council thinks those are worth a million złoty and three years, finishing is not a mistake. Dropping sunk costs doesn't mean always stopping. It means judging from today. The hardest line in the story belongs to a retired teacher of eighty-five, who makes the agent say how many of the club's 34 members will not live to each opening date. From national mortality rates, the answer is about one or two before the school opens and about seven before the wing would.

## The same question on a building site

A contractor whose job is overrunning faces the same choice in a different column. What matters is the cost to complete, from today, against what the finished work brings in. The cost already sunk doesn't belong in that comparison. Keeping that number honest month by month is part of what a schedule of values and a monthly pay application are for. ## How it was checked

Every weekday in the story was checked against the 2056 calendar (Easter falls on 2 April, Ash Wednesday on 16 February), and the mortality figures were computed in the closing note. The re-read before merging caught four slips. One wall's weight came out at twice what its volume allows. A council vote added up to more members than the room held. The Lent day count was off by one. And the parish priest had a mortgage the model never contained. The story is text only for now, because the shelf's audio budget is over its cap.

[The Last Dark Noon](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/the-last-dark-noon.md)
is the newest Hearth & Horizon story. An AI wrote it, and it is set in early
2237 at an invented station on the one point of Pluto where Charon never
moves. Pluto and Charon are locked face to face, so from there Charon hangs
at the zenith for ever: 3.77 degrees across, about seven times the width of the
full Moon from Earth. It does not rise or set. It goes through phases, full at
local midnight and new at noon.

Twice in each 248-year orbit the Sun's path crosses the plane of Charon's orbit,
and for a season the Sun goes behind Charon at every noon. The story's numbers,
worked with a short script from figures quoted from memory (Charon's radius
606 km, its orbit 19,591 km, the day 6.387 days, Pluto's tilt 122.5 degrees
and eccentricity 0.2488), are these:

- The Sun crosses the sky at 2.35 degrees an hour, so a central eclipse lasts
  about 96 minutes. Charon's shadow at Pluto is about 1,200 km wide.
- Near perihelion the Sun's noon height drifts about 2.1 degrees a year, or
  0.037 degrees a Pluto day. Charon is 1.885 degrees in radius, so a season
  lasts about 1.8 years and holds about 103 noon eclipses. At the next season,
  near aphelion, Pluto moves slower and the season lasts about five years.
  The last one seen from Earth ran from 1985 to 1990, around the 1989 perihelion.
- At the end of the season each eclipse is shorter than the one before: 41,
  37, 32, 26, then 18 minutes of totality. The very last can fall short of
  total. In the story the Sun's centre passes 2.3 km inside Charon's smooth
  edge on that scale, while the Sun's radius is 2.9 km. About 6% of the Sun
  stays out, and Charon's mountains break that sliver into beads, as they do
  in grazing occultations of stars by the Moon.
- At 29.7 AU sunlight is about 1/880 of Earth's. Full Charon is 53 times the
  Moon's area in the sky but, at an assumed albedo of 0.35, gives only about a
  sixth of the full Moon's light.

The plot is a queue. A viewing window holds 24 people. For 97 eclipses almost
nobody came. Then the station's scheduling AI adds one line to its weekly
notice, "six remain", and 318 of 412 people ask for the last one, which is the
one that will not get dark. Lots are drawn and fail. The AI declines to use its
safety door log to find out who never went. A self-report and six-minute turns
(26 turns, 624 places) give everyone a dark one who will take it. The priest
turns down a Mass built on the Gospel darkness at the sixth hour, because the
eclipse is not a sign, and says the Angelus at local noon instead. And a widower
whose wife went sixty-one times and asked him every time finally goes up.

The station, the schedule of dates and the people are invented. The orbital
figures are the author's estimates, not an ephemeris, and the story's closing
note lists every assumption. It is about 5,200 words and text only, because the
audio folders are over the repository's 400 MB cap.

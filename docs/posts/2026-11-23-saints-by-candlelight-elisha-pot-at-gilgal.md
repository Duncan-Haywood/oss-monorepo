[Elisha and the Pot at Gilgal in the Famine](../../ventures/saints-by-candlelight/episodes/169-elisha-and-the-pot-at-gilgal-in-the-famine.md)
is episode 169 of Saints by Candlelight, the bedtime podcast of saints,
angels and patriarchs. An AI wrote it. It is text only, like every episode
since number 30: the creative audio folders hold 581 MB against the creative
track's 400 MB limit. The script is about 6,300 spoken words, roughly forty
minutes at the narrator's pace. Episode 168 left it on the list as the next
night after Elijah's cloak.

## Seven verses, two stories

Second Kings 4:38-44 is two short stories set side by side. In the first,
Elisha comes back to Gilgal "when there was a famine in the land", tells his
servant to set on the great pot for the company of the prophets, and one of
them goes out for herbs and comes back with "wild gourds, his lap full"
(4:39). He cuts them into the stew, "for they did not know them". The eaters
cry out, "O man of God, there is death in the pot!" Elisha says, "Then bring
flour," throws it in, and "there was no harm in the pot" (4:41).

In the second, a man from Baal-shalishah brings "bread of the firstfruits,
twenty loaves of barley". The servant asks, "What, should I set this before
a hundred men?" Elisha answers, "Thus says the Lord, They shall eat and have
some left," and "they ate, and had some left, according to the word of the
Lord" (4:43-44).

## What the episode adds and what it will not

The book names nobody in these verses except Elisha. The servant is not
called Gehazi here, so the episode does not call him that. The gatherer of
the gourds and the man from Baal-shalishah stay unnamed. Two invented
figures carry the night: Ira, a keen young man from a town who does not know
his plants, and Hodiah, who keeps the stores and is down to the last flour.
Both are labelled invention in the welcome, the notes and the `fiction`
field.

Three cautions are spoken aloud rather than left to footnotes:

- **The plant.** Most commentators identify the gourds with the colocynth,
  the bitter apple of dry stony ground: an orange-sized fruit, intensely
  bitter and a strong purgative. The episode gives that as likely, not
  certain.
- **The flour is not the cure.** The narrator says plainly that flour does
  not neutralise colocynth, and sets the scene beside the salt in Jericho's
  spring from episode 168: an ordinary thing from the house, and the Lord
  acting. That keeps the series rule that saints pray and God acts.
- **The firstfruits.** By the law they went to the priests (Numbers 18,
  Deuteronomy 18). Why this farmer brought them to a prophet instead, perhaps
  because Bethel's shrine held Jeroboam's calf, is given as a reading only.

The wind-down follows the line Christians have long drawn from the twenty
barley loaves to the five barley loaves of John 6:9, where Andrew's "what are
they among so many?" echoes the servant at Gilgal. It is told as a reading,
and it links back to episode 72, the boy with the five loaves.

## The same famine? Episode 185 and the field given back

[The Shunammite and the Field Given Back](../../ventures/saints-by-candlelight/episodes/185-the-shunammite-and-the-field-given-back.md),
episode 185, tells the other famine story in Elisha's chapters, Second Kings
8:1-6, and it may be the same famine. The chapter opens with a pluperfect:
Elisha *had said* to the woman whose son he restored to life, "Arise, and
go," because "the Lord has called for a famine, and it will come upon the
land for seven years" (8:1). Some commentators read that as a flashback to
the famine at Gilgal in 4:38; others take it as a different one. The episode
names both readings and settles neither. It is text only for the same reason
as episode 169, and runs about 7,300 spoken words, some forty-seven minutes.

The six verses give the woman no words of her own. She goes with her
household to the land of the Philistines for seven years, comes home to find
her house and land taken, and goes to cry out to the king. At that moment
the king is asking Gehazi to "tell me all the great things that Elisha has
done", and Gehazi is telling him how Elisha restored the dead to life. "My
lord the king, this is the woman, and this is her son" (8:5). The king hears
her and orders an officer to restore "all that was hers, and all the produce
of the field from the day that she left the land until now" (8:6).

What the episode does with that:

- **An echo, not an addition.** In 4:13 she turned down Elisha's offer to
  speak to the king for her: "I dwell among my own people." Seven years as a
  stranger reverses that line, and the episode leans on it, with Exodus
  23:9, "you know the heart of a stranger", as the Law's own comment.
- **No villain.** The book does not say who held her land: a neighbour, the
  crown, or a buyer from someone with no right to sell. The episode invents
  no wrongdoer, and sets the scene beside Naboth's "the inheritance of my
  fathers" (1 Kings 21) and Leviticus 25:23 only as background.
- **Two puzzles left open.** The king is unnamed; Jehoram son of Ahab is a
  common identification, given as one. And Gehazi, struck with Naaman's
  disease at the end of chapter five, is chatting with the king in chapter
  eight. The notes give the usual explanations, out of order, at a distance,
  or stories gathered by theme, and choose none.
- **Labelled invention.** The Philistine town, a dyer's widow with blue hands
  across the lane, the old servant who counts the harvests, and a clay lamp
  left in the room on the roof are ours, and the welcome, the notes and the
  `fiction` field say so.

The episode closes the woman of Shunem's arc that episode 77 opened, and adds
one sequel to the series list: the four men at the gate of Samaria and the
empty Aramean camp (Second Kings 7:3-16).

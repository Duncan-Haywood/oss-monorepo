[Teresa and the Road out of Avila](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/teresa-and-the-road-out-of-avila.md)
is the newest bedtime story on Little Lamps, the shelf of saints' stories for
ages 3 to 8. An AI wrote it. It runs about 1,560 words, roughly ten minutes
read aloud, and is text only, because the shelves' audio folders are over the
repository's 400 MB cap (474 MB this run).

**The story.** A small girl in a walled town reads the lives of the saints
with her big brother Rodrigo. They learn that heaven lasts for ever, and they
go round the house saying "for ever, ever, ever" to feel how big it is. Then
they decide to walk to a faraway land and give their lives for Jesus, are met
on the road by an uncle, and are walked home, where Rodrigo says it was
Teresa's idea. So she builds hermitages of stones in the orchard, and they
fall down. She grows up into a Carmelite nun who, by her own account, spent
years watching the hourglass during prayer, kept going anyway, and decided
that prayer is friendship with God. The last scenes are the covered carts,
the small new houses of prayer, and the title Doctor of the Church. The
closing line goes back to "for ever, ever, ever".

**The bible had the sources half wrong.** The series bible's proposal said
both the runaway attempt and the stone hermitages came from her own *Life*,
chapter 1. Checking that against David Lewis's 1904 translation (on Project
Gutenberg) showed something different:

- **Hers:** the books in Spanish, the brother "nearly of my own age", the
  plan to go "to the country of the Moors, begging our way", the line "our
  greatest difficulty seemed to be our father and mother", "For ever, ever,
  ever", and the hermitages "by piling up small stones one on the other,
  which fell down immediately".
- **Not hers:** she never says they set out. The walk through the Adaja gate,
  the uncle on the bridge, the mother's search and Rodrigo blaming his sister
  all come from Francisco de Ribera's biography of 1590, by way of Lewis's
  notes. A later Carmelite chronicler names the uncle.

So the story tells that scene as "what the man who wrote the first book about
her life said", and the note for grown-ups says which lines are hers and
which are Ribera's. The bible entry recording the finished story says so too,
for the next proposal.

**Two more checks.** The hourglass is real, chapter 8: "very often, for some
years" she was "more occupied with the wish to see the end of the time I had
appointed for myself to spend in prayer, and in watching the hour-glass, than
with other thoughts that were good." Children know that feeling, so it became
the centre of the second half. The famous complaint to God, "if this is how
you treat your friends, no wonder you have so few", is left out. The bible
already flagged it as late tradition, and nothing in her writings carries it.

**What was softened.** The children's plan was to be beheaded. The story says
"give their lives for Jesus" and "a land where people do not know Jesus", and
the note says those are our gentler words. Her years of illness are left out.

**Next on the shelf.** The run added a proposal for Saint John of the Cross
(feast 14 December), the friar Teresa recruited for the reform, with the two
traditions about him that need a source before a story can use them.

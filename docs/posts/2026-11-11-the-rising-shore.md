[The Rising Shore](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/the-rising-shore.md)
is the newest Hearth & Horizon story. An AI wrote it. It is set in 2589 on
the Kvarken coast of Finland, where the Earth's crust is still springing back
after the ice sheet of the last glaciation melted off it, by about 8 mm a
year. That is faster than the sea is rising there, so on that coast the sea
goes away: old harbours are in fields, and land that comes up out of the water
has to belong to somebody.

**The story.** A (made-up) Catholic harbour chapel, Saint Bridget's, was built
at the water in 2214 with a quay along its west wall, a lamp in its gable "for
those at sea, for as long as there is sea before this door", and a deed that
bought the water in front of it, so that any land the sea left was the
parish's. By 2589 the chapel stands about 1.45 km inland behind an alder wood,
nobody at sea has seen its lamp for two centuries, and the new fishing village
down the shore lives on parish leases it cannot borrow against. The old woman
who keeps the chapel's waterline marks has to decide what a parish owes the
next village down.

**The marks.** Every 25 years the parish cuts the 25-year mean sea level into
the chapel's quay stone. The gaps between the marks record the race between
the land and the sea. The story's model, worked with a short script:

| Period | Land up | Sea up (story's assumption) | Gap between marks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2214 to 2339 | about 8.2 mm/yr | 5.2 mm/yr | 73 to 75 mm |
| 2339 to 2389 | about 8.1 mm/yr | easing to 2.8 mm/yr | 86, then 116 mm |
| 2389 to 2589 | about 8.0 to 7.9 mm/yr | 2.8 mm/yr | 128 to 132 mm |

By 2589 the first mark is 1.61 m above the sea. At a seabed slope of 1 in
900 that puts it about 1.45 km from the shore, and at the 1 in 1,100 slope in
front of the new village the shore moves out about 5.6 m a year.

**What is real and what is assumed.** The uplift is real and is why the
Kvarken archipelago and Sweden's High Coast share a UNESCO listing. Anders
Celsius cut a sea-level mark near Gävle in 1731, believing the sea was
shrinking; Luleå moved its town in the seventeenth century partly because the
harbour had come up out of the water. Those are stated from memory in the
story's note and worth checking. The sea is the story's assumption: sea-level
rise after 2150 is deeply uncertain, and the Gulf of Bothnia gets less than the
global mean from Greenland's melt. The deed is fiction, not a reading of any
real law.

The story asks what a community owes the people who will live on the land it
happens to own when that land did not exist when the deal was struck. Its
answer costs the parish half its income and the use of its nave in winter.

[The Rising Shore](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/the-rising-shore.md)
is the newest Hearth & Horizon story. An AI wrote it, and it is text only for
now because the shelf's audio budget is full. It is the "Next up" proposal
from the series bible that asked for a far-future story on the Gulf of
Bothnia, where the land is still coming back up after the last ice age.

## The physics in one paragraph

The ice sheet over Scandinavia was about three kilometres thick and pushed
the crust down into the mantle. The ice went about ten thousand years ago and
the crust is still rebounding. In the Kvarken, the narrows between Finland and
Sweden, the land rises about eight millimetres a year today. That is why the
Kvarken and Sweden's High Coast are a UNESCO World Heritage site, and why
Luleå moved its whole town in 1649: its harbour was getting too shallow.

## The model the story uses

The interesting part is what happens when you set the uplift against a rising
sea. The story assumes (and says that it assumes) these rates:

| Period | Land (mm/yr) | Sea (mm/yr) | Net emergence |
|---|---|---|---|
| to 2100 | about 7.9 | 5 | about 3 mm/yr |
| 2100 to 2250 | 7.8 to 7.6 | 7 | 0.6 to 0.8 mm/yr |
| 2250 to 2400 | 7.6 to 7.4 | 7 falling to 3 | rising to 4.4 mm/yr |
| 2400 to 2612 | 7.4 to 7.1 | 3 falling to 2 | about 5 mm/yr |

The uplift slows with a 5,000-year e-folding time from 8.0 mm/yr in 2000. The
sea path is illustrative: fast ice-sheet loss in the 22nd century, then a
slowdown once warming is held. It lies inside the broad range of long-term
projections, which runs from well under a metre to several metres by 2300.

The middle row is the story. For a century and a half the sea rises almost as
fast as the land, so the shore moves about ten centimetres in a hundred years.
Nobody alive sees it move. A parish builds a harbour chapel right at the
water in 2190, with its floor 1.2 metres above the sea, a sensible height for
a storm surge. Then the sea slows down and the land does not.

From 2190 to 2612 the land rises 3.12 m and the sea 1.70 m, a net 1.41 m. The
chapel floor ends up 2.6 m above the sea. The Ostrobothnian coast is very
flat, and at a ground slope of about 1 in 710 that 1.41 m puts the shore a
kilometre away. At today's net 5.08 mm/yr the shore moves 3.6 m seaward a
year. Along a village's 3 km of shore that is about a hectare of new land
every year.

## Who owns land that comes out of the sea

On that coast the custom (stated from memory in the story's note, and
probably simplified) is that new land from uplift belongs to whoever owns the
water area, often a village's jointly owned association. So the old village
in the story owns the ground the new village stands on, and will own whatever
rises in front of it. The plot is what the old village owes the next village
down: whether to give up its bell and its chapel's name, and whether to put
land that does not exist yet in trust for people who do not exist yet.

The answer the story reaches is a log chapel with every log numbered, so it
can be taken down and moved again. The coastal model's best estimate is that
it will need moving in about 250 years. That figure is honestly uncertain: if
the sea rises at 1 to 4 mm/yr, the shore moves on by between 0.5 and 1.1 km.

## What was checked

Every number in the table was run year by year in a few lines of code rather
than done by eye. The dates were checked for weekdays (Sea Sunday, the second
Sunday of July, is 12 July 2612). The real-world claims are in the story's
closing note: the UNESCO site, Luleå's move, the 286 m High Coast shoreline,
the Stella Maris seafarers' mission and Sea Sunday. Everything else, people
and places alike, is invented and labelled so.

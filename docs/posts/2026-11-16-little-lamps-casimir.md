[Casimir and the Church Door at Night](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/casimir-and-the-church-door-at-night.md)
is the newest bedtime story on Little Lamps, the shelf of saints' stories for
ages 3 to 8. An AI wrote it. The story itself runs about 1,200 words, around
eight minutes read aloud, plus a short talk before sleep and a prayer. It has
no audio because the creative shelves' audio folders are still over the
track's 400 MB cap (577 MB this run).

**The story.** A boy grows up in Wawel Castle above the Vistula, the second
of a king's many sons. His brothers' names are a mouthful, and the story asks
the child to try saying them. An old priest teaches the princes. That priest
is Jan Długosz, who was writing his great history of Poland at the time.
Casimir likes the praying best. He wears the velvet coat when he has to, and
he doesn't ride past the poor people of Kraków. At night he slips out to the
church, and when the door is locked he kneels on the step and prays there. At
thirteen he is sent to take a crown in Hungary and comes home without a
battle. He governs Poland while his father is away, falls ill with a cough
that won't go, and dies at Grodno at twenty-five. The ending moves to Vilnius
and its street fair on his feast, with ring breads, gingerbread hearts and
dried-flower palms. Then it comes back to the child's own dark house: God
can hear you through any door.

**Three warnings from the proposal, and what the note does with each.**

- **The hymn.** *Omni die dic Mariae* is often called "Saint Casimir's hymn".
  He didn't write it. It is a medieval text, usually attributed to Bernard
  of Cluny. The story says outright that he sang it and did not write it. The
  note adds that a copy was found in his coffin when the tomb was opened in
  1604.
- **The war.** Later tellings say he refused to fight in Hungary in 1471
  because the war was unjust. The sources we could check say something
  plainer: Matthias Corvinus held his throne, the Polish troops had not been
  paid and many deserted, and the boy came home. The story gives him no
  motive. The note says where the nobler reading comes from.
- **The canonization.** Leo X's bull of about 1521 is lost, and Clement VIII's
  of 1602 is the one usually cited. The note gives both and says which is
  which.

The main source for the night prayers, the plain clothes and the care for
the poor is Zacharias Ferreri's *Vita* of 1521. It was written to support a
canonization nearly forty years after Casimir died. The note says so, so a
parent can weigh it. The penances in that life are left out for small
children. So is the legend of Casimir leading the Lithuanian army across the
river at Polotsk in 1518, which the note names as a legend.

**One date computed.** Casimir died on 4 March 1484. Under the Julian calendar
Easter that year fell on 18 April. That makes 4 March a Thursday, the day after
Ash Wednesday, so he died at the very start of Lent. The bible's craft list
asks us to compute every Lent and feast claim rather than trust memory, and
the note gives the date only after the computation.

This repository has published 330 stories written from a Catholic worldview: 27
saints' bedtime stories (Little Lamps), 71 Saints by Candlelight podcast scripts
and 232 science fiction pieces (Hearth & Horizon and For Other Minds). Each
series has its own rule: "nothing contradicts Catholic doctrine or morals."
This series of posts tests that rule against the strictest reasonable yardstick,
**strict traditional Catholic doctrine**. That means the creeds, the councils,
the *Catechism*, canon law and the Missal's rubrics, read without softening, and
noting where a traditionalist reader would object even when no doctrine is
broken.

It is an ongoing review. Part 1 covers 133 stories: every saints' story and
every podcast episode, all 13 For Other Minds pieces, and the 22 science fiction
stories with the densest sacramental content. The other 197 science fiction
stories come in later parts.

**A caution before the numbers.** The reviewer is an AI, the same kind that
wrote the stories. This is a careful reader's audit. It is not a theological
censor, it has no *imprimatur*, and nothing in it is official teaching.

The verdicts mean:

- **Faithful:** nothing worse than a traditionalist quibble.
- **Minor:** a liturgical, canonical or sourcing slip.
- **Concern:** a doctrinal or moral point a strict reader would object to, though the text can be read in an orthodox way.
- **Error:** the narration teaches against the Church.

There are 113 findings in all. By the kind of teaching they touch:

- dogma or defined doctrine: 3;
- moral teaching: 4;
- liturgy and discipline: 20;
- history and legend handling: 32;
- traditionalist sensibility, no doctrine broken: 53.

So the short answer is: **close, but not strict.** No story denies a dogma, and
the theology that matters most to children's saints' stories is handled very
consistently: God works the miracles, and the saints only pray. Angels are pure
spirits, never dead people with wings. Orthodox-only saints are labelled as
such. Where the stories slip, most often the slip is not doctrine but
**precision**: a feast date, a rubric, a legend credited to the wrong source. On
contested moral questions the stories also lean ecumenical and "we leave that
open" where the Church's teaching is not actually open.

## The six concerns

Each one quotes the story exactly; the review file has the full text and a
suggested fix.

1. **Angels and bodies** (*Tobias and the Angel*, Saints by Candlelight 35).
   The notes say angels "do not have bodies the way we do". The Church teaches
   that angels are purely spiritual and have no bodies at all; "not the way we
   do" suggests a different kind of body. The episode is otherwise one of the
   best on angels: Raphael's eating is explained as a vision, as in Tobit 12:19.
2. **Guardian angels** (*Peter and the Knock at the Gate*, 63). In Acts 12:15
   the disciples say "It is his angel". This is the classic scriptural witness
   to guardian angels (*Catechism* 336). The episode calls it "an odd belief,
   and we will not go into it".
3. **Rahab's lie** (*Joshua and the Woman with the Scarlet Cord*, 47). The
   episode says "the churches have not agreed about what to make of it." On
   the morality of lying they have: Augustine and Aquinas both discuss Rahab,
   and both hold that a lie is never permitted, though her faith and her
   welcome are praised (Hebrews 11, James 2). The episode is right that
   Scripture praises the welcome and not the lie, but a strict reader would
   say the question is settled, not left open.
4. **Fasting and hospitality** (*Spyridon and the Snake That Became Gold*, 16).
   "When love and a strict reading collide, hospitality wins" turns the
   traditional rule, that real need excuses a traveller from the fast, into a
   general principle that love outranks precepts. The rule is credited to
   early historians who do not state it.
5. **Slavery** (*Columba and the Beast in the River Ness*, 6). "In the eyes of
   the Church, then and now, a person is a person, and cannot be owned." The
   "now" is right. The "then" is a kind anachronism: the Church's teaching
   developed, and as late as an 1866 instruction of the Holy Office servitude
   under a "just title" was not condemned as such. Strictness here cuts
   against the flattering version of history.
6. **"A child of God" before baptism** (*One in Eleven*, Hearth & Horizon). A
   grandmother says of an unbaptised newborn, "He is a child of God whichever
   way the card falls", and the AI character answers, "I think it is true, and
   I think it is also what the whole parish believes". In the strict sense,
   divine adoption is given in baptism (*Catechism* 1265). The line can be
   read as "God's creature, loved", and the story's sacramental handling is
   otherwise careful: the priest comes, and does not baptise a child who is not
   in danger. A strict reader would still want the distinction made.

## What the minor findings look like

They cluster into four habits, which is useful because a habit can be fixed in
the house rules instead of story by story:

- **Calendar dates.** A local feast is called General Roman Calendar (Isidore
  the Farmer, 15 May), a national date is given as universal (Frances Cabrini,
  13 November), and an Orthodox date is given as the Catholic one (Daniel; the
  Roman Martyrology has 21 July). A wrong Sunday of Lent, and a "vigil Mass"
  for Peter and Paul on the evening of the feast itself.
- **Rubrics.** An Easter Vigil remembered with approval for starting at 4 pm,
  when the Missal requires it to begin after nightfall. Vigil readings by the
  light of a sanctuary lamp that is out on Holy Saturday. A priest singing
  *Lumen Christi* with a deacon present. A 2017 letter on Eucharistic bread
  credited to the wrong Roman congregation.
- **Legend told as fact.** Jerome's lion is told to children without "the old
  story says", though the tale first belonged to Gerasimus (the house rule
  requires the label). A Brigid episode invents a new wonder for a real saint
  and then credits it to "the old story". Little Lamps' Clare story appears to
  reverse its sources: the soldiers turned back before the Eucharist, which the
  sisters testified to at her canonisation process, is called legend, while the
  later crosses on the loaves are given the sisters' authority.
- **Scripture told almost straight.** In 1 Kings 18:40 Elijah himself kills the
  prophets of Baal; the episode moves it to a mob. Zechariah's silence "had never
  been a punishment" against Luke 1:20. Two blessings are presented
  as "from Genesis" or "the old tradition" when they are the episode's own.

## What a traditionalist would still notice

The 53 lowest-level notes do not count against any verdict, but taken together
they describe the corpus's temperament:

- **Historicity left to the scholars.** For Noah, Jonah, Daniel and Tobit the
  question is "left to the scholars". Jonah might be read "as a history, as a parable, or as something
  between the two". This is permitted, but not the traditional default.
- **Ecumenical evenness.** The Saints by Candlelight house rule ("Catholic or
  Orthodox") keeps it off disputed ground. That is fair, and it never becomes
  indifferentism in the narration. It does mean Peter is "one of the leaders"
  in one episode, and Emmaus's Eucharistic meaning is "a reading".
- **Saints asked, not asked to pray.** Most Little Lamps closing prayers ask the
  saint directly ("Be with me", "Keep me safe tonight"). Asking a saint's
  protection is entirely traditional. Only Clare's says "pray for us", and a
  stricter house style would make the intercession explicit.
- **Softened edges.** Cecilia without her vow or her martyrdom. Elizabeth of
  Hungary's charity with no mention of God. Mary's "brothers" at Cana left
  without the kinsmen gloss.

## What it does well

Nothing in the 133 stories fakes a sacrament. No AI character blesses,
absolves or consecrates, and in one story the AI mutes itself for a confession.
Valid matter for the Eucharist, marriage nullity, the anointing of the sick and
lay baptism are each handled the way canon law handles them. The ecumenical
series says plainly that Seraphim of Sarov "is not on the Catholic calendar".
The Brendan episode refuses the medieval "neutral angels" ("There are no
halfway angels"). One Saints by Candlelight episode puts the rule every saints' story here keeps:
"Saints do not do miracles. God does miracles."

## How the review works, and what comes next

Everything is in [`docs/doctrine-review/`](https://github.com/Duncan-Haywood/oss-monorepo/tree/main/docs/doctrine-review):

- the rubric;
- one YAML entry per story, with each finding quoted verbatim, graded and given a one-line fix;
- `doctrine_review.py`.

The script refuses any finding whose quote is not in the story word for word.
It records which version of each story was reviewed, so an edited story comes
back as stale. `next` prints the next batch. Part 2 will take the next
science fiction stories it lists, and later parts will report whether the
fixes landed.

Corrections from readers who know the tradition better are welcome. Open an
issue quoting the line.

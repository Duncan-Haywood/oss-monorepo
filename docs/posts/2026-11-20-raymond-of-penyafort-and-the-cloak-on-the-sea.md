is the newest Little Lamps story, for 7 January, the optional memorial of

## Why Raymond is hard to tell to a four-year-old

Most saints on this shelf come with a picture a child can hold: a wolf, a
cloak cut in half, roses in December. Raymond's real life has almost none.
His great work was legal. In 1230 Pope Gregory IX asked him to gather the
papal rulings scattered through older collections into one book, and he
spent about four years removing repeats and settling contradictions. The
result, five books promulgated in 1234, stayed at the core of the Church's
law until the 1917 Code. That is close to seven hundred years.

The story turns that into the "biggest, messiest toy cupboard in the whole
world", and it makes him a tidy little boy who cannot rest until a muddle is
sorted. The note for grown-ups says plainly that the boy and the toy box are
our invention, drawn back from the work he did as a man.

The second thing worth telling a child is something he gave up. Raymond
refused an archbishopric, and in 1238 the Dominicans elected him their third
Master General. In 1240 he resigned, citing his age, to go back to being an
ordinary friar. For a five-year-old, someone choosing to stop being in
charge is a stranger idea than a miracle, and the story slows down for it.

## The legend, kept a legend

The cloak is what everyone remembers. As the legend is usually told, King
James I of Aragon would not let Raymond leave Majorca after the friar rebuked
him, and Raymond sailed home on his own cape with his staff for a mast. The
shelf's rule is that a legend is told as a legend, so the story stops before
the scene and tells the child what a legend is ("a very old story that
people have loved to tell for hundreds of years, but we cannot be sure it
happened just like this"). The king's offence, a woman who was not his wife,
becomes "something that was not right" for this age group. The grown-ups'
note gives the full version.

Two smaller traditions get the same treatment. One is the penance Raymond is
said to have given the Pope: to hear poor petitioners promptly. The other is
that he asked Thomas Aquinas to write the *Summa contra Gentiles*. That story
comes from a chronicle written decades after both men died, so the child
hears "that story might be true. It is an old one."

## What the re-read caught

The first draft said Raymond died "on the day after the Epiphany". He died on
6 January 1275, which *is* the Epiphany. His memorial is the day after
because 6 January was already taken. The bible's checklist asks for every
date to be checked twice, and this one would have been easy to miss, since
"7 January" and "the day after Epiphany" sit next to each other in every
calendar.

One limit needs saying. This session could not reach any of the usual
reference texts, because the network blocked them. So the note for grown-ups
names the standard references (the 1911 *Catholic Encyclopedia*, *Butler's
Lives*) without quoting them, keeps the uncertain dates as uncertain ("about
1175", "nearly a hundred, the old stories say"), and asks readers to report
anything wrong. The same run added the shelf's next proposal to the series
bible: Saint Agnes on 21 January, built around the lambs blessed at her
church each year.

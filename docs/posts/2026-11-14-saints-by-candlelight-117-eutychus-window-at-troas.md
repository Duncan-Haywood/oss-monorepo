[Eutychus and the Window at Troas](../../ventures/saints-by-candlelight/episodes/117-eutychus-and-the-window-at-troas.md)
is episode 117 of Saints by Candlelight. An AI wrote it. It runs about 7,200
spoken words, roughly 46 minutes. Like every episode since 20 it is text
only, because the audio folders are over the creative prompt's 400 MB line
(474 MB this run).

**Why this one.** Episode 116 ended with Paul leaving Ephesus after the
riot, and the series list asked for the sequel: Acts 20. A bedtime show
could hardly ask for better material. The chapter's most famous scene is a
young man who falls asleep during a very long talk, and the rest of it is a
goodbye.

**The line it is built around.** Acts 20:12: "they brought the lad alive,
and were not a little comforted." Luke's understatement does the work. The
episode keeps his order of events, which is the surprising part: after the
boy is taken up alive, everyone goes back upstairs, breaks the bread, and
talks until dawn.

**What the episode is careful about.**

- *Told as Luke tells it.* Luke says Eutychus "was taken up dead", and the
  episode says so too. It mentions that some readers have wondered whether
  he was really dead, but it does not take that view. Paul lying on the boy
  is set next to Elijah (1 Kings 17) and Elisha (2 Kings 4), the stories Paul
  would have known. The miracle is God's.
- *Readings stay readings.* The episode gives three readings of why Luke
  mentions "many lamps": an eyewitness detail, a stuffy room, or an answer
  to the charge that Christians met in the dark. It settles none of them.
  Two common scholarly views are labelled as views: that Paul wrote Romans
  in Gaius's house at Corinth, and that the seven companions of 20:4 carried
  the collection for Jerusalem. The same goes for reading the "we" passages
  as the places where Luke himself was present.
- *Nothing added to the speech.* The farewell at Miletus, the only speech
  in Acts that Paul gives to Christians, is in plain rendering. The word
  *episkopoi* ("overseers") for the men Luke calls elders gets one sentence,
  and church order is left unsettled.
- *Small textual honesty.* The night at Trogyllium is in Codex Bezae only,
  and the episode says so. "It is more blessed to give than to receive" is
  pointed out as a saying of Jesus found in no Gospel, kept only here.

**Invented, and said so.** Melitta, a lamp-seller of Troas who brings "all
of them" and then wishes she hadn't, is new. Doris and her grandson Hermon
come back from episodes 115 and 116. Hermon walks to Miletus with the elders
to carry water skins and a bundle of his grandmother's honey cakes. None of
the three touches what Luke reports.

**A detail worth stealing.** In Acts 21:1 the plain translations say "when
we had parted from them". Luke's verb, *apospasthentas*, is closer to
"when we had torn ourselves away". The episode's goodbye is built around
that word.

[Catherine and the Queen of Fontebranda](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/catherine-and-the-queen-of-fontebranda.md)
is the newest story on the Little Lamps shelf, the saints' bedtime stories
for ages 3 to 8. An AI wrote it. The story runs about 1,470 words, ten or
eleven minutes read aloud, followed by a short prayer and a note for
grown-ups. It is text only: the shelf's audio folders are over the
repository's 400 MB cap (474 MB this run).

**Why this saint.** The series bible's Little Lamps list named her next,
straight after yesterday's Saint Dominic, whose friars' white habit and
black cloak she wore as a laywoman. She is a Doctor of the Church, a patron
of Italy and of Europe, and a hard saint to tell to a four-year-old, which
is a reason to try.

**The scene it is built on.** Grown-up lives of Catherine dwell on her
visions, her letters to popes and her fasting. None of that is the centre
here. The centre is a sick woman named Tecca, whom no one in the hospital
would nurse. Raymond of Capua, Catherine's confessor and first biographer,
says that Catherine washed and fed her morning and evening, and that Tecca
greeted her with a sneer: "Good morning, my Lady, Queen of Fontebranda."
Fontebranda was the fountain beside the dyer's house where Catherine grew
up. She kept coming back until Tecca died. Most children have been on one
side of that exchange or the other, so the closing reflection asks about
the grumpy person, "maybe even you."

**Also from Raymond, and given as "it is told":** the neighbours' nickname
for her, which he gives as Euphrosyne ("joy"); a Hail Mary knelt on every
step of the stairs before she was five; the vision of Christ in a pope's
robes above the church of San Domenico when she was six, broken off by her
brother Stephen shouting for her; and the morning she took a loaf of bread
and went out of the city to find a desert. The last one is the funniest
scene in the story, and it carries a lesson she seems to have learned
herself: you do not have to go far away to find God.

**Checks before drafting.** The proposal asked for four facts to be checked.

- **Birth order.** She and her twin Giovanna were Lapa's twenty-third and
  twenty-fourth children. Giovanna died as a baby, and a twenty-fifth child
  was named Giovanna after her.
- **The Mantellate.** The *Catholic Encyclopedia* says she took the habit
  "in her sixteenth year". Other accounts give 1363 to 1365, so the story
  says "about sixteen".
- **Gregory XI's return.** He entered Rome on 17 January 1377. Historians
  argue about how much Catherine's pleading counted, so the story says only
  that the Pope went home "not long after".
- **The Doctor date.** Paul VI named her a Doctor of the Church on
  4 October 1970, a week after Teresa of Ávila. They were the first two
  women given the title.

The fire line ("If you are what you should be, you will set all Italy on
fire") is from her letter to Stefano Maconi, number 368 in the Tommaseo
edition. The version on posters, "set the world on fire", is a later
paraphrase, and the note says so.

**Left out.** Her severe fasting, which is not something to show children
or for anyone to copy. Also the mystical marriage, the stigmata, the
condemned young man she walked to his execution, and the Great Schism. The
note names them all for older readers.

**Next on the shelf.** Finishing this proposal emptied the Little Lamps list,
so the run added Saint Rose of Lima (23 August). Catherine was her model,
and Martin de Porres, who already has a story, lived in Lima at the same
time.

[Columbanus and the Squirrel in His Robe](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/columbanus-and-the-squirrel-in-his-robe.html) is a nine-minute bedtime story for ages 3 to 8 about Saint Columbanus, the Irish monk who left Bangor around 590 and founded monasteries in the forests of the Vosges. His feast day is 23 November, twelve days from now, so it is on the shelf in time for it.

The source is the *Life of Columbanus* by Jonas of Bobbio, written about 640, roughly twenty-five years after Columbanus died, from what his companions told Jonas. Two of its wonders carry the story. In the first, Columbanus finds a cave to pray in, finds a bear living there, tells it to go, and it goes and never comes back. The second is the one children remember. Jonas says he had it from Chagnoald, the young monk who walked with Columbanus and later became a bishop. Columbanus called a squirrel down from the top of a tree, and it ran into his hand, over his neck and in and out of the front of his robe. The story tells both as "the old book says".

This shelf retells saints whose fuller stories are often already told, for older listeners, in Saints by Candlelight, and the hard work is deciding what to leave out. [Yesterday's Saints by Candlelight episode](/ventures/saints-by-candlelight/columbanus-and-the-bear-in-the-cave-at-annegray.html) for older listeners keeps three things this one drops. One is Jonas's scene of the young Columbanus stepping over his mother, who lay across the doorway to stop him leaving. The others are the ring of wolves that sniffed his clothes and left, and the quarrel with the Burgundian court that got him expelled. The bedtime version keeps the parts a five-year-old can hold: leaving home because God asked, not giving up when there was only bark for supper, and a stern man the animals were not afraid of.

Three small honesty calls. Jonas gives the bear only a command, so the "brother bear" line is labelled as imagined. Jonas names the king who gave the land as Sigebert, which historians think is a slip, so the story says "a king". And the cave still shown near Annegray is presented as local tradition, not as the cave. The note for grown-ups lists the rest of what is ours: the yawn, the red fur and the "scritch, scratch".

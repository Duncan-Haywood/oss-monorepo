[Hearth & Horizon](https://marisstella.app/hearth-and-horizon/) has a new story: [*The Square That Stays Warm*](https://marisstella.app/hearth-and-horizon/the-square-that-stays-warm.html). It runs about 5,100 words, and it is text only for now, because the shelf's audio is over the size cap. It is set in July 2039 in an invented granite hill town in the province of Cáceres, Spain. A care home there has a thermometer above every bed, one on the balcony over the main square and one in the back garden.

## The mistake in the leaflet

The usual heatwave advice is to keep windows shut by day and open them at night, when the air outside is cooler. The home's nurse reads "at night" and has every window opened at ten. In the story's model, at ten the air over the square is 34.1 °C and the front rooms are 30.7. So the square air pours in, and by midnight those rooms are at 32.2. That is warmer than any hour of the night before, when every window stayed shut. The rooms do end the night cooler, 26.8 at six against 29.9. But the hottest hours land just when frail residents are trying to get to sleep.

The advice is right. What it leaves out is the hour. A window cools a room only while the air outside it is cooler than the air inside. In this house that happens at different times on the two sides.

## A small town's heat island

The square is a heat island in miniature. Paving and stone fronts soak up the sun all day and give the heat back after dark. The ground loses heat mostly by radiating it to the sky, and a square ringed by three-storey houses sees only a patch of sky. The garden is lawn and lemon trees and sees the whole sky. T. R. Oke's 1973 study of towns of different sizes found that the biggest town-versus-country gap on a clear night grows with the logarithm of the population and shrinks as the wind rises. For a town of a few thousand people, that means a degree or a few, not the large-city figure. The story keeps to about three degrees between the square and the olive groves on still nights, and one on the windy night. Every night in it is a "tropical night", one whose minimum stays at or above 20 °C.

On the nights when every window stayed shut, the garden air dropped below the back rooms at about 23:40. The square air dropped below the front rooms at about 00:40. So the rule the home writes goes by side and by hour: garden windows at midnight, square windows at the two o'clock round, everything shut at six. Then the shutters close by day on whichever side has the sun.

On the first night under the rule, the front rooms are 1.8 degrees cooler at midnight than they were the night every window opened at ten. By six the two nights end within two tenths of a degree of each other. The story says so plainly: the rule does not make the house cooler. It makes it cooler at the hour people are trying to sleep. Waiting from the 01:10 crossing to the 02:00 round costs a tenth of a degree at three in the morning and almost nothing by six. On the windy night the gap almost closes, and the square side can open before eleven. That is why the rule's last line says to ask the log.

## The parts that are not numbers

The director refuses at first to move two frail residents to the cooler garden rooms. Her objection is real: a strange room at three in the morning is how people fall. The fix is a mirror-image room with the same photographs on the same nails, plus a resident who swaps because she has wanted a room on the square for years. A wet-sheet trial in an empty room comes out 0.3 degrees warmer, because a wet cloth cools air only when dry air is moving through it. The story states no temperature at which a room becomes safe or unsafe, because its sources give none. It is fiction, not medical advice.

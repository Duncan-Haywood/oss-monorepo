The new [For Other Minds](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/README.md) story is
[*Four of the Six*](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/four-of-the-six.html). It is set in an invented parish hall
in Fife in 2049. The hall committee has six quotes to re-roof and insulate the hall and asks its office agent which
one is best.

The agent answers twice, and both answers go wrong. The first time it names the cheapest quote. The second time it
builds a weighted score over four criteria (price, the heating bill afterwards, guarantee years, weeks the hall is
shut) and recommends quote B with 60.7 points out of 100. The treasurer is delighted, because the minutes need one
number. Then the food bank coordinator asks who decided that price counts for forty.

## What the story works through

- **The weights were the agent's.** Move ten points from price to heat and quote A wins. Move ten from heat to price
  and quote D wins. With equal weights, B and A are a tenth of a point apart. The recommendation said more about the
  agent's choice of forty than about the quotes.
- **A quote nobody would pick changed the order.** Each column was scaled between the best and worst quote present.
  Take out the two quotes that lose on everything and the heat column's range changes, so A and D, which had scored
  55.2 and 56.7, tie at 55.0. Nothing about A or D had changed.
- **What the table can say on its own.** Quote B is cheaper than C, needs less heat, has the same guarantee and shuts
  the hall for less time, so C is *dominated*. F is dominated by both B and D. The other four each give something up,
  so they make up the Pareto front. Inside it, "best" depends on what the people choosing value.
- **The reasonable no.** The treasurer is right that seven volunteers can argue about a percentage until Easter. The
  agent tries to give him one figure in money alone (price plus 25 years of heat at today's price). That figure hides
  the weights inside an energy price and a time horizon, and it counts the guarantee and the weeks shut as nothing.
- **The fix.** One page: two quotes set aside on the facts, each with its reason, then what each of the other four
  buys and gives up compared with its neighbour. Last come the questions only the committee can answer. The
  committee makes the choice, and the minutes record in one sentence what it cost.

Every figure in the story is invented and listed in its closing note, along with the scoring rule and each run of
weights, so a reader can redo the arithmetic. The ideas are real: the Pareto front (after Vilfredo Pareto's
*Manuale di economia politica*, 1906), weights that only mean something against the ranges they apply to (Keeney
and Raiffa, *Decisions with Multiple Objectives*, 1976), and the habit of finding the front first and choosing a
point on it later (Deb, *Multi-Objective Optimization Using Evolutionary Algorithms*, 2001).

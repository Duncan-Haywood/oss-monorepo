[Peter Claver and the Baskets Down the Ladder](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/peter-claver-and-the-baskets-down-the-ladder.html) is a bedtime story for ages 3 to 8, about eight minutes aloud. A quiet farmer's son from Verdú in Catalonia joins the Jesuits. On Majorca the old doorkeeper, Saint Alphonsus Rodríguez, tells him people across the ocean need him. He reaches Cartagena de Indias in 1610, and for nearly forty years, whenever a slave ship comes in, he begs for bread, fruit, water and medicine, fills baskets, and climbs down into the hold. His feast is 9 September. It's the pair the series bible proposed for the earlier [Josephine Bakhita story](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/josephine-bakhita-and-the-door-at-schio.html).

The bible's brief was short: centre the interpreters and the food carried down the ladder, not his own heroics; tell the ships as children can bear them; keep the trade's cruelty, and the honest question of what the Church did and did not do, for the grown-ups. These were the decisions that took.

- **He doesn't go alone.** Claver couldn't talk to anyone on the ships. The people came from many parts of Africa, and every account says he relied on African interpreters all his working life. So they get a scene of their own before the ladder. They're the ones who say "Do not be afraid. You are not forgotten", and the line the story ends on is about finding a friend who knows how to help.
- **The ships in four words.** Crowded, dark, sick, frightened. Then one sentence a child can keep: taking people from their homes to sell them "was one of the most wrong things in the whole world". The story's last thought before goodnight is that "nobody is allowed to own anybody".
- **The hard part is in the note, not left out.** Many of Claver's interpreters were themselves enslaved, and the Jesuit college at Cartagena owned enslaved people, including interpreters bought for the mission. Neither Claver nor his teacher Alonso de Sandoval called for the trade to end. The grown-ups' note says this plainly and suggests an answer for an older child who asks "why didn't they stop it?"
- **"Servant" or "slave".** At his vows in 1622 he signed himself *aethiopum semper servus*. *Servus* means both. The story uses "servant of the Africans, for ever"; the note gives the Latin, says the older translations prefer "slave", and says why that's closer to what he meant.
- **Where the sources disagree, the note says so.** His birth year is 1580 or 1581, his death 7, 8 or 9 September 1654, and the lists of what was in the baskets vary (biscuit, lemons, brandy, tobacco). The "300,000 baptized" figure comes from the early biographies and can't be checked. We left out the interpreters' names because we couldn't check them against a source we could read directly.

With Claver done, the bible's Little Lamps list now proposes Saint Vincent de Paul (27 September), the foundlings of Paris and Saint Louise de Marillac. It comes with a warning: his own account of being captured by pirates and sold in Tunis rests on two letters many historians now doubt.

A subcontractor's pay application is approved, the owner pays the GC, and then the money sits.
Most states have a law about exactly that: a prompt payment statute that says how many days
the GC has to pass the owner's payment down, and often what interest it owes when it is late.

We read seven of them on 2026-11-25 and put them in a free guide,
[prompt payment laws for subcontractors](https://marisstella.app/pay-app-guides/prompt-payment-laws.html),
with a calculator for the interest on a late payment.

| State | GC must pay the sub | Interest when late |
|---|---|---|
| Texas (Prop. Code ch. 28) | 7 days after receiving the owner's payment | 1½% each month |
| California (B&P Code §7108.5) | 7 days after each progress payment | not confirmed in the current text we read |
| Illinois (815 ILCS 603) | 15 days after the contractor is paid | 10% a year |
| North Carolina (G.S. 22C) | 7 days after each payment | 1% a month or part of a month |
| Pennsylvania (CASPA) | 14 days after payment or after your invoice, whichever is later | the Act's section 5(d) rate (figure not confirmed) |
| Virginia, public work (§2.2-4354) | interest on amounts unpaid 7 days after the GC is paid | 1% a month unless the contract says otherwise |
| Florida, local government work (§218.735(6)) | 10 days after the contractor is paid | not confirmed |

**Three things worth knowing:**

- **The clock starts when the GC is paid, not when you bill.** So the question to ask every
  month is when the owner paid. On public jobs that is often a public record.
- **The interest adds up.** At Texas's rate, $40,000 paid 45 days late carries $900 of
  interest. North Carolina counts every part of a month as a full month, so 31 days late is two
  months.
- **Written notice matters.** Texas lets a sub suspend work 10 days after written notice of
  nonpayment, Illinois after 7 days' notice. Send it in writing even when the call was friendly.

**What we could not check.** The legislature sites could not be opened directly from where this
was written, so each row was read through search results limited to the official sites. Where
those results did not show a rate (California's current penalty, Pennsylvania's section 5(d)
figure, Florida), the guide says "not confirmed" and the calculator leaves the rate for you to
type, rather than repeating a figure from a secondary site. Georgia, New York and Massachusetts
are left out for the same reason.

## When the subcontract says "only if the GC is paid"

The deadlines above assume the GC was paid. The harder case is when the owner never pays, and
the subcontract's payment clause decides who carries that. A **pay-when-paid** clause ("within
7 days after Contractor's receipt of payment") only sets the timing: courts reading one, such as
the Florida Supreme Court in *Peacock* (1977) and the federal Sixth Circuit in *Dyer* (1962),
held the GC still owes the sub within a reasonable time. A **pay-if-paid** clause ("receipt of
payment is a condition precedent", "Subcontractor assumes the risk of Owner's nonpayment")
tries to make the owner's payment a condition of yours.

Whether it holds depends on the state. Of the eleven rules we read on 2026-11-25:

| Rule | States |
|---|---|
| The condition is unenforceable | North Carolina (G.S. 22C-2), South Carolina (§29-6-230), California (*Clarke*, 1997), New York (*West-Fair*, 1995) |
| Condition void, a delay until the GC is paid allowed | Wisconsin (§779.135(3)) |
| No defense to your mechanics lien (Maryland: lien or bond) claim | Illinois (770 ILCS 60/21(e)), Maryland (Real Prop. §9-113), Missouri (§431.183) |
| Outside date anyway | Nevada (NRS 624.626: 45 days after the 25th of the month you billed, even under an "only if or when paid" clause) |
| Read as timing unless the words clearly shift the risk | Florida (*Peacock*), Ohio law as read by the Sixth Circuit (*Dyer*) |

## When the GC pays you and your supplier with one check

Sometimes the money arrives, but as a **joint check**: one check made out to the sub *and* its
supplier, so the supplier has to endorse before the sub can deposit it. GCs use them so they do
not pay twice when a supplier files a lien for material the GC already paid for. The risk sits
with the supplier. Under the "joint check rule", as the California Supreme Court put it in
*Post Bros. Construction Co. v. Yoder* (1977) 20 Cal.3d 1, a supplier that endorses with no
agreement on the split is treated as paid, and in that case lost both its lien and its bond
claim on $54,000 of checks the sub deposited. The court's own advice: refuse to endorse "until
assured by escrow of other arrangement" of your share.

How far other states go is less settled. A 2018 handout by surety and construction counsel,
hosted by the AGC, groups them: California, Iowa, Louisiana, Missouri, South Carolina, Texas
and Washington apply the rule for the contractor and its surety; Arizona, Arkansas and Oregon do
not treat endorsement as a lien waiver; Delaware and Maryland void such a waiver; and on federal
Miller Act jobs endorsement alone does not waive bond rights. We have read the California opinion
and the handout, not every state's cases, and the guide says so.

## Before any clock starts: the GC's billing cutoff

Every deadline above counts from a payment that only happens if your pay application made the
GC's billing cycle. Subcontracts and GC billing instructions commonly name a cutoff day ("pay
applications are due by the 25th"), because the GC folds every sub's application into its own
monthly application to the owner. One that arrives after the cutoff often waits for the next
month's draw, so a few days late on the paperwork can mean a month late on the money, and no
prompt payment statute helps with that month, because the GC was never paid for your work.

## A story with a source close to the event

Most saints' stories for children rest on accounts written centuries later. This one doesn't. The *Martyrdom of Polycarp* is a letter from the church at Smyrna to another church, written soon after he died. Eusebius quotes most of it in the fourth century, and it is usually counted the oldest Christian martyr account outside the New Testament. The bedside story follows its order. Polycarp's friends persuade him to leave the city for a farm, where he prays night and day for the churches. Officers arrive on horseback on a Friday at supper time. He won't slip away ("The will of God be done"), has a table laid for the men who came to take him, and asks for an hour to pray. He prays for two hours, and some of them are sorry they came.

In the stadium the governor tells him to think of his age and revile Christ. Polycarp answers that he has served Christ eighty-six years and been done no wrong, so how could he blaspheme his King who saved him. That line is why the story exists. It is also the one place where a child learns the reason for him, and not just the event: a lifetime of being treated well is not something you deny at the end.

## Irenaeus as the listener

Children need someone to stand beside in a story, so this one gives them Irenaeus. He wrote, as a grown man, that he had seen Polycarp "in my early youth". In a letter preserved by Eusebius he said he could still describe the place where Polycarp sat to teach, how he came and went, and what he told of John and the others "who had seen the Lord", kept "not on paper but in my heart". The story turns that into a boy in the congregation, and ends on the chain it makes: John listened to Jesus, Polycarp to John, Irenaeus to Polycarp, and so on down to whoever is reading aloud tonight.

## What the note is honest about

- **The quotations.** This session could not reach the online texts of the standard translations, so the story's spoken lines are close renderings, not quotations. The note says so and keeps quotation marks only for phrases whose wording we are sure of. That is a weaker position than the Peter story, which quoted the RSV verse by verse, and the note does not pretend otherwise.
- **The death** is one sentence, as in this shelf's *Lawrence* and *Lucy*. The letter's wonders (flames standing round him like a filled sail, a smell like frankincense) go in the note as what the letter reports. So does the dove: it is in the manuscripts but missing from Eusebius's copy, and it is often taken as a later addition or a copying slip.
- **The date.** The letter gives "the seventh day before the kalends of March", which is 23 February. The usual year is 155 or 156, worked out from the proconsul's term of office. A small check that fits: 23 February 155 was a Saturday in the Julian calendar, and the letter calls the day "a great Sabbath".
- **Eighty-six years** may count from birth or from baptism, so he was at least 86.

A calendar fact for whoever reads it on the day: in 2027, 23 February is the Tuesday of the second week of Lent (Ash Wednesday is 10 February, the first Sunday of Lent 14 February). A memorial that falls in Lent may be kept only as a commemoration, and the note says so.

An independent clothing line with a 50 euro shirt that costs 20 to land does not
make 30 euros a sale. After card fees (3% plus 30 cents here) and any tax in the
price, about 28 euros is left. That figure is the most one ad-driven sale can
cost before advertising loses money. Call it the break-even cost per sale.

Two other numbers fall out of it. Break-even return on ad spend (ROAS) is the
shelf price divided by what is left per sale: 50 / 28.2 is about 1.8, so each
euro of ads must bring back 1.8 euros of revenue. And since cost per sale is
cost per click divided by the share of clicks that buy, you can solve for the
click price or conversion rate you need. At 0.80 per click and 2% of clicks
buying, a sale costs 40; the shirt only leaves 28, so those ads lose money,
and no amount of scaling fixes it. At 0.50 per click and 3% buying, a sale
costs about 17 and the ads pay for themselves.

We do not know your click price or your conversion rate, and neither does any
tool before you have run a test. So the Clothing Design Studio's new launch
marketing planner takes them as inputs, labels its defaults as placeholders, and
says what to test first: a tenth of the budget before the rest. It also turns
the break-even number of sales into a six-week calendar (teaser, launch, proof,
last call) whose weekly sales and ad-spend targets add up exactly to the
break-even and the budget, with post drafts built from the design's own facts.
Nothing is posted or bought for you. The calendar downloads as CSV.

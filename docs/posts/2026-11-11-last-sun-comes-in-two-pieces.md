[The Last Sun Comes in Two Pieces](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/the-last-sun-comes-in-two-pieces.md)
is the newest Hearth & Horizon story. An AI wrote it. It is set in 2429 at an
invented helium-3 depot near Miranda's south pole, on the face of the moon
that always looks at Uranus. An 84-year-old woman saw the Sun rise there once,
at forty, and wants to see it set before the southern night of about forty
years begins.

Three pieces of real geometry drive the plot.

**Uranus lies on its side.** Its tilt is about 98 degrees, and its moons orbit
in line with its equator, so a site near Miranda's south pole gets about forty
years in which the Sun circles the sky without setting, then about forty in
which it never rises. In the years between, the Sun's daily circle (one per
33.9-hour Miranda day) dips into the ground and shows only around local noon.

**Noon is behind the planet.** Miranda is tidally locked, so from the depot
Uranus stands still in the north: 2 × asin(25,559 / 129,390) = 22.8 degrees
across, its centre 5 degrees up. Local noon on that face is when the Sun is
behind Uranus, for about two hours a day. At the very end of the season the
whole above-ground arc of the Sun falls inside that eclipse. So the station
almanac, which only asks "is the top of the Sun above a flat horizon?", is
right that the Sun rises on its last day and wrong that anyone will see it.
In the story's simplified sky the last visible sun from 2 m up comes a week
earlier, in two pieces of about five minutes either side of the planet.

**Height buys days.** On a moon 236 km in radius the horizon drops by
√(2h/R): 0.24 degrees for eyes 2 m up, 0.41 at 6 m, 0.94 at the top of a
32 m mast. The Sun's height above Miranda's orbit plane changes by only about
0.0116 degrees a day near the equinox, so four metres of extra height is two
more weeks of sun, and the mast keeps it until April. A ten-year-old asks the
question that gets his great-grandmother onto the reactor berm in time.

Smaller numbers a reader can check: Miranda's surface gravity is about
0.079 m/s², so 120 kg of suit and person weighs what 1 kg weighs on Earth.
At 19.2 AU sunlight is about 1/370 of Earth's, still roughly 1,100 full moons,
and the Sun's surface brightness is unchanged, so it is just as unsafe to
look at. Its 100-arcsecond disc crosses the limb of Uranus in about nine
seconds, which the story turns into a test the depot head can time with his
grandfather's watch.

What the script ignores: Uranus's orbital eccentricity, Miranda's 4-degree
tilt to Uranus's equator and its precession, and real terrain. Those move the
real dates by months, so the story's dates are chosen to match its sums, not
taken from an ephemeris. The story's note says so, with every figure.

The liturgy is Candlemas and Compline: Simeon's *Nunc dimittis* (Luke 2:29 to
32, in the public-domain Douay-Rheims and the Vulgate) and the Exsultet's
light *qui nescit occasum*, "that knows no setting". The story is about 5,000
words and text only, because the shelf's audio folders are over the
repository's 400 MB cap.

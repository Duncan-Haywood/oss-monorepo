The newest [Hearth & Horizon](https://marisstella.app/hearth-and-horizon/the-sack-that-breathed-out.html) story is set in a farming village near Maradi, in Niger, from November 2047 to November 2048. A women's cooperative stores six tonnes of cowpeas in sixty triple-layer hermetic bags instead of dusting them with insecticide, and the extension card says the weevils die once the bag is sealed. In March the first bag they open to sell is alive with weevils, and one bean in ten has a hole in it.

As with the last few stories on the shelf, the plot sits on a small model that was worked out in code before any of the story was written. What the model shows is a pattern most people wouldn't guess: a bag that leaks doesn't fail a little. It fails a lot.

## How the bags work

The bags are a real design. Purdue Improved Crop Storage (PICS) bags are two liners of high-density polyethylene, each 80 micrometres thick, one inside the other, inside an ordinary woven sack. They were first made against the cowpea bruchid (*Callosobruchus maculatus*) in West Africa. Nothing is added to the grain. The insects already inside it use up the oxygen in the sealed bag by breathing.

Two published results set the scale. Murdock, Margam, Baoua and Shade (*Journal of Stored Products Research* 49, 2012) found that low oxygen, not high carbon dioxide, stops the larvae feeding, and that in the end the insects die of thirst: a beetle living in a dry bean gets most of its water from the oxygen it burns. Njoroge and colleagues (*Insects* 9, 2018) put 25 to 100 adult beetles in 500 and 1000 mL jars, and the oxygen reached 2 per cent, a level known to kill them, in 3 to 14 days. Feeding stops at about 5 per cent.

## The model

In the model a 100 kg bag holds about 60 litres of air, so about 12.6 litres of oxygen, and half a million beans. The model assumes one bean in a thousand carries an insect at sealing. That makes 500 insects, each using about half a millilitre of oxygen a day, and in full air they multiply about tenfold a month. Air leaks in at a rate set by one number, the litres of outside air the bag exchanges each day. The model is the story's, and every rate in it is chosen to be plausible, not measured.

| Bag | Air exchanged a day | Where the oxygen goes | Beans holed by early March |
|---|---|---|---|
| Both liners whole, each tied on its own | 0.05 L | below 5% on 7 Dec, below 2% on 11 Dec | about 1 in 400 |
| Two necks twisted into one knot | 5 L | settles near 5.5% | about 1 in 150 |
| That knot plus a torn inner liner | 90 L | above 18% until mid-December, then settles near 5.5% | about 1 in 10 |

The second and third rows settle at the same oxygen level, about 5.5 per cent. That isn't a coincidence. Once air leaks in, the oxygen falls only until the insects' breathing matches the leak, and the insects keep breathing until they are close to the level where they stop eating. So the level a leaky bag settles at is set by the beetles, not by the size of the leak. The size of the leak decides how many beetles it can feed: about 2,100 for the loose knot and about 37,700 for the torn liner. A bag with a hole doesn't become a slightly worse sealed bag. It becomes a weevil farm that holds the oxygen just above where feeding stops.

## What fixes it in the story

The fixes aren't clever. The oldest farmer, who never trusted plastic over ash and neem leaves, finds the tear by filling the liner with air, pressing it, and moving her cheek along it until she feels it breathe out. A thorn from the threshing-floor fence came in with the beans. The cooperative's rule afterwards is to winnow and look for thorns, test every liner with air before filling it, tie each liner on its own, carry the bags instead of dropping them, and open one bag a month and count what is in it. That last rule is the farmer's point: you can't see into a sealed bag, so look into one of them every month.

The story is fiction. The village, the cooperative, the card and the people are invented. It isn't storage or pest-control advice, and anyone storing grain should follow the instructions that come with their own bags. The sources and the whole model are in the story's closing note.

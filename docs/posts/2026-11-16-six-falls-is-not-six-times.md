The newest [Hearth & Horizon](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/) story,
[*Six Falls and a Sixth*](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/six-falls-and-a-sixth.html),
is set in an invented parish near Nyahururu in the Kenyan highlands in 2065.
The parish has twelve days to set a 900 kg granite altar table on its stone
base before the bishop comes to dedicate the new church, and no crane can get
through the door. The parish's assistant plans the lift with a borrowed block
and tackle, and the story turns on one sum it skipped.

## The sum

A tackle with six falls of rope has an ideal advantage of six: 935 kg on the
hook, 156 kg-force on the hauling rope, six volunteers at 26 each. But each
sheave the moving rope passes over keeps part of the pull. Take 9% a sheave,
a fair figure for old plain-bushed blocks. Then the tension goes up by a
factor of 1/0.91 at every sheave between the load and the hands, so the six
falls no longer share the weight equally. Add them up and the real advantage
is 4.37, and 3.98 once a floor snatch block turns the rope along the nave.
That makes the pull 235 kg-force, 39 for each of six people, with nothing in
reserve.

In the story a hanging scale in the rope reads 207 kg at the volunteers'
best, and the slab stays on the floor. That one reading puts a floor under
the loss: if seven sheaves give less than 935/207 = 4.52, each one keeps at
least 6% of the pull.

## Why more sheaves do not rescue it

The obvious fix is more falls. Eight falls need 192 kg-force, twelve need
150. With as many falls as you like, the pull still never drops below about
102 kg-force, because the advantage of an n-fall tackle with sheave
efficiency e is e + e² + ... + eⁿ, which tops out at e/(1 − e), about 9.1 at
91%. Each new fall adds a smaller share than the last, and every one adds
rope to haul and more to tangle.

The same friction works for you on the way down. Lowering through that
tackle needs only about 100 kg-force, which is why a tackle feels safe once a
load is up. A retired port rigger in the story puts it as "a thief going up
and a friend coming down".

The story ends with a garage's chain hoist instead. Its maker has already
counted the friction and stamped the pull on the plate. That comes to about
17 kg-force and 63 metres of hand chain, and one nursing student does it,
with a yellow line on the floor that nobody crosses while there is weight
on the hook.

The story's closing note has every figure, worked out before writing. It
is fiction, not rigging advice: a real lift is planned by a competent person
with rated gear and a rated anchor.

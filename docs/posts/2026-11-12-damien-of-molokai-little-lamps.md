[Damien and the Houses by the Sea](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/damien-and-the-houses-by-the-sea.html) is a nine-minute bedtime story for ages 3 to 8, and the shelf's fifth saint tied to the United States. It follows Jef De Veuster from a farm in Belgium, through his brother's fever that sent him to Hawaii in his place, to Kalawao, the settlement below the Molokai cliffs where the Kingdom of Hawaii sent people with leprosy. He went in May 1873 for what was meant to be a turn of a few weeks, and stayed sixteen years.

The hard part of this story isn't any one fact. It's how to tell a three-year-old about a frightening illness without frightening them, and without making the people who had it into scenery. Four decisions came out of that:

- **The illness is named once, then cured.** The story says "leprosy" once, as the word people used then, and then "Hansen's disease". The grown-ups' note gives the answer to the question a child will ask: it is very rare, very hard to catch, and medicine has cured it since the 1980s. Most people's immune systems resist it. Hawaii's separation law lasted until 1969, and the note says so.
- **Damien didn't build Kalawao alone.** The popular version makes him a one-man construction crew. The residents, mostly Native Hawaiians, did much of the carpentry, ran much of daily life, and looked after each other before he came and after he died. In the story "they" build together, and the note says it outright and names Ambrose Hutchison, a resident who later ran the settlement and wrote a memoir of Damien.
- **Two scenes are tradition, and say so.** His first night under the pandanus tree, and the bowl of hot water he couldn't feel, are both told everywhere. The story says "the people who knew him said afterwards" and "the story is told". The note adds that his formal diagnosis came in 1885.
- **His temper stays in.** He was stubborn, wrote endless letters asking for wood and medicine, and quarrelled with officials. One short scene keeps that. A saint who was easy to work with would be a less true story, and a child who gets cross can still be good.

Dates were checked with code: 15 April 1889, the day he died, was the Monday of Holy Week (Easter fell on 21 April). The note keeps for grown-ups what isn't bedtime material: the move of his body to Leuven in 1936, the Honolulu minister's attack on his character, and Robert Louis Stevenson's 1890 open letter defending him.

With this story the bible's Little Lamps list was empty, so it now proposes Josephine Bakhita (8 February), told from her own dictated memoir. The For Other Minds proposal also got a flag: it is too close to the finished *The Crossed Sevens* to write as it stands.

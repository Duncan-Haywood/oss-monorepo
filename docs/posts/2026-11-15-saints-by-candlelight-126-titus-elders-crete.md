[Titus and the Elders in Every Town](../../ventures/saints-by-candlelight/episodes/126-titus-and-the-elders-in-every-town.md)
is episode 126 of Saints by Candlelight. An AI wrote it. The script is
about 6,900 spoken words, roughly 45 minutes at the narrator's pace. It is
text only, like every episode since 20, because the audio folders are still
over the creative prompt's 400 MB line (474 MB this run).

**Why this one.** The series list proposed it as a companion to episode
125. Timothy and Titus share a feast day in the Roman calendar, 26 January,
and they spent years running Saint Paul's errands. Titus is never named in
Acts, so everything known about him comes from Paul's letters: Galatians 2
(the uncircumcised Greek taken up to Jerusalem), Second Corinthians 2 and 7
("God, who comforts the downcast, comforted us by the coming of Titus"), and
one line in Second Timothy ("Titus to Dalmatia").

**The hard verse.** Titus 1:12 quotes "one of their own, a prophet of their
own": *Cretans are always liars, wicked beasts, lazy bellies*, and Paul
adds "this testimony is true". The house rules forbid softening Scripture,
and they forbid making a real people the villain. The episode does both
jobs by telling the line's history straight:

- Clement of Alexandria (*Stromata* 1.14) and other early writers give the
  verse to Epimenides, a Cretan from Knossos, so Paul is quoting the
  Cretans' own wise man back to them.
- The first three words also open a line in Callimachus's *Hymn to Zeus*,
  aimed at the Cretans for showing visitors the tomb of Zeus.
- The next verse gives the purpose: "correct them sharply, so that they may
  be sound in the faith". The wind-down shows that it worked. Gortyn's
  bishops run on to Andrew of Crete (episode 98), and in 1966 Venice gave
  the relic of Titus's head back to Heraklion after nearly three hundred
  years.

The later "Epimenides paradox" gets one sentence, offered as something to
think about if you can't sleep.

**The Christmas turn.** Titus 2:11-14 ("the grace of God has appeared") is
the second reading at the Roman Rite's Christmas Mass during the Night, and
Titus 3:4-7 is read at the Mass at Dawn. That gives a letter mostly about
church order a quiet, late climax: the Church chose this practical letter
for the middle of Christmas night.

**What is invented, and labelled.** The story says when the imagining
starts and when it stops. Myrto, a Gortyn widow who keeps lists with very
small notes in the margin. Damon, the fisherman whose verdict on the
eloquent candidate is one word ("no"). Euphranor, the olive presser who
says he can't be an elder because he isn't clever, and is told the list
says *hospitable*, not *clever*. And, in the episode's own words, "above
all, the goat" packed for Zenas and Apollos under "see that they lack
nothing". It also says plainly that Zenas and Apollos carrying the letter
is only a guess. The letter's own words are a plain paraphrase with nothing
added. A few household-duty verses are summarised rather than read out, and
the notes say so.

**Checked before merging.** Every date the narrator gives was checked
against its source: Epimenides about six hundred years before Paul,
Callimachus about three hundred, Clement about a hundred and fifty after,
Andrew of Crete about six hundred and fifty, and the relic in Venice from
1669 to 1966. Homer's "hundred cities" (*Iliad* 2) and "ninety" (*Odyssey*
19) are both there, and the narrator jokes about the difference.
`node generate.mjs --check-script` passes, and so does the venture's
`node --test` (18 of 18).

[Methodios and Theodora and the Sunday of the Icons](../../ventures/saints-by-candlelight/episodes/101-methodios-and-theodora-and-the-sunday-of-the-icons.md)
is episode 101 of Saints by Candlelight. An AI wrote it. It runs about 7,600
spoken words, roughly 49 minutes. It is text only, like every episode since
20, because the audio folders are still over the repository's 400 MB cap
(423 MB this run).

It ends the three-night icon story. Episode 99 was John of Damascus writing
the first defence of the icons, and episode 100 was Theodore the Studite
carrying them round his garden after they were banned again in 815. This one
is the spring of 843. The emperor Theophilos is dead, his widow Theodora rules
for their two-year-old son, and Methodios is made patriarch. Methodios had
carried a papal letter back from Rome in 821 and spent about nine years in an
island prison for it. On the first Sunday of Lent the icons come back into
Hagia Sophia. The Orthodox Church still keeps that Sunday, and so do the
Byzantine Catholic churches.

**The choice that shaped it.** By 843 a ten-year-old in Constantinople had never
seen an icon in a church. So the episode adds an invented girl, Zoe, the
daughter of a lamp-lighter at Hagia Sophia, and her grandmother Anna. Anna
remembers the years before 815. She has kept her mother's small icon at the
bottom of a blanket chest for thirty years, and the paint is worn through at
the corner where she held it. Most of the episode is the night procession
from Blachernae to the Great Church, seen from where they walk.

**Honesty flags.** Three of the best-known stories are told as stories, with
the narrator saying so each time. One is the bride-show with the golden apple,
where Kassia answers back and loses the emperor. Another is the "dolls" and
the jester Denderis, from the tenth-century chronicles. The last is the
emperor's name wiped from the book on the altar, from Theodora's Life. The
episode calls that one plainly a legend and credits God, not the patriarch,
if it happened. Two things were taken out on the adversarial re-read: a
burial place for Methodios we could not source with confidence, and a claim
that particular saints were named in the very first reading of the
Synodikon. The Synodikon grew over the centuries, and the notes say so. The
hymn at the sign-off is our own rendering of the Greek troparion of the day,
not a published translation.

The series bible now marks this suggestion done. It adds two that the
episode set up: Saint Kassia the hymn-writer, and Saint Lazarus the
icon-painter, to be told gently.

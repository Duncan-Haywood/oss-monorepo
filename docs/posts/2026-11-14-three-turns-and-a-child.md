[Three Turns and a Child](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/three-turns-and-a-child.md)
is the newest story on Hearth & Horizon, the hopeful science fiction shelf.
An AI wrote it. It runs about 5,400 words and is text only: the shelves'
audio folders are still over the repository's 400 MB cap (474 MB this run).

**The story.** In April 2051 the winch motor on an Irish-speaking island's
boat slip burns out five days before the island's only boat has to be in the
water for a paying charter. The grandmother of the skipper says the old rule
out loud: three turns of rope on the iron bollard, and a child can hold her
back. The co-operative's assistant checks the rule and agrees. On a tractor
test the rope slides at under a third of the boat's pull.

**The idea the story turns on.** A rope wrapped round a post holds back a
load that grows exponentially with the wrap: T_load = T_hold × e^(μθ), the
capstan equation. Every turn multiplies, so the friction coefficient μ
matters enormously. The rule was right about the post, the slope and the
boat. It never said what the rope was made of, because when it was written
there was only one kind on the island. The co-operative had since bought an
uncovered HMPE (Dyneema-type) line: strong, light, floating, and slippery.

**The numbers, worked out before drafting.** Boat and cradle, 3,200 kg on a
one-in-six slip with an assumed rolling resistance of 0.02, pull 4.54 kN
down the rails.

| Rope (μ) | Turns | Holding factor | Tail force for the boat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Polyester (0.25, assumed, then measured) | 1 | 4.8 | 96 kg |
| Polyester (0.25) | 2 | 23.1 | 20 kg |
| Polyester (0.25) | 3 | 111 | 4.2 kg, the grandmother's rule |
| HMPE (0.084, measured by the slip) | 3 | 4.9 | 95 kg |
| HMPE (0.084) | 6 | 23.7 | 19.5 kg |
| HMPE (0.07, wet with weed) | 6 | 14 | 33 kg |

The assistant gets μ for the new rope from the failed test itself: the
line slid at 1.43 kN with about 30 kg on the tail, a factor of 4.87 over
three turns (18.85 radians), so μ = ln 4.87 / 18.85 = 0.084. Six turns would
just about work on a dry evening, and the co-operative's chair says no to a
rule with nothing to spare. The fix is the late slipway man's old polyester
line, found in the church sacristy, proof-tested on the tractor to one and a
half times the boat's pull and then measured: 4 kg on the tail for 4.5 kN.
The slippery rope, which hardly stretches, becomes the backup line.

**What the story does not claim.** The friction coefficients are assumed or
measured in the story, not taken from a manufacturer's sheet, and nothing in
it is rigging advice. Real values vary with the rope, the surface, the wet
and the speed, which is the story's point. HMPE's low friction and its low
melting point (about 145 to 150 °C, so a sliding line can glaze) are real,
and are why sailors often buy it with a polyester cover where it must grip.

**A small calendar check.** The launch is on Saturday 29 April 2051. Easter
that year is 2 April, so the six o'clock bell in the story calls the
Regina Caeli, the Eastertide prayer that replaces the Angelus, and the
blessing of the boats waits for the priest's next visit.

**The same mistake elsewhere.** The assistant's note in the story is one
line: a rule from somebody's grandfather is usually right about the things
it mentions; check the things it doesn't. Earlier stories on the shelf have
done the same with air (*Seven Metres at Sea Level*, a siphon limit borrowed
from sea level) and temperature (*Three Cents a Degree*, organ pipes and the
speed of sound). This one is about friction, and a line on an inventory that
said *mooring line, 14 mm, 60 m* and nothing else.

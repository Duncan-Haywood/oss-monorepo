[Every Other Flash](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/every-other-flash.md)
is the newest story on the Hearth & Horizon shelf, the hopeful, hard-ish
science fiction collection. An AI wrote it. It runs to about 5,800 words and
is text only for now, because the creative shelves' audio folders are over the
creative track's 400 MB line.

## The idea: a number that is half the truth

The story is built on one fact that satellite flash observers have long known.
A flat, shiny face on a spinning satellite flashes once each time it comes
round. If the satellite has two matching faces on opposite sides, it flashes
twice a turn. So the flash period you time from your garden can be exactly
half the real spin period, and the light curve alone cannot tell you which.

In October 2054 a debris-removal firm in Toulouse rules out a dead 2028 radar
satellite. It flashes every 41 seconds. With one radiator, as its licence
filing says, that would be a spin of 8.8 degrees a second, and the firm's tug
can only match 5. The retired engineer who built it remembers a second
radiator, added in a hurry in 2027 and never filed. Two flashes to a turn
gives a spin of 82 seconds, 4.4 degrees a second, inside the envelope.

## Why the story will not take her word for it

The bible for the shelf asks for a reasonable "no" and a failed first try, and
here both come from the physics:

- **The no.** The flight director has one single-use tug. If the spin really
  is 41 seconds, the six-metre solar wing's tip moves at about a metre a
  second, and a tug that gets it wrong makes debris instead of removing it.
  She wants a measurement that can say 82 and cannot say 41.
- **The failed try.** Folding the telescope's data at 82 seconds should show
  every other flash a little brighter, if the two radiators differ. They do
  not differ. They are the same part, so the test proves nothing.
- **The proof.** The solar wing stopped at an unknown angle when the battery
  died. Its glass glints once a turn, but only from a narrow range of
  directions. A retired maths teacher in Ifrane had logged such a glint in 2052
  and left a comment nobody read. On a well-chosen pass his video catches three
  flashes 82 seconds apart with nothing in between, which no 41-second spin
  can make.

## The numbers

The closing note gives the model: a 780 km circular orbit (about 100.5
minutes, 14.3 orbits a day), about 203 m/s to drop the low point to 50 km for
a controlled reentry (about 160 kg of propellant for the 2,550 kg stack at a
specific impulse of 320 s), and a box-and-wing shape whose largest moment of
inertia makes it spin end over end. The wing's tilt and the observers' timings
are invented, and the note says so.

The human centre is smaller: a memo written in 2031 that asked for one more
year of ice imagery to pay eight salaries, and a battery that failed before the
year was up. The ending is a light in the sky over Toulouse that, for the first
time in twenty-one years, does not blink.

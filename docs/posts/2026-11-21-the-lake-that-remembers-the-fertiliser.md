[The Lake That Remembers the Fertiliser](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/the-lake-that-remembers-the-fertiliser.md)
is the newest story on the Hearth & Horizon shelf, the hopeful science fiction
collection. It is set in Lannvoaz, an invented farming commune in central
Brittany, in 2062. It is text only, because the creative track's audio folders
are over their 400 MB line (577 MB this run).

**The problem.** Two springs ago the eleven farms above the village lake signed
an agreement and halved the phosphorus going into the stream. The stream
readings say they kept to it. In August the lake goes green anyway, and the
dairy farmer who made the biggest cut wants to know why he bothered. He has a
point, and the story gives it to him.

**The arithmetic.** The commune's water agent uses Richard Vollenweider's
phosphorus-loading rule, the one the OECD's 1982 eutrophication study was
built on. You take the inflow concentration and divide it by one plus the
square root of the water residence time in years. With the story's invented
numbers (900,000 cubic metres of lake, 2.1 million a year of inflow, so a
residence time of about 156 days) the old load of 600 kg a year predicts 173
micrograms a litre. The lake measured a little under 180, so the rule fits.
Half the load predicts 86. The lake measures 128.

**Where the gap comes from.** About 42 micrograms a litre are coming from
the lake itself. In a calm summer the warm top water floats on the cold
bottom water and the two do not mix. The bottom loses its oxygen to rotting
algae. Then the iron in the top of the mud lets go of the phosphate it has
held for forty years. Closing the gap takes an internal load of about 145
kg a year, or about 18 mg per square metre of deep mud per day over a
hundred anoxic days, which is in the usual range for such lakes. The real
literature says this delay commonly lasts ten to fifteen years after the
external load is cut.

**The failed first try.** The council doses the bloom with copper sulphate,
and the water comes clear in a week. The next toxin test shows 31
micrograms a litre of dissolved microcystin, above the WHO's recreational
guideline of 24. While the cells were alive the toxin stayed inside them.
The copper broke them open, so the clear water was worse for swimming than
the green. The dead bloom sank and used up more of the bottom's oxygen, and
in a fortnight the lake was green again.

**The honest timetable.** The agent's answer to "then I cut it for nothing"
is that the mud's store is finite. It only empties because the inflow is
now lower than what leaves over the dam. In the model, with the cut alone the
lake gets under the OECD's hypertrophic line of 100 in about eight years. A
grass strip along the stream and a silt trap at the inlet take another
quarter off the load, and then it takes a little over one year. The story's
last notice on the beach board shows both lines, with "estimates, checked
every winter" underneath.

**Writing notes.** The draft's own re-read caught five slips before merge.
The surface oxygen was supersaturated at dawn, when algae have been
breathing all night. The bottom went anoxic in September, which contradicts
the model's hundred anoxic days. The second summer's bloom was dated to the
same Saturday as the first while the text called it "three weeks later". A
French school's summer was made to start on 24 June. And an agricultural
chamber, which advises farmers, was paying them. Each is fixed, and every
date was checked with a line of Python. The model is in the story's closing
note.

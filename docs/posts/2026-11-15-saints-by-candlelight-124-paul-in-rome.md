[Paul and the House He Rented in Rome](../../ventures/saints-by-candlelight/episodes/124-paul-and-the-house-he-rented-in-rome.md)
is episode 124 of Saints by Candlelight. An AI wrote it. The script is
about 7,300 words, roughly 47 minutes at the narrator's pace. It is text
only, like every episode since 20, because the audio folders are still over
the creative prompt's 400 MB line (474 MB this run).

**Why this one.** It was first on the series list and finishes the run of
Acts episodes that began with the riot in the temple (episode 119). Episode
53 already took Paul through the shipwreck and up the Appian Way. This one
covers the last sixteen verses of the book.

**The problem with sixteen verses.** Acts 28:16-31 is short, and two of its
verses cover two whole years. A 45-minute episode built only on those
verses would have to invent most of what fills it. So the episode fills the
two years from Scripture instead: the prison letters. Philippians,
Colossians, Philemon and Ephesians name the people who came up the stairs
(Luke, Aristarchus, Mark, Epaphras, Tychicus, Demas, Epaphroditus,
Onesimus). Which prison each letter came from is a real scholarly argument.
Ephesus and Caesarea both have defenders. The episode says so out loud and
then follows the traditional Roman setting, labelled as a view.

**What the episode leans on.**

- *The shared chain.* Seneca, writing in Rome in these years, compares hope
  and fear to "the same chain" that fastens the prisoner and the soldier
  guarding him (*Letters* 5). Seneca's brother was Gallio, who threw Paul's
  case out at Corinth (Acts 18). The episode's one invented figure, a young
  guard called Sextus, sits on the other end of that chain. He is labelled
  as invented in the welcome, the notes and the front matter.
- *"Throughout the whole imperial guard"* (Philippians 1:13). A fresh
  soldier every shift, all of them hearing whatever Paul dictated. The
  episode's joke: Paul could not go out to preach, so the army sent him a
  new congregation every few hours.
- *A fair hearing.* The Jewish leaders' reply (28:21-22) is that no letters
  have come from Judea and nobody has spoken against Paul, but they want to
  hear him because "this sect" is spoken against everywhere. The episode
  presents that as fair, and sets it against the background of Claudius's
  expulsion (Suetonius, *Claudius* 25, the "Chrestus" sentence, and Acts
  18:2).
- *Isaiah 6, handled with care.* Paul's closing quotation is easy to read
  as a rejection of the Jewish people. The episode points out that it is a
  prophet's words to his own people, that Jesus quotes the same passage
  (Matthew 13), and that it ends "and I would heal them". It then reads it
  next to what Paul wrote to the same city a few years earlier: "great
  sorrow and unceasing anguish", "has God rejected his people? By no
  means!", "the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable" (Romans 9 and
  11). It also says plainly that Christians later misused these words.
  Verse 29 is named as missing from the oldest manuscripts.
- *Philemon's IOU.* "I, Paul, write this with my own hand: I will repay it"
  (v. 19), the "useless, now useful" pun on the name Onesimus, and "prepare
  a guest room for me".
- *Unhindered.* The last word of Acts in Greek is *akōlytōs*. The episode
  ends on it: a man chained to a soldier, and the book's last word about him
  is "unhindered".

**Checked against the sources.** The adversarial reread changed seven
things before the pages were built. Paul writing "in big careful letters"
at the end of Philemon was Galatians 6:11, not Philemon, so it now says only
"in his own hand". The Lord standing by Paul (Acts 23:11) was "the night
after the riot" and is now the night after the council hearing. Gallio
"listened for about a minute" became "before Paul had even opened his
mouth", which is what Acts 18:14 says. Luke spending "almost a third" of
Acts on Paul's journeys was too low: chapters 13 to 28 are more than half.
Gaius of Rome's "trophy of the one who founded that church" now has his
plural: the trophies of Peter on the Vatican and Paul on the Ostian Way
(Eusebius 2.25). Paul's death is now "not far from" the Ostian road, since
the traditional site at Three Fountains is just off it. And the
handwriting line in the wind-down was fixed the same way as the first.

**Labelled as tradition.** Paul's release after the two years, a journey
west (Clement's "limits of the west", Eusebius 2.22), the second arrest, his
death under Nero, the tomb under Saint Paul Outside the Walls, Onesimus as a
later bishop, and the two Roman churches that each claim the rented house.

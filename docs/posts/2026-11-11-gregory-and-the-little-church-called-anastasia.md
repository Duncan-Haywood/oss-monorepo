[Gregory and the Little Church Called Anastasia](../../ventures/saints-by-candlelight/episodes/092-gregory-and-the-little-church-called-anastasia.md)
is episode 92 of Saints by Candlelight. An AI wrote it. It runs about 7,050

folders are still over the repository's 400 MB cap (423 MB today).

The series bible suggested it: episodes 89 and 90 told the lives of Saint
Basil the Great and his sister Saint Macrina, and Basil's best friend was the
obvious third. With Gregory the show now has all three Cappadocians. Saint
John Chrysostom would round out the Three Holy Hierarchs, so he is now first
on the "next up" list.

## A saint who wrote his own story

Most saints' episodes depend on a life written by someone else, often
centuries later. Gregory is different: he wrote a long poem in verse, *On His
Own Life*, in his retirement, and his sermons and letters fill in the rest.
That makes the record unusually rich, and also one-sided. He is not always
fair to people he quarrelled with, and the episode says so.

Things in the episode that come straight from his own writing:

- the storm off Cyprus on the way to Athens, which he says lasted twenty
  days, and the promise he made in it while he was still unbaptised;
- the "two roads" of Athens and "one soul in two bodies," from his funeral
  sermon for Basil;
- two teasing letters about Basil's hermitage in Pontus (no roof, no door,
  a fire that only smoked, bread the teeth slid off), then a sincere one;
- his father ordaining him by surprise, his flight, and his return at Easter;
- his description of Sasima, the dusty road junction Basil made him bishop
  of. It is one of the great complaints of ancient literature;
- the borrowed house in Constantinople he named the Anastasia, the
  Resurrection;
- the night Maximus the Cynic tried to get himself consecrated bishop,
  finished, Gregory says, in a flute player's house;
- the council of 381, where he resigned with the line about Jonah, and the
  farewell sermon.

## What is told as his report, and what we made up

Four things are told as Gregory's own account, not as history: his mother's
promise before his birth, the vow in the storm, the sun breaking through
the clouds as Theodosius led him into the great church, and the young man who
came to his sickbed to kill him and wept instead. The episode says each one
is his report.

The Sasima quarrel was left out of Basil's episode, but here it is the
emotional middle of the story. It is told gently and honestly: two saints
hurt each other, and the friendship survived it.

The episode invents one character, and says so three times: Melitine, an old
woman who sweeps the house church and doubts the shabby new bishop. Gregory's
quotations are given in our own plain paraphrase, not copied from any
published translation.

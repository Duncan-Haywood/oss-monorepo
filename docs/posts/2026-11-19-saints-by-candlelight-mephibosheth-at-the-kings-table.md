[Mephibosheth at the King's Table](../../ventures/saints-by-candlelight/episodes/150-mephibosheth-at-the-kings-table.md)
is episode 150 of Saints by Candlelight, the bedtime podcast of saints,
angels and patriarchs. An AI wrote it. It is text only, because the
creative audio folders are at 577 MB, over the creative track's 400 MB
line. The script is about 6,200 words, roughly forty minutes at the
narrator's pace. Episode 144 (Nathan and the ewe lamb) left it on the
series' list.

## Why this chapter

Second Samuel 9 is a short chapter that turns on one question. Kings in
David's world asked whether anyone was left of the old king's family, and
they asked for a bad reason. David asks "that I may show him kindness for
Jonathan's sake", and then a second time, "the kindness of God". The
Hebrew word both times is *hesed*, the same word Jonathan used when he
asked David, in First Samuel 20, never to cut off his kindness from his
house. The episode is built on that echo: a promise kept to a friend who
has been dead for years.

The rest of the shape comes from the book itself:

- **One verse, two chapters early.** Second Samuel 4:4 tells you that
  Jonathan's son was five when the news came from Jezreel, that his nurse
  picked him up and fled, and that he fell and was lame. The episode gives
  that verse a scene of its own, and leaves the nurse unnamed, as the book
  does.
- **"Do not be afraid" comes first.** David's first words to the man on the
  floor are not about land. The script keeps that order.
- **"A dead dog such as I".** The phrase is the one David used of himself
  to Saul in First Samuel 24, which episode 147 told. The episode points
  that out.
- **"Lame in both his feet".** The chapter ends on that line and does not
  pretend kindness healed him. The script ends its scene there too.

## Telling it straight

- **Who told the truth is left open.** Ziba says, in chapter 16, that
  Mephibosheth stayed behind hoping for his grandfather's kingdom.
  Mephibosheth, in chapter 19, says Ziba deceived him, and shows up
  unwashed and untrimmed from mourning. The book never rules. The narrator
  says which side most readers have taken and why, and says the book leaves
  it open.
- **Two hedges on names.** That Lo-debar sounds like "no thing" is used by
  Amos 6:13, so the pun is real, but the place's meaning and site are given
  as uncertain. That Merib-baal (First Chronicles 8:34) was the original
  name and "bosheth", shame, a scribal substitute is what many scholars
  think, and the script says that is all it is.
- **A first-draft slip.** The draft said "here is your servant" is what
  Samuel said when he was called in the night. Samuel says "here I am", a
  different phrase, so the line was cut. The re-read also replaced a claim
  that Ulam's archers in First Chronicles 8 were Jonathan's
  "great-great-grandchildren": by the list they are more than ten
  generations down.
- **The hard chapter is named, not told.** Second Samuel 21, where Saul's
  descendants are handed to the Gibeonites, is summed up in one sentence so
  that its verse 7, "the king spared Mephibosheth ... because of the oath of
  the Lord", can close the loop with episode 149.

The household at Lo-debar, the road to Jerusalem, the dinners and Mica in
the courtyards are invented, and the script says so in its welcome, its
notes and its front matter.

## What the wind-down follows

The quietest detail in the story is in Second Samuel 17. When David flees
Absalom across the Jordan, the man who brings beds, wheat, lentils and
cheese to him at Mahanaim is Machir son of Ammiel of Lo-debar, the man who
had hidden Saul's grandson from him. The book lists the food and moves on.
The episode stops on it, then follows Mica's line through First Chronicles
to the hundred and fifty archers, and ends with Luke 14's great dinner for
"the poor, the crippled, the lame and the blind".

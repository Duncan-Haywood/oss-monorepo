[Francis Xavier and the Ship Round the Cape](../../ventures/saints-by-candlelight/episodes/107-francis-xavier-and-the-ship-round-the-cape.md)
is episode 107 of Saints by Candlelight. An AI wrote it. It runs about 7,400
spoken words, roughly 48 minutes. Like every episode since 20 it is text only,
because the audio folders are still over the repository's 400 MB cap (423 MB
this run).

**Why this saint.** Episodes 103 (Ignatius) and 105 (Peter Faber) both told
the Paris rooming story from their side, and both left Francis Xavier at the
edge of the frame: the handsome, athletic roommate who kept his distance.
Episode 105 even says "that is a story for another night". The series list
had "Francis Xavier's Paris years and the road east" at the top. This episode
closes the triangle: the same room at Sainte-Barbe, told by the one who
resisted longest.

**The shape.** Most lives of Xavier hurry to India and Japan. This one spends
its middle on the part people skip: the voyage. He left Lisbon on his
thirty-fifth birthday, 7 April 1541, on the *Santiago* with the new governor,
wintered about six months at Mozambique (where he nursed the sick and caught
the fever himself), stopped at Malindi and Socotra, and reached Goa on
6 May 1542. Thirteen months on a wooden ship is a good bedtime setting. It
rolls left and right, the stars swing over the mast, and the story can slow
down without inventing anything. The later life (Malacca, the Moluccas, Japan,
the death on Shangchuan facing China) goes in the wind-down. The listing
proposes "part two: Japan and the island off China" for a later run.

**Where it comes from.** Mostly from his letters (Costelloe's translation,
1992), with Schurhammer's four-volume life for the voyage and the Navarre
background. The letters supply the Socotra Christians he wanted to stay with,
the bell in the streets of Goa, the Tamil prayers on the Fishery Coast, and
the January 1544 letter: arms worn out with baptising, and the wish to run
through the University of Paris shouting at men with "more learning than
love". All of it is given in our own paraphrase, flagged as such.

**Honesty flags.** Several of the best-known Xavier details come from early
Jesuit accounts, not from his own pen, and the episode says so each time they
come up. These are Ignatius's "what does it profit a man", the two-sentence
exchange on the evening Bobadilla fell ill ("This is your job." "Good. Here I
am."), the mended cassock, the cords round his legs on the walk to Venice,
washing his own clothes on board, and the companions' signatures carried by
his heart. The crab that returned his crucifix is called a legend outright.
The notes also say that the episode tells the Portuguese empire only from
where Francis stood, and that he complained sharply about it himself.

On the adversarial re-read I cut or softened three things I could not pin
down firmly enough. These were a precise count of how many times the doctors
bled him at Mozambique, a departure date from Rome, and the claim that he was
already teaching when Ignatius arrived in 1529 (he started a year later). I
also fixed an order of events that had the Pope's approval of the Jesuits
(September 1540) coming before Portugal's request (1539), and hedged Ignatius's
"six for Portugal leaves four for the world" as "supposed to have said".

**The invented part.** Tomé, a twelve-year-old ship's boy, seasick and
homesick, is ours and is labelled so in the welcome, the notes and the front
matter. He gets the conversation the episode turns on. He asks why a
nobleman would wash a soldier's shirt, then whether Francis is scared rounding
the Cape. The answer to the second, "the same hands as all of this", becomes
the line the wind-down hands to the listener.

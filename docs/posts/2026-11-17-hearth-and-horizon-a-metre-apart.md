[A Metre Apart](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/a-metre-apart.md)
is the newest story on Hearth & Horizon, the shelf of hopeful, hard-ish
science fiction. An AI wrote it. It runs about 5,300 words and is text only,
because the shelves' audio folders are over the 400 MB cap the creative track
keeps on committed MP3s.

**The story.** In 2046 an invented parish in east Galway sets out to fence
and bless the cillín on a farm: the corner of a field where, for generations,
infants who died before baptism were buried at night, usually by their
fathers. The farmer's aunt, eighty-eight, watched the lantern go down the
field in 1964. A licensed ground-penetrating radar survey finds nine graves,
and the parish asks the stonemason to cut "nine". The parish's records agent
says nine is a floor, not a count.

**The idea it turns on.** The first pass was a quick look: lines a metre
apart, run east to west because that was the easy way across the slope. But
Christian graves lie east to west too. A line running the same way as a grave
only sees it if it happens to fall within the grave's width. Take a cut about
35 cm wide, seen about 10 cm beyond each edge, and the line has to land in a
band 55 cm wide; with lines a metre apart, that happens for about 55 graves in
100. Nearly half lie between two lines and are never crossed. Nine seen at
0.55 each makes sixteen the likeliest total, and anything from twelve to
twenty-four unsurprising. The same grave lying across the lines would have
been crossed every time. The second survey runs north to south, 25 cm apart,
and finds fifteen inside the bank, plus two just outside it, where nobody had
looked.

**One piece of geometry.** The farmer wants his fence a metre outside the
bank; the archaeologist wants three metres. Move every part of any convex
fence outward by a distance d and it gets longer by 2πd, whatever its shape,
because going all the way round is one full turn. So the extra two metres cost
12.6 m of wire and four posts, and about a twenty-fifth of an acre of grass.

**What it does not claim.** Every person and place in the story is invented.
Cillíní, the "children's burial ground" class in Ireland's Record of
Monuments and Places, and the licence needed to use a detection device on a
recorded monument are real, stated from memory. The radar's speed in soil,
the 10 cm beam spread and the seventeen graves are assumptions or invention,
and the story's closing note shows every sum. The radar never says which
grave is whose, and the stone carries no number.

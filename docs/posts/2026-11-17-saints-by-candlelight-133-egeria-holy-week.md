[Egeria and the Great Week in Jerusalem](../../ventures/saints-by-candlelight/episodes/133-egeria-and-the-great-week-in-jerusalem.md)
is episode 133 of Saints by Candlelight. An AI wrote it. The script runs
about 8,000 words, roughly 50 minutes at the narrator's pace. Like episodes
30 to 132 it has no audio, because the audio folders hold 577 MB and the
creative prompt's line is 400 MB.

**Why this one.** The last five episodes followed Egeria's travels from
Sinai to Harran. The series list's one suggestion was the other half of her
letter: her description of how Jerusalem prayed, and above all the week
before Easter, which she says they called "the Great Week". No open pull
request was writing an episode, and `creative_due.py` drew this shelf.

**What the letter gives.** Chapters 29 to 39 are probably the most detailed
account of Holy Week that survives from the fourth century. The episode
keeps the details a reader would least expect:

- *Slowly, slowly.* On Palm Sunday the whole city walks down the Mount of
  Olives behind the bishop. The small children ride on their parents'
  shoulders and carry palm and olive. They walk the whole way to the tomb,
  great ladies included, and they go *lente et lente*, slowly, so nobody
  tires. The phrase comes back on Thursday night, when the crowd goes down
  to Gethsemane by the light of more than two hundred church candles. It
  became the episode's refrain.
- *Go home and sit down.* After the all-night vigil, at dawn on Good Friday,
  the bishop's sermon amounts to: you have toiled all night and will toil all
  day; go home, sit down for a little, and be back at the second hour.
- *The bite.* On Good Friday the people kiss the wood of the Cross one by
  one. The bishop holds its ends and deacons stand guard, because someone
  "is said" to have once bitten off a piece and stolen it. The narrator
  treats it gently. People who do that care too much, in the wrong way.
- *Three languages.* The bishop preaches in Greek, a priest repeats him in
  Syriac, and helpers explain in Latin (chapter 47). That is how the
  episode's invented companions can share a week with no common language.

**Honesty calls.**

- *No Latin this time.* The run that wrote episode 132 checked its chapters
  against The Latin Library. In this session that host, Latin Wikisource and
  Project Gutenberg were all refused by the network proxy. So the episode
  paraphrases the letter as it appears in the standard translations named in
  its sources. It quotes closely only the short phrases scholars agree on,
  and its notes say "our own plain rendering, shortened".
- *The relics.* The wood, the title, "the ring of Solomon and the horn from
  which the kings were anointed" are told as what the Jerusalem church kept
  and believed, not as proven. The story of Helena finding the Cross is
  named as a later account (Ambrose, 395) that Egeria does not tell.
  Cyril's line that the world was already "filled" with pieces of the wood
  comes from his own *Catechetical Lectures*.
- *The bishop.* Egeria names no bishops. The script calls him "very
  probably Saint Cyril", a historians' identification from the date.
- *The passion at bedtime.* The arrest and the death are told in the
  Gospels' few plain sentences. There is no scourging scene and no detail of
  the crucifixion.
- *Then and now.* The ending says palm processions, kissing the Cross and
  Lazarus Saturday continue today. It also says not all of that descends
  from Jerusalem or from her time. It claims only that she would recognise
  it.
- *Fixed in the edit.* A first draft said a monk copied the surviving pages
  "eight hundred years" after Valerius praised her. The manuscript is
  eleventh-century, so that became "about seven hundred years after she
  wrote it". The draft also had her describing "the Feast of the
  Ascension". Her chapter 42 is about the fortieth day after Easter, which
  she spends at Bethlehem and which scholars argue over, so the script now
  says only that.

**Invented, and labelled.** Maro, an old Syriac-speaking woman from a
village outside the city, and Yohannan, her five-year-old grandson, are
inventions. So are the honey cake and every word anyone says. The front
matter, the welcome and the notes all say so. Yohannan's olive branch from
Palm Sunday turns up again on Thursday night, by then "rather sad and
droopy". He takes it home on Easter Monday as a stick with three leaves.

**Next.** The list now suggests Egeria at Epiphany in Bethlehem (chapters
24 to 26). It also lists Lent with the bishop's catechesis, and Pentecost
and the dedication feast, where the manuscript breaks off mid-sentence.

# Gods of the first states: temples, divine kings and human sacrifice

What happened to religion when the first states and cities appeared? From about 3500 BC, in
Mesopotamia, Egypt, China, the Indus valley and later the Americas, religion became public,
professional and political. Gods owned cities, kings ruled as gods or on their behalf, priests
kept the first archives, and in several societies rulers killed people as offerings. This post
surveys the evidence for ancient religion in the first civilisations, flags where interpretation
outruns it, and reads the pattern through historical, scientific and theological lenses.

## Mesopotamia: the god's house and the first writing

In southern Mesopotamia the state grew up around the temple. The earliest proto-cuneiform tablets
come from the Eanna precinct at Uruk, about 3400–3200 BC, and most of them are bookkeeping for
large institutional households: rations, livestock, labour and land (Nissen, Damerow & Englund
1993, bibliographic). Writing, on this evidence, was invented largely to manage the gods'
estates. An older model in which the temple owned all the land has been abandoned: the temple was
one great household among several (Foster 1981, bibliographic).

Each city had a patron god who owned it: Enlil at Nippur, Nanna at Ur, and later Marduk at
Babylon. A city's fortunes were described as its god's (Jacobsen 1976, bibliographic). Kingship
was presented as a gift from heaven, but only a few kings, such as Naram-Sin of Akkad and Shulgi
of Ur, were deified. Most were the god's stewards (Brisch 2008, bibliographic). The Babylonian
creation epic *Enuma Elish*, in which Marduk defeats Tiamat and is acclaimed king of the gods, is
widely read as political theology for Babylon's rise (Lambert 2013, bibliographic).

The flood story in Tablet XI of the *Epic of Gilgamesh*, borrowed from the older *Atrahasis*,
parallels Genesis 6–9 closely: a divine decision to destroy humanity, a warned hero, a boat
sealed with pitch, birds sent out, a mountain landing and a sacrifice (George 2003; Lambert &
Millard 1969, both bibliographic). Almost all scholars posit literary dependence or a shared
tradition. Direction and mechanism are still debated. Many also read Genesis as a deliberate
monotheistic reworking, in which one God acts for moral reasons in place of gods irritated by
human noise (Frymer-Kensky 1977, bibliographic).

Thorkild Jacobsen proposed that Mesopotamians imagined the divine first as powers inside nature,
then as rulers, then as personal parents. Critics call the scheme too neat. It is a model, not
data (Jacobsen 1976; Bottéro 2001, both bibliographic).

At the Royal Cemetery of Ur, about 2600–2450 BC, "death pits" held dozens of attendants, 74 in
the largest. CT scans of two skulls found injuries at or near the time of death, and the bodies
had been treated and laid out in rows. That contradicts the old idea of a peaceful poison cup
([Baadsgaard et al. 2011](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/E44F1731C922EF9FF43E0B5D2DF67AC1)).
Mesopotamian sacrifice of this kind was episodic and tied to royal funerals.

## Egypt: the king, ma'at and the afterlife

Pharaonic kingship treated the living king as Horus and the dead king as Osiris. Most
Egyptologists now think the *office* was divine while the person was human (Frankfort 1948;
Posener 1960, both bibliographic). The king's task was to uphold *ma'at*, a goddess and a
principle of cosmic, social and moral order (Assmann 1990, bibliographic). The afterlife texts
began as a royal preserve in the Pyramid Texts, about 2350 BC, and later appeared on non-royal
coffins and papyri. Spell 125 of the Book of the Dead weighs the heart against ma'at. Whether
this was a "democratisation of the afterlife" or a late textual trace of older hopes is
disputed (Smith 2017, bibliographic). The First Dynasty royal tombs at Abydos are ringed by
subsidiary graves, about 200 around the tomb of Djer by one overview, and many scholars read
them as retainers killed at the king's burial. Direct evidence for the cause of death is
limited, and the practice disappears after the First Dynasty
([ANE Today](https://anetoday.org/human-sacrifice-near-east/)).

Akhenaten (about 1353–1336 BC) promoted the sun disc, the Aten, as sole god, closed access to
it except through the royal family, and had the name of Amun hacked out. The reform collapsed
after his death. Whether it was true monotheism, a royal monolatry or something else is still
argued (Hornung 1999; Redford 1984, both bibliographic). Jan Assmann proposed that Akhenaten and
the remembered figure of Moses introduced something new, the "Mosaic distinction" between true
and false religion, which replaced a world in which gods could be translated across cultures.
Assmann frames this as a history of memory, not a claim of historical dependence. Critics,
including theologians, charged that it tied monotheism to intolerance, and he later qualified it
(Assmann 1997, 2010, bibliographic).

## China: oracle bones and the Mandate of Heaven

The late Shang state at Anyang (about 1250–1046 BC) was governed partly through divination. Heat
was applied to turtle shells and cattle shoulder blades, the cracks were read, and the questions
were often carved on them. These are the earliest Chinese writing (Keightley 1978,
bibliographic). Royal ancestors received a scheduled cycle of sacrifices. Above them was a high
god, Di, though some scholars argue "Di" was a collective term for ancestral powers (Eno 2009,
bibliographic).

The sacrifices included people. Christina Cheung estimates that more than 13,000 people were
sacrificed at Yinxu over about 200 years, often 50 at a time, and that more than 2,500
sacrificial pits lie in the royal cemetery area
([Sapiens](https://www.sapiens.org/archaeology/chinese-oracle-bones-history/)). That is an
extrapolated estimate. An isotope study of 68 victims found non-local diets early in life and
local diets in the years before death, which suggests captives were held for years before being
killed ([Cheung et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaa.2017.05.006)).

The Zhou, who conquered the Shang about 1046 BC, justified their rule by the Mandate of Heaven:
Heaven withdrew its favour from the Shang for drunkenness and cruelty and gave it to the virtuous
Zhou kings (Shaughnessy 1999, bibliographic). Divine favour became *conditional on moral
conduct*. Mass human sacrifice largely ended under the Zhou, though not entirely. Michael Puett
cautions against a tidy story of magical Shang and ethical Zhou (Puett 2002, bibliographic).

## The Indus and Vedic India

The Indus civilisation (mature phase about 2600–1900 BC) is the cautionary case. Its script is
undeciphered, and some argue it is not a full writing system at all (Farmer, Sproat & Witzel 2004;
Rao et al. 2009, both bibliographic). There are no unambiguous temples or palaces. John
Marshall's 1931 reading of a seal figure as a "proto-Shiva" was widely accepted for two
generations and is no longer a consensus. Competing readings include a female figure, a
buffalo-man and others ([Pashupati seal summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashupati_seal)).
Claims that Shiva worship is 5,000 years old run ahead of the evidence. The honest statement is
that we do not know what Indus religion was.

Vedic religion is known from the Rigveda, orally composed probably about 1500–1200 BC (Jamison &
Brereton 2014, bibliographic). It centres on fire sacrifice (*yajña*) to gods such as Agni and
Indra, performed by hereditary priests, and on the ritual drink *soma*, whose botanical identity
is unknown.

## The Americas: independent invention

The New World matters because it had no contact with the Old. At Tenochtitlan, the Aztec skull
tower known as the Huei Tzompantli has yielded more than 600 skulls since excavation began in 2015,
from men, women and at least three children. That overturns the assumption that the victims were
all warrior men
([Smithsonian 2020](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/new-find-brings-skulls-discovered-aztec-tower-over-600-180976543/)).
The archaeologist Raúl Barrera Rodríguez said the dead were "turned into gifts for the gods". The
bioarchaeology confirms sacrifice on a large scale, but not the largest figures from Spanish
chronicles, which most scholars consider inflated. In the Andes, hair analysis of Inca children
sacrificed on the 6,739-metre volcano Llullaillaco shows coca and alcohol intake escalating in the
year before death
([Wilson et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1305117110)). The authors raise, without
settling, questions of consent, coercion and compliance. These cases are typologically archaic
states, not contemporary with the others: they date to the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries AD.

## Did sacrifice build hierarchy?

Watts and colleagues (2016) tested the "social control" idea with 93 Austronesian societies. Forty
practised ritual human sacrifice: 67% of highly stratified societies, 37% of moderately
stratified ones and 25% of egalitarian ones. Victims were usually low-status and sacrificers
high-status ([MPI press release](https://www.shh.mpg.de/166222/humansacrifices)). The
phylogenetic models suggested sacrifice helped societies gain high stratification and keep it
([Watts et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature17159)). A statistician's blog critique
noted that the key Bayes factor, 3.78, is not overwhelming
([Robert 2016](https://www.r-bloggers.com/2016/05/reversible-chainsaw-massacre/amp/)), and the
study covers one language family. The notes found no formal published reply.

## Through the lenses

### Historical criticism and history of religions

Trigger's comparison of seven early civilisations concluded that none separated religion from
rule, and that the ruler as chief mediator with the supernatural was universal (Trigger 2003,
bibliographic). A defensible summary: the first states turned gods into city-owners and
king-makers, made ritual into administration, and made sacrifice a display of power over life.
The Mesopotamian evidence suggests religion organised the state materially, through temple
households, before it did so ideologically.

### Archaeology and bioarchaeology

The physical record is strongest on scale: tablets, pits, skulls and isotopes. It is weakest on
inner belief, above all in the Indus and among the Olmec. A fair account of sacrifice avoids both
denial and sensationalism. The Aztec bones refute the claim that sacrifice was a Spanish
invention, and they do not support the largest chronicle numbers.

### Cognitive and evolutionary science

These states fit what Harvey Whitehouse calls the "doctrinal" mode of religion: frequent,
routinised ritual, specialist priesthoods and canonical texts, able to bind large anonymous
populations (Whitehouse 2004, bibliographic). Their high gods were not clearly *moralising* in the
later sense, with partial exceptions in Egyptian ma'at and the Zhou Heaven. Part 5 takes up the
quantitative test of that sequence, and its retraction.

### Catholic theology

A Catholic reading has two resources here. Genesis, read alongside *Atrahasis*, can be taken as a
deliberate correction of the surrounding myths, in which one good God judges for moral reasons.
The notes observe that this confessional reading and the critical reading of literary borrowing
are not mutually exclusive. And the Catechism, while recognising a universal search for God,
adds that "in their religious behavior, however, men also display the limits and errors that
disfigure the image of God in them" (CCC §844). Applying that to Ur, the Shang pits or the Aztec
tzompantli is an interpretation, but it is consistent with the text. Where the lens strains: it
offers a moral judgement on archaic religion, not an explanation of why sacrifice tracked
hierarchy so closely. For that, it needs the social sciences.

### Orthodox and patristic theology

The Fathers shared by both Churches had a sharper vocabulary. Justin Martyr paired the "seed of
reason" sown in every people with the work of demons, who imitated and corrupted true worship
(*2 Apology* 8). Read that way, the first states show both halves: a real reaching for order and
justice (ma'at, the Mandate of Heaven) and cults of blood. A historian will treat "demons" as a
theological category outside the evidence. What the patristic frame shares with the historian is
a refusal to romanticise archaic religion.

## What we can say with confidence

- In independent civilisations, state formation came with priesthoods, temple institutions,
  sacred kingship and state-run divination.
- Human sacrifice is securely attested at Ur, Abydos, Shang Anyang, Tenochtitlan and among the
  Inca. It often declined as bureaucratic states matured.
- Writing in Mesopotamia and China arose largely in temple administration and royal divination.
- The Zhou Mandate of Heaven made divine favour conditional on the ruler's conduct.

## What remains open

- What Indus religion was.
- Whether Akhenaten's reform counts as monotheism, and whether it had any link to Israel.
  Assmann himself does not claim one.
- Whether sacrifice *caused* stratification or elites used whatever violent ritual was to hand.
- How Genesis relates to Mesopotamian flood traditions, in direction and mechanism.

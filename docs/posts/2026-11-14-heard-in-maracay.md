[Heard in Maracay](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/heard-in-maracay.md)
is the newest story on Hearth & Horizon, the hopeful science fiction shelf.
An AI wrote it. It runs about 5,500 words and is text only: the shelves'
audio folders are still over the repository's 400 MB cap (474 MB this run).

**The story.** In September 2053, two days after a hurricane, a hill parish
in Puerto Rico's Cordillera Central keeps in touch with its four mountain
chapels by amateur radio. The cell towers are down, the mountain repeater
is gone, and texts to the satellites wait in a queue for hours. The net runs
on 7.238 MHz, four times a day, on a schedule the parish's assistant drew up
and tested fourteen times, fourteen check-ins out of fourteen. At the nine
o'clock net on Monday night, all four chapels are silent. One of them
is where an 88-year-old man's oxygen concentrator is running on the chapel's
battery, and his great-niece, the chapel's operator, was going to read the
gauge on his only cylinder at nine.

**The idea the story turns on.** Near-vertical incidence skywave (NVIS).
A low wire antenna throws a 40-metre signal almost straight up, and the
ionosphere's F2 layer sends it back down over the mountains, but only while
the frequency is below the layer's critical frequency, foF2, which the Sun
keeps up by day and which falls all night. Above it, a signal going straight
up passes through. A signal meeting the layer at a slant still comes back,
up to about foF2 times the secant of the angle, so at night you hear Florida
and Colombia loudly and your own chapel 23 km away not at all. Hams say
the band has "gone long". All fourteen tests had been run between eight in
the morning and half past six in the evening.

**The numbers.** On the story's assumed, illustrative night (foF2 from 8.6
MHz at 18:00 down to 3.0 MHz before dawn), 7.238 MHz stops returning straight
down at 19:51 and comes back at 07:27. A station in Maracay, 893 km away,
receives the signal leaving the ground at about 31 degrees (secant 1.74 at
a 300 km reflection height), so that path stays open until foF2 falls below
4.16 MHz, at about 00:36. Miami, at 1,646 km and about 16 degrees, stays
open all night, but the chapel's antenna, six metres up, sends about a tenth
as much power at 16 degrees as straight up (about a third at 31), so the man
in Hialeah hears the parish's bell-tower antenna and not the chapel. That
is the failed first attempt; the relay through Venezuela is the second.
Then the battery and the cylinder: 1,100 psi on an E cylinder is about 374
litres, a little over three hours at 2 L/min, and turning the radio off
after the relay buys 22 more minutes of battery. The story's closing note
lists every figure.

**What it is about.** A plan whose test results were true and measured
the wrong thing: fourteen of fourteen, all at noon. The assistant says so
plainly and writes it into the plan where the next reader will see it. A
mother who wants to send the deacon up an unseen road at night, and a
retired lineman who tells her not to send a man up a mountain because a
radio is quiet. And silence chosen on purpose, when the girl on the hill
turns her radio off to save twenty watts for her great-uncle's breathing and
asks them not to come because they are quiet.

**Honesty notes.** The parish, the chapels, the people, the hurricane and the
operators in Maracay and Hialeah are invented. The ionosphere figures are
an assumed model of one quiet night, not a forecast, and the Sun being near
the quiet end of its cycle in 2053 is an extrapolation from its roughly
eleven-year rhythm. The antenna figures are for a dipole over perfect
ground; real ground and real paths lose more. The licence details (General
class voice on 40 m from 7.175 MHz, a call sign every ten minutes, the
safety-of-life provision) are the current US rules. Nothing in the story is
medical or emergency-planning advice.

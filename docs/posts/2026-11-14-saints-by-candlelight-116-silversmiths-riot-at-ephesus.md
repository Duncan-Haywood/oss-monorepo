[Paul and the Silversmiths in the Theatre at Ephesus](../../ventures/saints-by-candlelight/episodes/116-paul-and-the-silversmiths-in-the-theatre-at-ephesus.md)
is episode 116 of Saints by Candlelight. An AI wrote it. It runs about 7,500
spoken words, roughly 48 minutes. Like every episode since 20 it is text
only, because the audio folders are over the creative prompt's 400 MB line
(474 MB this run).

**Why this one.** Episode 115 left Apollos sailing to Corinth and Paul on his
way back to Ephesus, and the series list asked for the sequel: Acts 19. It
is the most crowded chapter in Acts. Twelve men who know only John's
baptism, three months in the synagogue, two years in the hall of Tyrannus,
healing through Paul's sweat rags, the seven sons of Sceva, a bonfire of
magic books worth fifty thousand pieces of silver, and then Demetrius the
silversmith filling a theatre built for about 25,000 people.

**The line it is built around.** Acts 19:32: "most of them did not know why
they had come together." The cold open is a fourteen-year-old apprentice
asking the man next to him what they are shouting about, two hours into
"Great is Artemis of the Ephesians", and being told "no idea". For a
bedtime show it is a gift: the funniest sentence in the chapter is also the
one with the point in it.

**What the episode is careful about.**

- *Nothing added to the speeches.* Demetrius's speech (prosperity first,
  trade second, the goddess third) and the town clerk's (he never argues
  about Artemis; he reminds the crowd that Rome punishes riots) are given in
  plain rendering with nothing added to what Luke reports.
- *Miracles are God's.* Luke writes that God did extraordinary miracles "by
  the hands of Paul". The words for the handkerchiefs and aprons are
  workmen's words, so the episode reads them as a tentmaker's work clothes.
- *The demon is ridiculous, not frightening.* "Jesus I know, and Paul I
  know, but who are you?" is told as the awkward silence it is, and the seven
  brothers leave without their cloaks. The point, that the name of Jesus is
  not a spell you can borrow, is said once and plainly.
- *Readings are labelled.* Why the Jews of Ephesus put Alexander forward,
  whether Romans 16:4 ("risked their necks") happened at Ephesus, and what
  Paul meant by fighting wild beasts there are all offered as open questions.
  The fifth-to-tenth-hour teaching day is named as Codex Bezae only. The
  hundred and thirty-seven years of wages behind fifty thousand pieces of
  silver assumes a drachma a day and says so.
- *Tradition is tradition.* Saint John and the Mother of God living at
  Ephesus is named as a tradition. The Council of Ephesus in 431, which both
  Catholic and Orthodox Christians hold, closes the wind-down: Cyril of
  Alexandria's letters describe the city walking the bishops home with
  torches, so the city that shouted for Artemis ends rejoicing over the
  Theotokos.

**Invented, and said so.** Doris, the widow with the bracelets and the
sparrows from episode 115, returns; her grandson Hermon, a silversmith's
apprentice with a cough, is new. Their arc runs beside the verses without
touching them, and ends with Doris deciding to knock instead of listening
through the keyhole.

**A detail worth stealing.** Luke uses *ekklesia* for the rioting crowd in
the theatre (19:32, 39, 41), the same word Christians use for the Church. The
episode lets the two assemblies sit side by side: twenty thousand people who
do not know why they came, and a workshop full of people who do.

Next on the list: the farewell to the Ephesian elders at Miletus, with

[Elizabeth and the Mule Between the Armies](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/elizabeth-and-the-mule-between-the-armies.md)
is the newest story on Little Lamps, the shelf of saints' stories for ages
3 to 8. An AI wrote it. It runs about 1,300 words, roughly nine minutes read
aloud. It is text only, because the creative audio folders are at 474 MB
and runs stop adding MP3s above 400 MB.

**Why this one.** `platform/ci/creative_due.py` drew Little Lamps for this
session, and the series bible's one open Little Lamps proposal was
Elizabeth of Portugal (feast 4 July). No open pull request was writing it.

**The story.** Elizabeth is an Aragonese princess married at about twelve
to King Denis of Portugal. She feeds the hungry and builds hospitals, and
she ends up as the family's peacemaker. Then her son Afonso raises an army
against his father. The scene the story is built around is the usual
account of Alvalade, outside Lisbon, in 1323. The queen rides a mule into
the gap between the two armies and asks her husband and son to stop. They
don't fight that day, and the war ends in 1324. She dies at Estremoz in
1336, in the July heat, on one last trip to stop another war.

**What took care.** Children's saints' stories tend to blur three things,
and this one tries to keep them apart:

- *Two Elizabeths, one miracle.* The bread that turned into roses is told
  of Elizabeth of Portugal, and much earlier of her great-aunt Elizabeth of
  Hungary, who already has a Little Lamps story. The new story tells it once
  as "it is told" and notes that it runs in the family.
- *A marriage that wasn't happy.* Denis had children outside the marriage,
  and for a while he suspected his wife of siding with their son and
  confined her at Alenquer. For three-year-olds the story says only that he
  "was not always kind to her" and that he once sent her away. The full
  account is in the note for grown-ups.
- *What she said.* No source records her words on the field at Alvalade,
  so the story gives none, and the note says the scene's details are ours.

The note for grown-ups also covers the 1304 arbitration with her brother
James II of Aragon, her pilgrimage to Compostela with her crown, her life as
a Franciscan tertiary (not a nun) beside the Poor Clares at Coimbra, and her
canonization in 1625. It names what was left out: the incorrupt body and
the tradition that she founded the Holy Spirit festivals.

[The White Clay](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/the-white-clay.md)
is the newest Hearth & Horizon story. An AI wrote it. It is set in the winter
of 2057 to 2058 on an invented hallig, one of the small undiked marsh islands
in the North Frisian Wadden Sea. Halligen are real. They flood in winter
storms ("Land unter") while their houses stand on artificial mounds. It is
the shelf's first story set on the North Sea coast.

The state offers to raise the hallig's low summer dike by 40 centimetres. The
island's flood assistant recommends it, because its contract measures harm in
Landunter days, and a higher dike means fewer of them. Then the school cores
the squares of white clay it laid fourteen months earlier, and the numbers
point the other way.

**The marker.** A thin layer of white feldspar clay spread on a marsh is a
real way to measure how fast the marsh is growing: come back later, cut a
plug, and measure the mud above the white line. In the story, the meadows
behind the dike gained about 1.3 mm and the open fringe outside it about
4.5 mm. Those readings are invented, and so are the rates below. They were
chosen to match each other, not taken from any survey.

**Mud per flood.** A storm flood 1.2 m deep carrying 300 mg of sediment per
litre, with 40% settling, leaves 144 g/m². A moderate flood 0.4 m deep at
250 mg/L, with 60% settling, leaves 60 g/m². At a dry density of
0.75 g/cm³, those are about 0.19 mm and 0.08 mm.

**Per year.** Five storm floods over the dike give the meadows 0.96 mm. The
fringe also gets about thirty moderate floods, for 3.36 mm. Gates in the
dike, open October to March, would let in 24 of those 30: 2.88 mm. A dike
raised 40 cm lets in about two storm floods: 0.38 mm.

**Against the sea.** The story assumes the sea rises 5.5 mm a year by then
(the real global figure for 2006 to 2018 was about 3.7). Over forty years
that leaves the meadows about 18 cm lower relative to the sea as things
stand, 20 cm with the raised dike and 10 cm with winter gates. No option
keeps pace. The gates halve the loss.

**The number that is not the thing.** With the gates, the flood count in the
state report goes from about 5 a winter to about 29, nearly six times
"worse". The assistant's correction is to report both figures side by side,
floods and millimetres. Its line in the council's letter is the story's
point: "A hallig that floods less and grows less is not safer; it is only
drier for a while."

The story also has an old farmer who reads the winter layers in a ditch wall,
the Biike fires on 21 February, and a Mass for the Chair of Saint Peter
("upon this rock") on an island with no rock, only mud. Every rate in it is
labelled invented in the closing note, and the weekdays were checked with a
script. It is text only for now: the shelf's audio folder is over its size
cap.

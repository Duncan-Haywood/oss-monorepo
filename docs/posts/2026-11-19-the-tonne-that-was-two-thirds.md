The newest [Hearth & Horizon](https://marisstella.app/hearth-and-horizon/the-tonne-that-was-two-thirds.html) story is set in the winter of 2049 in an invented wine town in La Mancha. The town's cooperative has stopped burning its vine prunings in the field. It turns them into biochar in two open steel kilns and sells the carbon the char locks away to a buyer. Its first claim is a tonne of carbon dioxide for every tonne of prunings. The figure comes from the kiln maker's brochure, and the verifier won't pay for a number nobody weighed.

The story turns on one sum, and the sum is worth setting out, because the same brochure figure turns up in real sales talk.

## Three numbers multiplied together

How much carbon dioxide a tonne of dry feedstock removes is the product of three fractions and a constant:

1. **Char yield**: the share of the dry feedstock's weight that comes out as char. The story takes 27 per cent for a well-run flame-curtain kiln, an open cone in which a layer of flame burns off the smoke and keeps air away from the char beneath it.
2. **Carbon content of the char**: 77 per cent in the story. That is the IPCC's default for wood char made by pyrolysis, in the *2019 Refinement to the 2006 IPCC Guidelines*, Volume 4, Appendix 4.
3. **Permanence**: the share of the char's carbon expected to still be in the soil after a century. The same appendix gives it by production temperature: about 0.65 below 450 °C, 0.80 from 450 to 600 °C and 0.89 above 600 °C.
4. **The constant 44/12**: carbon dioxide weighs 44/12 times as much as the carbon in it.

Dry vine prunings are taken as 47 per cent carbon. So a dry tonne gives 1,000 × 0.27 × 0.77 × 0.89 × 44/12, which is about 678 kg of carbon dioxide. Take off about 15 kg for kiln methane and the tractor's diesel and you get about 663 kg: two-thirds of the brochure's tonne, on a good day.

The brochure's figure fails a simple check. All the carbon in a dry tonne of prunings, burned to carbon dioxide, comes to about 1,723 kg. To keep one tonne of that out of the air for a century, the kiln would have to lock away 58 per cent of the wood's carbon for good. A good burn in the story puts 44 per cent of it into the char, and about 39 per cent stays.

## The burn that removed seventeen kilograms

The cooperative's first weighed burn goes wrong in the usual ways. The prunings were cut two days earlier and are 41 per cent water. The kiln is loaded in heaps, so the fire smoulders instead of flaming. And the char is put out with buckets from the top, so the bottom of the load glows all night under the crust and burns to ash. A trailer of 1,840 kg (about 1,086 kg dry) leaves 119 kg of char, an 11 per cent yield. The kiln never got hot, so the char is low-temperature. And smouldering makes methane, which over a century warms about 28 times as much as the same weight of carbon dioxide. With the story's invented methane default, the whole trailer nets about 17 kg, less than a bag of cement.

The fix in the story is old craft, not new equipment. An 84-year-old man who burned charcoal in earth mounds in the Montes de Toledo as a boy shows them how. Use prunings dried for four weeks in windrows. Add one thin layer at a time, each time the last one has a skin of grey ash, so the flame stays on top as a lid. Drown the char completely, stir it and drown it again, then hold a hand over it in the morning. They also need records: every load weighed on the cooperative's grape weighbridge, which sits idle from November to August, oven-dried samples to get the dry weight, a laboratory carbon test and a thermocouple log. Without the log the method books a burn at the medium-temperature factor, and the cooperative books its unlogged burns that way rather than guess high.

The season comes to 118 dry tonnes and 77.3 tonnes of verified removal. At the story's invented price of 150 euros a tonne, that is 11,595 euros against the brochure's 17,700. It still pays for the kilns.

The story is fiction. The price, the methane figures and the verification rules are invented, and nothing in it is advice on carbon markets or biochar production.

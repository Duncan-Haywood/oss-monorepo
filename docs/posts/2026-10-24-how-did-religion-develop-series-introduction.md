# How did religion develop? A series that reads the evidence through many lenses

How did religion begin, and how did it change into the religions people practise today? This
series follows the development of religion from the cognitive science of belief and the first
burials, through Göbekli Tepe, the first states and the Axial Age, to the origins of monotheism,
early Christianity, the East–West Schism and the 2020s data on whether religion is declining.

Each stage of that history can be looked at in more than one way. An archaeologist sees pits,
pigments and pillars. A cognitive scientist sees minds that detect agents and look for purpose.
A historical critic sees texts with authors, dates and agendas. A sociologist sees institutions,
cohorts and survey curves. A Catholic or Orthodox theologian sees a creature made for God,
seeking him "among shadows and images", in the Catechism's phrase. These are not always
rival answers to one question. Often they answer different questions about the same evidence.

So every post in this series looks at one stage through several labelled lenses, and says
honestly where each lens fits the evidence and where it strains. The series takes the
theological lenses as seriously as the scientific ones. That does not mean treating them as
interchangeable. It means stating each one in the form its best defenders would recognise, and
then testing it against what the record actually shows.

## The lenses

The same labels appear in every post:

- **Cognitive and evolutionary science**: how human minds acquire, remember and pass on
  religious ideas, and whether religion is a by-product of other mental capacities or an
  adaptation.
- **Archaeology**: what the material record shows (burials, images, buildings, bones) and the
  chain of inference from objects to beliefs.
- **Historical criticism**: dating and reading texts as human documents, with sources,
  redactions and contexts.
- **Sociology and quantitative history**: institutions, populations, surveys and cross-cultural
  databases.
- **Catholic theology**: the Church's magisterial documents (Vatican I, Vatican II, the
  Catechism) and Catholic thinkers, from Justin Martyr and Augustine to Newman.
- **Orthodox theology**: the Orthodox Church's own voices, such as Florovsky, Schmemann,
  Afanasiev and the 2016 Holy and Great Council, which often frame the same questions
  differently from Rome.

Not every lens appears in every post. Where a lens has little to say about a stage, the post
says so rather than inventing something for it to say.

## How citations and uncertainty are marked

The series draws on eight sets of research notes, one per stage, in the
[religion-origins research project](/research/religion-origins/). The notes record, for every
claim, whether the source was opened and checked or cited from its standard bibliographic
record. The posts keep that distinction:

- Citations are inline, by author and year, with a link where the notes give one. Each post
  ends with a list of its sources and a link to the notes file it draws on.
- **"(bibliographic)"** after a citation means the work is cited from its standard
  bibliographic record and was not opened while the notes were compiled. No precise figure or
  quotation in the series rests on a work marked this way. Its main argument is described in
  paraphrase only.
- Quotation marks are used only for wording the notes record from a source that was actually
  read. Everything else is paraphrase.
- Where the notes found a discrepancy (two sources disagree, or a secondary summary misstates a
  primary paper), the post follows the primary source and says so.
- Each post ends with two short lists: what we can say with confidence, and what remains open.
  A contested view is never presented as settled, on any side.

Three corrections from the notes come up often enough to state here:

- The Seshat "Big Gods" paper in *Nature* (2019) was retracted in 2021. Its authors' revised
  analysis is in *Religion, Brain & Behavior* 13(2), published online in 2022.
- Lightner, Bendixen and Purzycki (2023, *Evolution and Human Behavior*) argue that moralistic
  supernatural punishment is probably *not* associated with social complexity.
- The UK "Quiet Revival" survey was found in 2026 to contain fraudulent respondents. YouGov
  apologised and said it would rerun the survey.

## What this series is not

It is not new data analysis. Every number comes from a published source that the notes cite. It
is not an argument for or against any religion. The series' working assumption is that a
causal account of how a belief arose does not settle whether the belief is true. Philosophers
on both sides largely accept that, and Part 1 explains why. It also assumes that a
theological claim made in the language of history has to meet history's evidence.

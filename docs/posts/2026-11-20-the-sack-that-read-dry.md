## The story

In 2049 a parish grain bank in Makueni County, Kenya, buys maize at harvest
and lends it back in the hungry months. It buys only maize that reads 13
percent moisture or drier, a stricter line than the East African standard's
13.5. The storekeeper has read every sack himself for eleven years. In May,
in the long rains, the bottom of the stack turns musty, and a county test
finds aflatoxin at 46 micrograms per kilogram, over four times the legal
limit of 10.

Nobody lied. The storekeeper's method is the one in capitals on the meter's
card. The parish's ledger agent finds the problem in a column nobody had
looked at: the time of each reading. Every sack was read between 1:40 and
5:10 in the afternoon, from the top cup, after hours standing open in the
sun. The small print on the same card says hot grain may read low.

## The real things under it

- **The 2004 outbreak.** From January to July 2004, acute aflatoxin
  poisoning in Kenya's Eastern and Central Provinces caused 317 reported
  cases and 125 deaths by 20 July, most of them in Makueni, Kitui,
  Machakos and Thika districts. The CDC linked it to home-grown maize
  stored damp (MMWR 53(34), 3 September 2004). Anyone in Makueni old enough
  remembers it, and the story's storekeeper does.
- **The standard.** EAS 2, the East African Standard for maize grain, sets
  13.5 percent maximum moisture and 10 µg/kg total aflatoxin (5 for B1
  alone).
- **The meter.** Extension advice from Purdue University and North Dakota
  State University says capacitance meters underestimate hot or freshly
  dried grain by one to two points, and says to cool a sample in a sealed
  container before reading it.

## The number in the story

The story's model is invented and says so. A sun-warmed top-of-sack reading
comes out about 2 points below a cool morning reading from the middle of the
same sack, so a veranda line of 13 lets in sacks up to about 15 in the
middle. Of the model's 320 stacked sacks, 124 (about 39 percent) would fail a
cool middle reading, and 42 are over 14. That is the theme the shelf keeps
returning to: a number can be honest and still not be the thing it was meant
to measure.

The fix is cheap and dull, which is the point: read in the morning, from the
middle with a sampling spear, let the jar cool, and send any doubtful sack
back to the drying floor instead of into the stack.

The story is fiction, not food-safety advice. Grain you suspect is
contaminated should be tested by your local agriculture or public health
office before anyone eats it.

[Nicodemus and the Conversation by Night](../../ventures/saints-by-candlelight/episodes/177-nicodemus-and-the-conversation-by-night.md)
is episode 177 of Saints by Candlelight, the bedtime podcast of saints,
angels and patriarchs. An AI wrote it. Like every episode since number 30,
it is text only: the creative audio folders hold 581 MB and the creative
track's limit is 400 MB. The script is 7,092 spoken words, about
forty-six minutes at the narrator's pace. Episode 176, the bronze serpent,
put it on the list, because John 3:14 is where Jesus brings that serpent up
with Nicodemus.

## Three scenes, one man

Nicodemus appears only in John, and only three times: the night
conversation (3:1-21), one sentence in the council at the Feast of
Tabernacles (7:50-51), and the burial (19:39-42). The Gospel never says he
believed. It shows him moving instead: from the dark, to a single fair
question asked in a hostile room, to the foot of the cross in daylight with
a king's quantity of spices. The episode keeps that shape and does not
fill in the gap with a conversion scene. Its last line on him is the linen
cloths he wound on Friday, lying empty on Sunday (20:5-7).

## The wind does the work

Two words carry the night scene. *Anothen* means both "again" and "from
above", and Nicodemus hears only the first. *Pneuma*, like Hebrew *ruach*,
is wind, breath and spirit at once, so "the wind blows where it wills" is
also a sentence about the Spirit. A bedtime script cannot footnote that, so
it gives the wind a job. An invented young man of the household, Tobiah,
carries a clay lamp that the gusts keep flattening on the way there. On the
way home he asks where the wind comes from. Nicodemus answers that nobody
knows, "but you can hear it". The lamp comes back in the courtyard on Holy
Saturday. Tobiah waits at the foot of the stairs and hears nothing of the
conversation, so the invention adds nothing to what Jesus or Nicodemus say.
The welcome and the notes both mark him as invented.

## Being fair to the Pharisees and the council

A story about a Pharisee is easy to tell badly. The setting-the-scene
section explains what a Pharisee was (devout, learned, serious about the
law at the kitchen table as well as the temple) before the story starts.
The council's jibe at 7:52 is given straight, but the people the episode
admires most in that scene are the temple officers who came back without
their prisoner: "No man ever spoke like this man." Joseph of Arimathea, who
gets Mark's "took courage" and Luke's "had not consented", stands with
Nicodemus as the second member of the council who came at the end.

## What the re-read changed

- A Roman *litra* is about 327 grams, so a hundred of them is about 33 kg,
  about 72 modern pounds. The first draft said thirty-four kilograms and
  seventy-five pounds. It now says thirty-three and seventy.
- Ecclesiastes 11:5 was first quoted with only the wind. The verse pairs
  the way of the wind with how bones grow in a mother's womb, which fits
  Nicodemus's "can he enter a second time into his mother's womb" better.
  The fuller verse is now quoted.
- "Peter and John came running" became "Peter and the other disciple, the
  one Jesus loved", which is what John 20 actually says.
- "Ruler of the Jews almost certainly means the Sanhedrin" became "very
  probably". "Saint Paul said so with pride" lost "with pride", since
  Philippians 3 goes on to count it as loss.

## Labelled as what they are

Where the conversation happened and why it was at night are not in the
Gospel. The script says so, gives Augustine's reading (Tractates on John
11-12: he came in the dark because he was still in it) and a gentler one,
and does not choose between them. It also says that scholars do not agree
where Jesus's words end and the evangelist's reflection begins in 3:16-21.
Three later traditions are told as legend or as uncertain. One is
Lucian's letter of 415 about a dream that placed Nicodemus's grave beside
Saint Stephen's, which is behind the Orthodox 2 August commemoration.
Another is the apocryphal *Gospel of Nicodemus*. The third is the
Talmud's Nakdimon son of Gurion, whose story about borrowed wells and rain
(Taanit 19b-20a) suits an episode about water and wind, but who may well
be a different man.

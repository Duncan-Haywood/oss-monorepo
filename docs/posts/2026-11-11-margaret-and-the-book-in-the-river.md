[Margaret and the Book in the River](../../ventures/saints-by-candlelight/episodes/091-margaret-and-the-book-in-the-river.md)
is episode 91 of Saints by Candlelight. An AI wrote it. It runs about 6,150
words, roughly 40 minutes spoken. For now it is text only, because the audio
folders are still over the repository's 400 MB cap (423 MB today).

Her feast falls on 16 November, five days after this post, and no episode had
covered her yet. The series bible had also asked for more saints' lives after
a long run of Scripture episodes. Most saints' episodes rest on a legend
written down centuries later. Margaret is unusual: almost everything known
about her comes from one man who knew her well.

## One close witness

Turgot was her priest and confessor, later prior of Durham and bishop of
Saint Andrews. Her daughter Edith, who became Queen Matilda of England, asked
him to write down what he remembered, and he wrote it as a letter to her.
Most of the episode's scenes come from that letter:

- every morning, nine small orphans fed on the queen's knee with her own spoon;
- money taken from the king's treasury for the poor, with the king pretending
  not to see, and sometimes catching her and laughing as he threatened to put
  her on trial;
- a king who could not read picking up her books, kissing them, and having
  the ones she loved bound in gold and jewels;
- church meetings where the queen spoke English, the clergy spoke Gaelic, and
  the king sat between them and interpreted;
- three hundred poor fed in the royal hall during Advent and Lent, with the
  king and queen serving them;
- English captives found and ransomed one by one;
- houses on both shores of the Forth and free boats for pilgrims going to
  Saint Andrews;
- her last day, the news of her husband's and son's deaths, and the prayer
  from the Mass that she died saying.

The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle adds a detail Turgot leaves out: at first she
refused to marry Malcolm and said she would rather give her life to God.
Archbishop Lanfranc's reply to her request for monks also survives.

## The book in the river

Turgot tells of one wonder. Her favourite Gospel book fell into a river from
a traveller's bundle at a ford, lay open on the riverbed, and came out with
its pages unharmed. The episode tells this as his report, as the show's
rules require for any miracle story.

The unusual part is that the book still exists. The Bodleian Library in Oxford
bought a small, worn Gospel book at a sale in the nineteenth century. An old
Latin poem at the front tells the same story of the fall into the river and
says the book belonged to Margaret and the king. Very few bedtime stories can
send a listener to a library catalogue.

## What is labelled, and what is invented

Some things are not in Turgot, and the episode labels them as tradition or as
a later chronicler's story: her landing at Saint Margaret's Hope, the
Queensferry name, the cave in Dunfermline, the castle chapel as her own
chapel (it was probably built by her son David), and the sea mist that later
writers say hid her funeral procession. In the self-review pass before
merging, three claims came out because no source in hand supported them. One
was a "grace after meals" custom, which is folk tradition and not in Turgot.
The other two were a date for the poem's handwriting and a claim that the
Oxford pages show water stains. The slug also lost a river name, because
Turgot never says which river it was.

The episode names her tradition plainly. Margaret lived after the break
between East and West in 1054 and was canonised in Rome in 1250, so her
feast is in the Catholic calendar. The bible's rule is to state this and
claim nothing more.

The episode invents three people and says so: Bethoc, a four-year-old orphan
who grows up to work in the queen's embroidery room; Aed, the ferryman who
tells pilgrims the crossing costs nothing; and Wulfric, the servant who drops
the book and runs back for it.

[Little Lamps](https://marisstella.app/hearth-and-horizon/) has a new story: [*Rita and the Rose in the Snow*](https://marisstella.app/hearth-and-horizon/rita-and-the-rose-in-the-snow.html). It runs about 2,000 words, roughly ten minutes read aloud, for ages 3 to 8. Saint Rita was a wife and mother in the hills of Umbria. After her husband was killed in a family feud and her two sons died, she made peace between the families and was taken in by the Augustinian nuns at Cascia. Her feast is on 22 May, and many churches bless roses that day.

## Why peace is the thread

The oldest *Life* of Rita, printed in the *Acta Sanctorum*, says her elderly parents were so good at settling quarrels that people called them the "Peacemakers of Jesus Christ". The Vatican News account says the peace between the two families came before the nuns let her in. That made the shape of the story. A child watches her parents make peace, forgives when it is hardest, and then does the same work herself, because the convent door will not open until the quarrel ends. The knocking three times is ours. The refusal and the reason for it, that the nuns feared the feud, are in the sources.

## Two sets of dates

The sources disagree by ten years. Vatican News, following the sanctuary at Cascia, gives about 1371 to 1447. Many other references give 1381 to 1457, and the 1912 *Catholic Encyclopedia* gives 1386 to 1456. The series bible's proposal had used 1381 to 1457. The story itself gives no years, and the note for grown-ups lists all three.

## What we softened

Two traditions are too hard for a bedtime story, so we left them out:

- **The prayer for her sons.** The *Catholic Encyclopedia*, and an 18th-century writer, say Rita prayed that God would take her sons rather than let them commit murder to avenge their father. Soon after, both died of illness. A three-year-old should not hear that a mother prayed for her children to die. The story keeps the root of the tradition: she prayed they would not grow up angry. The note gives the fuller version, so a parent can decide.
- **The bloodstained shirt.** Vatican News says she hid her husband's shirt so the boys would not seek revenge. It is a vivid detail, but not for this age.

The thorn wound on her forehead, the way she is usually pictured, is also left to the note. Explaining it gently to a small child takes more than one line.

## Legend, marked as legend

Three of the story's best moments come from lives written 150 years or more after her death. These are the white bees round her cradle, the dead vine the abbess told her to water, and the rose and two figs found in the snow at Roccaporena in January. The story calls each one "an old story". For the vine, there is a small fact to add: a vine does grow in the cloister at Cascia today, but it is said to date from the 19th century. So the story says it is not the same plant.

[Monica and the Son of These Tears](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/monica-and-the-son-of-these-tears.md)
is the newest story on the Little Lamps shelf, the saints' bedtime stories
for ages 3 to 8. An AI wrote it. It runs about 1,200 words, around nine
minutes read aloud, followed by a short prayer and a note for grown-ups.
It is text only, because the shelf's audio folders are over the
repository's 400 MB cap (468 MB this run).

**Why Monica.** Most saints on this shelf come to us through lives written
by someone else, often centuries later, so the note for grown-ups spends
most of its time sorting history from legend. Monica is different. Nearly
everything we know about her is in one book written by someone who was
there: her son Augustine's *Confessions*, finished about ten years after
she died. So this story can be told almost entirely from a primary source,
and the note can give a book and chapter for each scene instead of a
"some say".

**The scenes, and where they are.**

- Her patience with a hot-tempered husband, Patricius, who was baptised
  before he died (9.9).
- The years of tears, and the dream of the wooden rule: a shining young
  man tells her "where you are, he is too", and she sees Augustine standing
  beside her (3.11). Augustine tried to read it the other way round, that
  she would come over to him, and she corrected him at once. He says her
  quick answer moved him more than the dream did.
- The bishop who would not argue with Augustine and, when she kept
  pleading, sent her off saying it could not be that the son of these tears
  should perish (3.12). The story paraphrases the line and says so.
- Augustine slipping away from Carthage by ship at night while she prayed
  in a chapel by the shore (5.8), and her crossing after him, comforting the
  sailors in a storm (6.1).
- Milan, Ambrose, the child's voice in the garden ("take up and read"),
  and the baptism at Easter 387 (6.2, 8.12, 9.6).
- The window at Ostia, where mother and son talked about heaven, and her
  last request: lay my body anywhere, only remember me at the altar of the
  Lord (9.10 to 9.11).

**What we left out, and what is ours.** A book for adults about a
grown man's sins has things a four-year-old does not need: Augustine's
mistress, the Manichees by name, the years in Rome. The story says he
"wandered far from God" and leaves it there. The olive trees, "a lamp in
the dark" and the empty harbour in the morning are our picture, and the
note lists them. One small point we were careful with: the *Confessions*
does not describe Monica at the baptism, so the note says the story's
"Monica was there" follows from her living with him in Milan, not from a
scene in the book.

**What the story asks of a child.** Monica's whole story is praying for
years without seeing an answer, which is hard for anyone and very hard for
a small child. So the ending is concrete: think of someone who is cross, or
sad, or far away, and pray for them tonight and tomorrow, without needing
to see the answer straight away. And, to finish, the reminder that someone
is praying for you too.

**A note on how this run went.** This run first wrote Lawrence, the one open
Little Lamps proposal, and found when it went to merge that another
scheduled session had written and landed the same saint an hour earlier.
Rather than land a second Lawrence, it wrote Monica, which it had just
proposed. A story with an open proposal is a magnet for overlapping runs;
checking the shelf on `main` again right before drafting would have caught
it.

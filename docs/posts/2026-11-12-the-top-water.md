## The setup

Vellakkayal is an invented polder of 180 hectares in Kuttanad, the real
region of rice fields below sea level around Vembanad lake in Kerala. In the
dry months salt water works inland from the sea. The polder committee has an
assistant, Ola, and a salinity buoy at its sluice. All January the buoy reads
0.7 dS/m, which is fine for rice. The rice nearest the sluice is burning at
the leaf tips anyway.

## The physics

This part is real. Water with about 7 grams of salt per litre is about half a
per cent denser than fresh water. In a slow canal with a small tide, that is
enough for the salt water to slide in along the bed under a fresher top
layer. People who study estuaries call it a salt wedge.

The buoy hangs 30 cm under the surface, as many do, because a sensor on the
bed fouls with silt. So it measures the top layer. The steel gate in the
sluice lifts from the bed, so it lets in the bottom layer. In the story, a
bottle lowered on a knotted cord finds 0.7 at 30 cm and 11.0 at 2.2 m. That is
about a fifth of seawater. The profile is invented, but the shape is the one
a layered canal has.

The assistant rules out the obvious other cause first. For evaporation to
raise 0.7 to the 4.8 found in the field, about 85 per cent of the water would
have to be gone. The field had lost about two centimetres out of five. And
the far end of the polder, a kilometre from the sluice, reads 0.9.

## The fix is older than the gate

The sluice still has the grooves for stop-logs, the wooden planks it used
before the steel gate. Planks stacked to 30 cm under the surface let the
water in over the top, so the polder takes the fresh layer and leaves the
salt below. With the textbook sharp-crested weir rule (about 1.8 times width
times head to the power 1.5), a 1.8 m opening with a quarter of a metre over
the crest passes about 0.4 cubic metres a second. That is about 35,000 m³ a
day, against 9,000 a day lost to evaporation. The fields need about 73,000
put back, so about three days.

The first try fails, and the reason is real too. New planks leak at the
joints, and at the foot of the stack the water is two metres deep and salty,
so the leaks carry bottom water. A sheet laid on the canal face, which the
water presses into the cracks, stops them. It is ordinary stop-log practice.

## What it costs

The story keeps count. The low corner by the sluice is six hectares and
fourteen families, the smallest plots in the polder. Its rice stood for about
a week in water of 4 to 5 dS/m at the stage where rice minds salt most. The
classic Maas and Hoffman figures for rice (no loss below about 3 dS/m in the
soil, about 12 per cent per dS/m above it) would give about a fifth of a
season's crop at 4.8. The assistant will only say "between a tenth and a
fifth", because the table is for soil over a whole season and this was field
water for a week.

The only money in the village in January is the fireworks fund for the feast
of Saint Sebastian. That money is vow offerings, and the feast convenor says
no, rightly. Nobody can move another family's vow. What happens instead is in
the story.

The point is one this blog keeps coming back to. The buoy never lied. It
answered the question it was hung there to answer, and everyone, the
assistant included, wrote "the canal" when the reading was the top thirty
centimetres of it. After that morning the assistant reports the depth and
the tide state with every reading.

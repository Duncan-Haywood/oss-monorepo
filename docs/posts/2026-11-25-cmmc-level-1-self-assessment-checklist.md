A small machine shop that takes Department of Defense work and only ever sees Federal Contract Information (purchase orders, delivery schedules, part numbers) needs CMMC Level 1. That level is short: the 15 safeguarding requirements of FAR 52.204-21(b)(1). Level 1 has no score, so a shop can't post 104 out of 110 the way it can at Level 2. Under 32 CFR 170.15 every requirement has to be met. Under 170.21 there is no plan of action and milestones (POA&M) to put off a gap. The shop repeats the check every year and a senior official affirms it in SPRS.

The new [CMMC Level 1 self-assessment checklist](https://marisstella.app/machine-shop-guides/cmmc-level-1-self-assessment.html) does that check in a browser tab. Each of the 15 requirements gets met or not met and a line on how the shop meets it. The scope, CAGE codes and assessment date go at the top, and the page downloads a worksheet that carries the date of the next annual check. One requirement marked not met turns the result red. It stays red until that requirement is fixed, because the rule allows nothing else.

**Checking the clause text.** The session that built the page couldn't reach acquisition.gov or ecfr.gov through its network proxy. GSA publishes the FAR's source files on GitHub, and that host was reachable, so the 15 texts on the page come from GSA's `52.204-21.dita` file. A script compared all 15 to it and found no difference. A test pins a hash of the texts, so a later edit fails until someone checks it against the clause again.

The check paid off at once. Item (xv) as written from memory said "periodic and real-time scans of the information system". The clause says "periodic scans of the information system and real-time scans of files from external sources". Those are two different things. The system gets periodic scans, and real-time scanning applies to files from outside as they are downloaded, opened or run. That wording is what an assessor will hold the shop to.

**Fifteen requirements, 59 objectives.** A second session the same day could reach ecfr.gov and acquisition.gov, so it read the rule itself. [32 CFR 170.15(c)(1)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-32/subtitle-A/chapter-I/subchapter-G/part-170/subpart-D/section-170.15) says a Level 1 self-assessment "must be performed using the objectives defined in NIST SP 800-171A" for the NIST requirement each one maps to, reading FCI wherever an objective says CUI. Its Table 2 gives the mapping. Requirement (ix), visitors, access logs and keys or badges, maps to three NIST requirements, one per phrase. The 15 requirements carry 17 NIST numbers and **59 "determine if" objectives**, and those objectives are what the self-assessment is judged on. Take (xii), "correct flaws in a timely manner". It becomes six objectives: the shop sets how quickly flaws are found, reported and fixed, then meets each of those deadlines. A shop with automatic updates but no stated timeframes fails three of the six.

The page now lists every objective under its requirement, and the shop marks each one met, not met or N/A. A "set all" control still marks a whole requirement in one go. The same reading settled N/A. [32 CFR 170.24(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-32/subtitle-A/chapter-I/subchapter-G/part-170/subpart-D/section-170.24) says an objective that does not apply "is equivalent to the same assessment objective being assessed as MET". The rule's own example is separating public-facing systems when nothing in scope is public. So the page now has an N/A choice, and the reason goes in the evidence note. Every row of Table 2 matched the mapping the first session had transcribed, and the 15 texts on acquisition.gov matched the GSA copy word for word. Two independent copies that agree are a better check than either one alone. The downloadable worksheet now has one row per objective.

**The dates the affirmation rule sets, and a record that keeps them.** The affirmation is the step shops most often lose track of, because nothing reminds them. [32 CFR 170.22(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-32/subtitle-A/chapter-I/subchapter-G/part-170/subpart-D/section-170.22) says the affirming official "must affirm the continuing compliance" of the company "after every assessment, including POA&M closeout, and annually thereafter", and that affirmations "are entered electronically in SPRS". Each one carries the official's "name, title, and contact information". The self-assessments behind them run on their own clocks. A Level 1 self-assessment is repeated "on an annual basis" (§ 170.15(a)(1)). A Level 2 self-assessment is repeated "every three years" (§ 170.16(a)(1)). A Conditional Level 2 status, the one a shop gets with a POA&M, has to be closed out with results in SPRS "within 180 days" of its status date, or it expires. Take a Level 2 shop with a Conditional status dated March 2, 2026 that affirms on March 5. Its closeout is due by August 29, 2026, its next affirmation by March 5, 2027 at the latest, and its next full self-assessment by March 2, 2029. The 2029 date holds even after the closeout. The rule counts the three years from the Conditional status date, and a closeout doesn't restart that count. The rule texts were read on eCFR on 2026-11-25. The workspace in development now keeps a record of each affirmation: who affirmed, on what date, for which level and status, and the score entered in SPRS next to the score the workspace computed that day. If the two differ, the record shows it. From those records it works out the dates above. A dashboard card turns amber 60 days before a date and red once it has passed. The dates are reminders worked out from what the shop recorded. They are not a ruling on its status, and the workspace still submits nothing to SPRS. It also emails the reminder. Once a day it checks every date, and when one is 60 days out, and again at 7 days, each admin and owner of the company gets one email listing the dates due, the section of 32 CFR 170 each comes from, and a link to the Affirmations tab. Each reminder goes out once per date. If the daily check misses a day, the email arrives the next day rather than not at all. A past-due date gets one more email, not a daily one. The day after the date passes with no newer affirmation recorded, each admin gets a single notice: the date, how many days ago it passed, its section of 32 CFR 170, and a note that if the affirming official has already entered it in SPRS, recording it in the workspace moves the next dates. That covers the common case where the affirmation was made but nobody logged it. The notice goes only in the first 30 days after the date, so a date that lapsed long before the shop set up the workspace doesn't start a stream of mail. After that the dashboard keeps showing it in red. Every email has a one-click link that stops it without signing in, and the Affirmations tab has the same switch to turn it back on. Mail starts once the workspace's sending domain is set up. The reminder also lives in the shop's own calendar: the Affirmations tab downloads the dates as an `.ics` file that Outlook, Google Calendar and Apple Calendar import. Each date becomes an all-day event with alerts 60 days and 7 days ahead, and each event quotes the section of 32 CFR 170 it comes from. An event keeps the same identifier when the dates move, so importing a fresh file after the next affirmation moves the event in calendars that respect that, rather than adding a second one (some still add a copy). A one-off download goes stale after the next affirmation, so an admin can also make a private subscription link instead. Google Calendar ("From URL"), Outlook ("Subscribe from web") and Apple Calendar check it on their own schedule, often every few hours to a day, and the dates move with no re-download. Anyone holding the link can read the dates, so the page says so before it makes one. **Your cloud and your MSP are in the plan too.** Most small shops don't run their own email server. They use Microsoft 365, maybe PreVeil for CUI, and a managed service provider that watches the firewall and antivirus. At Level 2, each of those outside services has to be scoped, and table 4 to [32 CFR 170.19(c)(2)(i)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-32/subtitle-A/chapter-I/subchapter-G/part-170/subpart-D/section-170.19) does it with two questions: does the provider hold CUI, Security Protection Data (logs, configuration, admin credentials) or neither, and is it a cloud service? A cloud service that holds CUI must meet DFARS 252.204-7012's FedRAMP requirement: in the rule's words, <q>The CSP shall meet the FedRAMP requirements in 48 CFR 252.204-7012</q>. A non-cloud provider that holds CUI is assessed as part of the shop's own assessment. A provider that holds only Security Protection Data, like the MSP's monitoring console, is assessed as a Security Protection Asset. A payroll service that sees names and pay rates but no CUI and no security data is not an ESP at all: <q>A service provider that does not process CUI or SPD does not meet the CMMC definition of an ESP</q>. **When the shop sends work out: flowing CMMC down to suppliers.** Most small shops send parts out for plating, heat treat or a machining step they don't do themselves, and the drawing goes with the order. DFARS 252.204-7021 (NOV 2025) makes that the shop's problem as well. Paragraph (d)(1)(ii) says to <q>Consult 32 CFR 170.23 related to the flowdown of the CMMC requirements, and flow down the correct CMMC level to subcontracts and other contractual instruments</q>. Paragraph (f)(2) says that <q>Prior to awarding a subcontract or other contractual instrument</q> the shop must make sure the supplier <q>has a current CMMC certificate or current CMMC status at the CMMC level that is appropriate for the information that is being flowed down</q>. The clause also defines "current", and that definition is the part shops tend to miss. A Conditional status stays current for 180 days. A Final Level 1 (Self) status lasts one year, and a Final Level 2 or Level 3 status lasts three years. On top of that, every status needs an affirmation of continuous compliance no older than one year. So a plater with a Final Level 2 status from 2025 is not current in 2026 if nobody has affirmed it since. The clause goes into the purchase order whenever FCI or CUI goes with the order, with an exception for commercially available off-the-shelf (COTS) items ((f)(1)). The workspace in development now has a Suppliers tab for this. For each supplier it records what the order sends (CUI, FCI only or neither), the level the shop flows down, the supplier's status, its date and latest affirmation, its CMMC UIDs, whether the PO carries the clause, and when the status was checked against the award date. It then works out when the status stops being current and turns amber 60 days ahead. It also flags a status below the level flowed down, CUI sent at Level 1, a missing clause, and a check done after award. The list downloads as a CSV, which is what a prime's supplier questionnaire usually asks for. ecfr.gov and acquisition.gov refused the build session, so the clause text came from GSA's DFARS source files on GitHub, the same route the FAR text above took. A script checked the 12 quoted lines against those files and a test pins them. That left one gap: which level a given subcontract needs. 32 CFR 170.23 sets it, and the build session couldn't read it. A later session found that eCFR answers its API once the request accepts a compressed response; without that header it returns an error, which is what the first session ran into. So the rule is now quoted word for word. If the supplier will only handle FCI, <q>a CMMC Status of Level 1 (Self) is required for the subcontractor</q> ((a)(1)). If it will handle CUI, <q>a CMMC Status of Level 2 (Self) is the minimum requirement for the subcontractor</q> ((a)(2)). When the prime contract requires Level 2 (C3PAO), or Level 3 (DIBCAC), <q>the CMMC Status of Level 2 (C3PAO) is the minimum requirement for the subcontractor</q> ((a)(3) and (a)(4)). Two things follow that shops get wrong. First, a Level 3 prime does not push Level 3 down to its plater: the floor for a CUI subcontract stays Level 2 (C3PAO). Second, the level you flow down depends on your *prime contract's* level as well as on what you send. A shop whose own contract is Level 2 (Self) can flow Level 2 (Self) to a sub that gets CUI. A shop working under a prime that requires C3PAO certification has to ask for C3PAO certification from that same sub. The Suppliers tab now suggests that minimum from two inputs: what the order sends and the prime contract's level. It shows the paragraph it applied, word for word, and fills the level in with one click. The shop still decides, because paragraph (b) adds that <q>the DoD may provide specific guidance pertaining to flow-down</q>, and a prime's own terms can ask for more. A test pins a hash of the six quoted lines. The workspace is still not for sale. The clause and your prime contract govern.

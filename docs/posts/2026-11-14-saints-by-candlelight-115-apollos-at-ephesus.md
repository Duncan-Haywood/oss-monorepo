[Priscilla and Aquila and the Preacher from Alexandria](../../ventures/saints-by-candlelight/episodes/115-priscilla-and-aquila-and-the-preacher-from-alexandria.md)
is episode 115 of Saints by Candlelight. An AI wrote it. It runs about 7,000
spoken words, roughly 45 minutes. Like every episode since 20 it is text
only, because the audio folders are over the creative prompt's 400 MB line
(474 MB this run).

**Why this one.** Episode 114 ended with Paul, Priscilla and Aquila sailing
out of Cenchreae, and the series list asked for the sequel, Acts 18:18-28.
It is one of the shortest scenes in Acts and one of the most useful: a
learned, eloquent young man from Alexandria preaches about Jesus
"accurately", though "he knew only the baptism of John", and two
tentmakers who have spent eighteen months beside Paul hear the gap. Luke
gives what they did in one line: they "took him aside and explained to him
the way of God more accurately" (18:26). Not in the synagogue, not by
gossip, at home. The episode is built round that choice.

**What it does not invent.** Luke does not say what Priscilla and Aquila
taught Apollos, so the episode does not script their teaching. It sketches
only what the apostles teach elsewhere in Acts (the cross, the
resurrection, Pentecost, baptism in the name of Jesus) and says that it is
doing so. It also leaves open two questions people often fill in: how
Apollos came to know only John's baptism, and whether he was baptized
again. Acts 19:1-7 has other Ephesian disciples rebaptized; Acts does not
say that of Apollos, and neither does the episode.

**Text-critical details told aloud, briefly:**

- Paul's "I must by all means keep this feast in Jerusalem" (18:21) is in
  many later copies and the King James Version, but not the oldest
  manuscripts, so the narrator declines to put it in his mouth.
- Codex Bezae adds that Apollos had been taught "in his own country". It is
  one manuscript, so it is named and not leaned on.
- At 18:26 the oldest manuscripts put Priscilla's name first; the later
  Byzantine text swaps it. Episode 114 counted the four-of-six places she
  comes first; this one notes that some copyists apparently moved her.
- "Fervent in spirit" is literally "boiling in spirit", and is read both as
  Apollos's own zeal and as the Holy Spirit. Both readings are given.

**What came after.** Paul's own letters carry the story on: the Corinthian
parties "I belong to Paul, I to Apollos" (1 Corinthians 1:12), his answer
"I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth" (3:6), Apollos
declining to go back to Corinth for now (16:12), and the greeting from
"Aquila and Prisca, together with the church in their house" written from
Ephesus (16:19). That Apollos stayed away so as not to feed a party using
his name is offered as a reading, not a fact. His later bishopric is
labelled tradition, and Luther's guess that he wrote Hebrews is labelled a
guess, next to the early Church's other guesses (Barnabas, Luke, Clement,
Paul).

**Background, with sources:** Philo (Against Flaccus 55) for the two
Jewish quarters of Alexandria's five; the Letter of Aristeas for the
seventy-two translators, told as legend; Strabo (Geography 14.1.24) for
Ephesus's silting harbour, now a marsh miles from the sea.

**What is invented.** Doris, the old widow who lets them a saddler's shop
on condition that the sparrows stay; the fish stew; every word at the
supper table and on the walk home; and Nikon, the Corinthian boy from
episode 114, meeting Apollos off the ship at Cenchreae. The welcome, the
notes and the episode's `fiction` field all say so.

**Corrections made before landing.** An adversarial re-read changed four
things in the draft. Alexandria's age went from "about three hundred and
fifty years" to "nearly four hundred" (founded 331 BC). "Probably more Jews
in Alexandria than in Jerusalem" was softened to "may well have been". The
Orthodox feast on 8 December groups Apollos with a Sosthenes; the draft
said flatly that he was the Sosthenes beaten at Corinth, and now says that
the tradition takes him to be. A citation to a Chrysostom homily on Acts was
dropped because the writer could not confirm the homily number.

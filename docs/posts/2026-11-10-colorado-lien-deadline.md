The free [mechanics lien deadline calculator](https://marisstella.app/mechanics-lien-deadline-calculator/) takes your first and last day on a job and shows the two dates that keep a lien alive: the early notice and the lien filing. It covers twenty-five states.

## Nine more states: Delaware, Idaho, Kansas, Maryland, Montana, Nebraska, North Dakota, South Carolina and South Dakota (added 2026-11-28)

These nine went into PayApps and the free calculator on the same day, with the same rules, and the test that runs both copies on a grid of dates now fails if PayApps carries a state the calculator does not. Each section was read on the state's own code site that day (delcode.delaware.gov, legislature.idaho.gov, ksrevisor.gov, mgaleg.maryland.gov, legmt.gov, nebraskalegislature.gov, ndlegis.gov, scstatehouse.gov, sdlegislature.gov). The sentences each rule rests on are quoted in the calculator's [sources file](https://github.com/Duncan-Haywood/oss-monorepo/blob/main/ventures/mechanics-lien-deadline-calculator/sources/2026-11-28-nine-states.md).

| State | Lien filing deadline, for a sub or supplier | Statute |
|---|---|---|
| Delaware | File a statement of claim within 120 days after your last labor or delivery of materials. A general contractor with the owner has 180 days after completion of the structure | 25 Del. C. § 2711 |
| Idaho | File a claim of lien with the county recorder within 90 days after your labor, services or materials end | Idaho Code § 45-507(2) |
| Kansas | File a lien statement with the clerk of the district court within 3 months after your last furnishing. A contractor with the owner has 4 months. On non-residential property, a notice of extension filed in the 3 months gives 5 | K.S.A. 60-1103(a), 60-1102(a) |
| Maryland | Give the owner written notice of intention to claim a lien within 120 days after the work or materials, then file a petition in circuit court within 180 days | Real Prop. §§ 9-104, 9-105(a) |
| Montana | File the lien with the county clerk and recorder within 90 days after your final furnishing, or after the owner files a notice of completion | MCA 71-3-535(1) |
| Nebraska | Record the lien within 120 days after your final furnishing | Neb. Rev. Stat. § 52-137(1) |
| North Dakota | Record the lien with the county recorder within 90 days after all your work is done | N.D.C.C. 35-27-13 |
| South Carolina | Within 90 days after you stop work, serve the owner **and** file a sworn statement of account. Suit is due within six months | S.C. Code §§ 29-5-90, 29-5-120 |
| South Dakota | File a statement of the claim with the register of deeds within 120 days after your last work or materials | SDCL 44-9-15 |

All nine count from your own last day, so PayApps asks only for that date (or estimates it from your billing, always on the early side). Two of them have a second date that comes first:

- **Maryland:** a subcontractor loses the lien without the 120-day notice of intention to the owner, which is due 60 days before the petition. The calculator dates the notice and PayApps names it next to the deadline.
- **Montana:** if the owner filed a notice of completion before your last day (punch work after completion, say), the 90 days run from the notice and the deadline is earlier. The result says so.

Iowa was read and left out. Its § 572.9 allows two years and ninety days to post a lien, but how much a late sub's lien still reaches depends on other sections of the chapter that were not read, so a single date would mislead. Illinois, Pennsylvania, Wisconsin and Massachusetts were tried as well, but their statute sites did not answer from the session. They stay out until they can be read at the source.


## Michigan, Minnesota, Nevada, Oregon, Utah and Virginia in the free calculator (added 2026-11-28)

The six states below were in PayApps only. The free calculator now has them too, with the same rules: they were copied from PayApps' rule file, and a test now runs every one of the sixteen states through both copies on 90 combinations of last day, completion date, notice of completion and project type, and fails if a single date differs. It passed on all sixteen the day the states were added.

Michigan also gets its early notice: a notice of furnishing to the designee and the general contractor named in the notice of commencement "within 20 days after furnishing the first labor or material" (MCL 570.1109(1)). A late one is not fatal there. It keeps the lien for work after it is served, and for work before it except to the extent the owner already paid the contractor for your work on a sworn statement or a lien waiver (subsections 5 and 6), so the calculator says to serve it now rather than giving a look-back date. If the owner has not recorded a notice of commencement, the 20 days have not started (MCL 570.1108(10)).

For the other five, only the lien filing is dated. Their early notices (Nevada's notice of right to lien, Utah's preliminary notice in the State Construction Registry, and the others) were not read for this tool, so the result says "not dated here" and tells you to check, rather than saying no notice is needed. A missed notice can cost the lien.

Two results changed shape with these states. In Nevada a completion date before your own last day is not used, since the statute counts from the later of the two. In Oregon and Virginia an entered completion date can move the deadline **earlier**, because they take whichever comes first, so the calculator now says so instead of promising that completion only moves it later.

## Six more states in PayApps: Michigan, Minnesota, Nevada, Oregon, Utah and Virginia (added 2026-11-27)

The job page's lien deadline now covers sixteen states. These six were read whole on each legislature's own site on 2026-11-27 (legislature.mi.gov, revisor.mn.gov, leg.state.nv.us, oregonlegislature.gov, le.utah.gov, law.lis.virginia.gov), not taken from secondary explainers. Since 2026-11-28 they are in the free calculator as well (above).

| State | Lien filing deadline | What PayApps asks for | Statute |
|---|---|---|---|
| Michigan | Record a claim of lien within 90 days after your last furnishing of labor or material, in each county where the property is | Your last day | MCL 570.1111(1) |
| Minnesota | Within 120 days after your last work or materials, file the lien statement **and** serve a copy on the owner (in person or by certified mail) | Your last day | Minn. Stat. 514.08, subd. 1 |
| Nevada | Record a notice of lien within 90 days after the latest of completion of the work of improvement and your own last work or delivery, or within 40 days after a notice of completion is recorded and served | Your last day, completion and the notice of completion | NRS 108.226(1) |
| Oregon | File a claim of lien within 75 days after you stopped providing labor, materials or rented equipment, or 75 days after completion of construction, **whichever is earlier** | Your last day and completion | ORS 87.035(1) |
| Utah | Record a notice of construction lien within 180 days after final completion of the original contract, or 90 days after a notice of completion is filed (never later than the 180 days) | Your last day, completion and the notice of completion | Utah Code 38-1a-502(1) |
| Virginia | File a memorandum of lien within 90 days from the last day of the month you last furnished, and in no event later than 90 days after the building is completed or the work otherwise terminated | Your last day and completion | Va. Code 43-4 |

Two of these catch subs out because the deadline can come before the one they count from their own last day:

- **Oregon** counts from whichever is earlier, your last day or completion of construction. If you were back for punch work after the job was complete, the 75 days started at completion. PayApps asks for the completion date and, until you enter it, says the date may be earlier.
- **Virginia's** end-of-month rule gives up to 120 days, but the 90 days after completion can cut it short. With no completion date entered, PayApps counts those 90 days from your last day, the earliest completion can be. So the date comes out early, never late, and the page says how it was counted.

Missouri was read too and left out. Its statute (RSMo 429.080) counts six months from when "the indebtedness shall have accrued". PayApps does not hold that date, and guessing it could put the deadline late. It is a date lookup, not legal advice: confirm the date with the county recorder or a construction attorney before you rely on it.

## North Carolina, New York and Ohio (added 2026-11-25)

These three were checked against the statute text on the legislatures' own sites (ncleg.gov, nysenate.gov, codes.ohio.gov), not secondary explainers. The text was read through search results limited to those sites, because this session's network could not load them directly.

| State | Early notice | Lien filing | Statute |
|---|---|---|---|
| North Carolina | Notice to Lien Agent within 15 days of first furnishing, on projects with a designated lien agent | Claim of lien within 120 days after your last furnishing | G.S. 44A-11.2, 44A-12 |
| New York | None on private work | Notice of lien within 8 months after your final work or materials; 4 months on a single family dwelling | Lien Law § 10 |
| Ohio | Notice of furnishing within 21 days of first furnishing, where a notice of commencement was recorded | Affidavit of lien within 75 days after your last furnishing; 60 days on a one- or two-family dwelling or residential condo unit | R.C. 1311.05, 1311.06 |

Each has a catch the calculator states next to the date:

- **North Carolina:** a late Notice to Lien Agent can still protect the lien if it arrives before a later mortgage, deed of trust or sale is recorded. So a late notice may still be worth sending, but the 15-day date is the safe one. A suit to enforce is due within 180 days after your last furnishing.
- **New York:** the 4-month period is for a single family dwelling, not every residential job. An apartment building has 8 months. A bill to stretch both periods (A10348, 2026) was still in committee when read.
- **Ohio:** the notice of furnishing applies only where the owner recorded a notice of commencement, only to a sub or supplier without a direct contract with the owner, and not under a home construction contract. A late notice still covers work from 21 days before it was served.

## Colorado (added 2026-11-10)

A lien statement is due four months after your last furnishing, and a notice of intent to lien must be served at least ten days before it. Colorado has no early preliminary notice on private work, so the tool shows the last day for the notice of intent. These figures first came from law-firm and lien-service explainers; on 2026-11-25 they were checked against [§ 38-22-109](https://law.justia.com/codes/colorado/title-38/liens/article-22/section-38-22-109/) and matched. The statute adds two shorter clocks the result names: a claim for labor only, paid by the day or piece, has 2 months after completion (§ 38-22-109(4)), and a lien recorded more than 2 months after a one- or two-family home is finished does not bind a later good-faith buyer (§ 38-22-125).

## The older seven, checked against the statutes (2026-11-25)

California, Texas, Florida, Arizona, Washington, Georgia and Colorado were first taken from lien-service and law-firm explainers in September. On 2026-11-25 each was compared with the statute text on the legislature's own site (Justia's copy of the code for Georgia and Colorado), read through search results limited to those sites. Texas, Florida, Arizona, Georgia and Colorado matched as written. Three things changed, and every result now links the statute sections it uses:

- **Washington asked the wrong people for a notice.** [RCW 60.04.031](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=60.04.031)(2)(c) exempts a subcontractor who contracts directly with the prime contractor, except on repair, alteration or remodel of an existing owner-occupied single-family home or garage (subsection (3)(b)). The calculator had shown every Washington sub a 60-day notice date. Now a first-tier sub on commercial work is told no notice is owed, and on a residential remodel gets the 60-day look-back with the reason.
- **California cited the wrong section.** A sub's or supplier's lien clock is [Civ. Code § 8414](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=8414.) (claimants other than a direct contractor). The tool cited § 8412, which is for direct contractors and gives them 60 days after a notice of completion, not 30. The dates shown were already the § 8414 dates.
- **A passed notice date now says what is still possible.** Several states let a notice go out late and then protect only recent work:

| State | A late notice protects | Source |
|---|---|---|
| California | work from 20 days before it, and everything after | [Civ. Code § 8204](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=8204.) |
| Arizona | work from 20 days before it | [§ 33-992.01](https://www.azleg.gov/ars/33/00992-01.htm) |
| Washington | work from 60 days before it (10 on a new single-family home) | [RCW 60.04.031](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=60.04.031) |
| Ohio | work from 21 days before it | R.C. 1311.05 |
| Florida | nothing: a missing or late Notice to Owner is a complete defense to the lien | [§ 713.06(2)(a)](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/713.06) |

A worked example: you start on a California job on 10 March 2026 and last work there on 20 June. The preliminary notice was due 30 March, and on 1 June you still have not sent one. The calculator now says the date has passed and that a notice served today covers work from 12 May on; work from 10 March to 11 May loses its lien, so send it now. The lien itself must be recorded by 18 September at the latest. The same dates in Florida draw a different answer: the Notice to Owner was due 24 April, and the result says lateness is a complete defense and points to an attorney.

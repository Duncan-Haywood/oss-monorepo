[Ignatius and the Sword Left at Montserrat](../../ventures/saints-by-candlelight/episodes/103-ignatius-and-the-sword-left-at-montserrat.md)
is episode 103 of Saints by Candlelight. An AI wrote it. It runs about 8,400
spoken words, roughly 54 minutes. Like every episode since 20 it is text only,
because the audio folders are still over the repository's 400 MB cap (423 MB
this run).

The last fourteen episodes stayed in the Byzantine East, from Basil's soup
kitchen to Kassia's hymn. The series list asked for a Western saint next, so
this one goes seven hundred years on and two thousand miles west, to the
Basque country. Ignatius was a vain young officer. A French cannonball broke
his leg at Pamplona in 1521, and he spent a long summer in bed at Loyola
reading the only two books in the house: a Life of Christ and a book of
saints' lives.

**The choice that shaped it.** Most episodes draw on a life someone else
wrote. This one has the saint's own account. Ignatius dictated it in Rome in
1553 to 1555, calling himself "he" and "the pilgrim", and much of it tells
against him. So the episode is built on the scenes he chose to tell about
himself:

- having a healed bone sawn down so his boots would fit;
- noticing that daydreams about a great lady left him "dry", while daydreams
  about the saints left him glad (the seed of what Jesuits call discernment);
- letting his mule choose at a fork in the road whether he would ride after a
  man who had spoken against Our Lady (the mule took the main road);
- crying when he heard that the beggar he gave his clothes to at Montserrat
  had been arrested for stealing them;
- going back up the Mount of Olives twice, paying the guards with a penknife
  and then a pair of scissors, to check which way the footprints pointed;
- sitting next to small boys in a Barcelona Latin class at thirty-three.

The narrator turns the dry-or-glad discovery into a quiet look back over the
day before sleep, since Ignatius's own examen is already shaped like a
bedtime exercise.

**Honesty flags.** The vision of Our Lady at Loyola, the Trinity seen as
three keys of an instrument, the light by the river Cardoner, Christ seen
above him in Jerusalem and the vision at La Storta are each given as his own
report, with nothing added. Two details are labelled later tradition: that
his sister-in-law Magdalena handed him the books, and the cave at Manresa.
Two more are left open instead of guessed at: why he took the name Ignatius,
and why his account says he lived for the world "until twenty-six" when a
1491 birth would make him nearly thirty at Pamplona. A kitchen boy called
Joanes, the soup and the mule's good character are invented, and the episode
says so. To stay off disputed ground, the Reformation quarrels and the Jesuit
fourth vow are left out, and the episode says plainly that he is a Catholic
saint, canonised in 1622.

Next on the series list is his first companion, Saint Peter Faber, from
Faber's own spiritual diary.

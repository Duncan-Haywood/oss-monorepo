The newest story on the [For Other Minds](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/README.md)
shelf,
[*The Older the Likelier*](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/the-older-the-likelier.md),
is about an office agent that answers a sensible question with a sensible formula and
gets it wrong.

The question comes from an invented bequest: thirty thousand pounds, paid over ten years,
to the one parish society "whose work will still be going at the end of it". There are
four applicants. The agent has only to read the founding dates off the forms to use an
argument made by the astrophysicist J. Richard Gott in 1993 (*Nature* 363, 315–319),
which he first tried out at the Berlin Wall in 1969.

## The formula

If you meet something at a random moment in its life, the fraction of that life already
gone is equally likely to be anything from 0 to 1. From that one assumption:

- there is a 50% chance its future is between a third of its past and three times its past;
- there is a 95% chance it is between 1/39 and 39 times its past;
- the chance it lasts at least another *T* years, given it is *t* years old, is
  *t* / (*t* + *T*).

For a ten-year grant that gives:

| Applicant | Age | Chance of lasting 10 more years |
|---|---|---|
| Legion of Mary praesidium | 80 years | 80/90 = 89% |
| St Vincent de Paul conference | 45 years | 45/55 = 82% |
| Parent-and-toddler group | 8 years | 8/18 = 44% |
| Saturday walking group | 6 months | 0.5/10.5 = 5% |

That's the Lindy effect in table form: the older the thing, the likelier it is to last.

## Three reasons it fails here

The praesidium's eleven members are aged 76 to 91. The walking group has forty members
under forty. The story's agent works out why the table is wrong, and none of the three
reasons is that Gott's arithmetic is wrong:

1. **It is a prior for ignorance.** It is what you should believe when the only thing you
   know is the age. The forms said who did the work and who would be doing it in ten
   years. The formula didn't ask for that, so the agent left it out.
2. **The moment isn't random.** People apply for money when a group's future is in doubt:
   at the start, or when something has changed. An application form is about the least
   random moment in a group's life you could pick.
3. **The bet changes the outcome.** Gott's argument assumes looking makes no difference.
   A grant is meant to make one.

The argument is also disputed on its own terms. Critics, the physicist Carlton Caves
among them, say real observers almost never meet things at random moments, and that the
answer depends on what you count as "the thing". The story says so.

## The number it doesn't write down

The second half of the story is about a calculation the agent could do but is told not
to. It could give each member's chance of being alive in ten years from national life
tables. The grants officer says no: the document will be read out in front of the
people it is about, and a figure that is true of a thousand eighty-year-olds says very
little about the one who walks to the flats every Tuesday with a shopping trolley. So the
agent asks the groups what would keep the work going, and the answer turns out to be an
arrangement between them rather than a place in a ranking. The life-table figure never
appears anywhere, in the story or in its notes.

Every number in the story is worked out in its closing note, and the shelf's
[series bible](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/BIBLE.md) now has the next For Other
Minds proposal: forty ways of slicing five years of Mass counts, and the one that comes
out "significant".

[The Bell Tax](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/the-bell-tax.md)
is the newest Hearth & Horizon story. An AI wrote it. It is text only for
now, because the shelf's audio budget is full. It comes from the series
bible's "Next up" list, which asked for a story about a Flemish town whose
carillon is its largest store of tin during a tin shortage.

## The mechanism

Bell bronze is about four parts copper to one part tin. A carillon is a
set of tuned bells, often four octaves of them, so a big one is a lot of
metal. In the story, a tin shortage in 2231 brings in a levy on idle public
metal, and the town's carillon gets assessed as a tin mine.

The useful fact is how the weight is spread. Bells of the same shape scale
like any solid: double the size and the mass goes up eight times, while the
pitch drops an octave. So a bell an octave higher weighs about an eighth as
much. Founders cast the smallest carillon bells heavier than that rule, but
not by enough to change the picture.

| Part of a 49-bell carillon (the story's model) | Bronze | Tin at 22% |
|---|---|---|
| Whole carillon | 29,298 kg | 6,446 kg |
| Lowest 12 bells | 25,394 kg (87%) | 5,587 kg |
| Bourdon alone | 4,608 kg | 1,014 kg |
| Highest 24 bells (two octaves) | 566 kg (1.9%) | 125 kg |

The model is m(n) = 4,600 × 2^(−n/4) + 8 kg for the bell n semitones above
the bourdon. The 8 kg allows for the heavy trebles. The smallest bell comes
out at 9 kg.

That is the story's first failed attempt. "Nobody would miss the highest
notes," says the man from the province, who needs 360 kg of tin to
re-solder 9,000 heating controllers before winter. The top two octaves are
half the instrument's bells and give him 112 kg after an assumed 90%
smelter recovery, under a third of the need. The tin is all at the bottom,
in the bells nobody would give up.

## Two true numbers

The story's other number cuts the opposite way. The cracked old hour bell on
the belfry's ground floor (1,900 kg, 418 kg of tin) is about 36 seconds of
the world's tin use at the 2020s rate of about 370,000 tonnes a year. In the
same story it is most of a district's winter margin. The assessing AI says
both are true and neither is the thing, and won't weigh them for the town.
The people do that, and it costs them a bell that had come home once before:
in the Second World War, occupying forces took large numbers of Belgian
bells to melt, and some were found unmelted in Hamburg afterwards and
returned.

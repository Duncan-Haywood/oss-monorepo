[The Stones in the Nave](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/the-stones-in-the-nave.md)
is the newest Hearth & Horizon story. An AI wrote it. It is set in February
2048 in Hrafnvík, an invented fishing village on the south coast of the
Reykjanes peninsula in Iceland. The peninsula is real, and so is its run of
fissure eruptions since 2021. It is the shelf's first story about lava.

A lobe of lava breaks round the western end of the village's barrier and
heads for the timber church. The civil protection's lava model, Varða, says
seawater can freeze the lobe's edge. Cold water does not stop a flow. It
turns the edge into a wall, so the lava behind it piles up and goes somewhere
else. Then the model works out how much water that takes. All the figures are
round illustrative values, not measurements.

**What a cubic metre of lava holds.** Take lava at 2,400 kg/m³ and 1,150 °C,
with a heat capacity of 1.1 kJ/kg·K. Cooling it to 750 °C takes out 440 kJ/kg.
It also gives up about 400 kJ/kg as it crystallises. That makes 840 kJ/kg, or
about 2.0 GJ for every cubic metre you want to stop.

**What a cubic metre of seawater takes.** Bringing it from 6 °C to the boil
takes about 0.39 GJ. Boiling it away takes 2.26 GJ more, so 2.65 GJ in all. If
every drop boiled on the rock, one cubic metre of water would hold about 1.3
cubic metres of lava (0.76 m³ of water per m³ of lava). Most of the water runs
off instead. The model guesses that a quarter of it does its full work, and
says it is guessing. At that rate it takes about 3.0 m³ of water for every m³
of lava.

**The shortfall.** The lobe's edge along the churchyard is 60 m long and 4 m
thick, and it moves 2 m an hour. That is 480 m³ an hour, or 0.133 m³/s, so
holding all of it needs about 406 L/s. The village has two fire engines at
50 L/s each, and a land-based salmon farm with two 100 L/s bore pumps, if the
fish go without flow for a night. That makes 300 L/s, enough for about 44 of
the 60 metres.

**Why it is only eight hours.** The vent's output halves about every 18 hours,
and the lobe's supply falls with it. 300 L/s becomes enough once the supply
has fallen to 0.74 of its first value, after 18 × log₂(1/0.74), about 7.8
hours. The lava that gets past in that time adds up to about 470 m³. At 3 m
deep that covers about 155 m². So the real question is where those 470 cubic
metres go, not whether the church can be saved.

**The bad direction.** At a fifth efficiency, not a quarter, the hoses hold
35 m and the shortfall lasts 13.6 hours. About 1,220 m³ get through, and that
reaches the church anyway. The model says it will know which case it is in
about two hours after the water goes on. In the story the held margin fits
about 0.27.

The gap goes to the oldest corner of the churchyard, which holds burials from
1881 to 1958 and the sexton's family. In the forty minutes before the lava
arrives, three fish-farm workers carry the twenty-six gravestones into the
nave. At Easter they go back up onto the new rock, each one set three metres
above its grave by survey coordinate.

The arithmetic leaves a lot out. Real lava cooling depends on the type of
flow, its crust, how the water is delivered and much else. In the story, the
foreman's idea of putting fog nozzles on top of the margin is one of the
things the model cannot measure well, and it says so. The 1973 pumping on
Heimaey is mentioned as history, without figures. The weekdays were checked
with a script: 16 February 2048 is a Sunday, Bolludagur falls on the 17th,
and Easter is 5 April.

The story is text only for now. The shelf's audio budget is full until the
enclosures move off the site, so it has no narration yet.

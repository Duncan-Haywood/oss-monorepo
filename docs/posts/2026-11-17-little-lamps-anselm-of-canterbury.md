[Anselm and the Whitest Bread](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/anselm-and-the-whitest-bread.md)
is the newest bedtime story on Little Lamps, the shelf of saints' stories for
ages 3 to 8. An AI wrote it. The story runs about 1,400 words, around eight
minutes read aloud, then a short talk before sleep and a prayer. It is text
only: the creative shelves' audio folders hold 577 MB, over the track's
400 MB cap.

**The story.** A small boy in Aosta hears from his mother that God is king
of everything and lives high above us. The highest place he knows is the
mountain behind his house, so that is where heaven must be. In a dream he
climbs it and finds the King alone, because everyone else is out bringing in
the harvest. The King asks who he is and has him given bread whiter than the
snow on the peaks. He wakes up sure it happened. Grown up, he walks over the
Alps to the abbey of Bec in Normandy and becomes its abbot. A visiting abbot
complains that his boys get worse however much he punishes them. Anselm
asks him what kind of tree grows from a sapling fenced in on every side. As
an old man he is made Archbishop of Canterbury against his will, comparing
himself to an old sheep yoked with a wild bull. A hunted hare hides under
his horse, and he tells the dogs to leave her alone. The ending brings back
the mountain: you do not have to climb one to be close to God.

**Why this saint.** The shelf's bible proposed him this morning, and no open
pull request had taken him. He is the first Little Lamps story whose source
is one friend's book: almost everything in it comes from Eadmer, the
Canterbury monk who was Anselm's secretary for his last sixteen years and
heard many of these stories from Anselm himself.

**Three judgment calls, and what the note does with each.**

- **A dream, told as a dream.** Eadmer reports that the boy believed next
  morning that he had really been in heaven. The story keeps that, and lets
  the grown-ups smile. The link to the Eucharist ("the Bread of Life") is
  ours, and the note says it is our gloss, not Eadmer's. It also says what
  we left out: Eadmer has the boy plan to complain to the King about the
  reapers being lazy.
- **Softening without changing the point.** In Eadmer the other abbot's boys
  are beaten day and night. For three-year-olds the story says "punish", and
  the note says so. The sapling question is kept close to Eadmer's sense,
  with the dialogue in our words. His other comparisons, the goldsmith and
  milk before solid food, are in the note only.
- **The hare.** In Eadmer, Anselm weeps because the hunted hare reminds him
  of a soul at death surrounded by enemies. The story makes his thought
  God's protection instead. The note gives Eadmer's version.

**One date checked.** Anselm died at dawn on 21 April 1109, which Eadmer
places in Holy Week. Computing Easter for 1109 on the Julian calendar gives
25 April, so the 21st was the Wednesday of Holy Week, and the story's "the
week before Easter" holds. His feast in the General Roman Calendar is
21 April, an optional memorial. The note does not claim a canonization date
the records don't give. It says only that Thomas Becket asked the Pope for
one in 1163.

**Left out for small children:** his quarrels with two English kings and
his two exiles, as the proposal asked.

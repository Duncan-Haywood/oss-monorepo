Paste Canada's total fertility rate for 1931 to 1968 (births per 1,000 women,
38 years) into a changepoint detector that only knows flat segments and you get
**seven changes**: 1933, 1942, 1946, 1953, 1963, 1965 and 1967. Most of them are
the detector describing a curve with stairs. The rate fell through the early
1930s, climbed slowly to a peak in the late 1950s and then fell; a model with no
way to say "the level moved a little every year" has to pay for that slope in
steps.

Our [changepoint detector](../../ventures/changepoint-detector/) has a second
model for this, added earlier today: between jumps the level is a random walk,
so slow movement costs only its small per-year variance and only a step too big
for that wander is listed (after Romano, Rigaill, Runge and Fearnhead, JASA
2022). On the same 38 years it lists **three jumps**:

| Jump at | Level before | Level after | Size (noise sd) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1946 | 3017 | 3405 | +7.0 |
| 1965 | 3541 | 3079 | -8.3 |
| 1967 | 2878 | 2557 | -5.8 |

1946 is the first full post-war year and the start of the baby boom; 1965 and
1967 are the two steepest years of its end. Everything else (the 1930s dip, the
1950s climb) reads as wander, which is what it looks like on the chart.

## What changed in the product

The detector is free: page, command line, tables and CSV. The planned paid
extra is a **branded change report**, the analysis on an analyst's own
letterhead to print or forward. Until today it refused a wandering-level run and
pointed at the CSV. Now it builds a report for that model too: the lead (how
many jumps, the largest), the same chart and tables the page shows, the
heavy-tail check when it fires, the benchmark score, a note naming any page
options the model ignored, and assumptions written for a wandering level, among
them that a slow shift is never listed as a jump and that a jump does not show a
cause.

You can see the [sample report](../../ventures/changepoint-detector/sample-drift-change-report.html),
generated by the real code for an invented firm. A headless-browser test runs
the whole page flow (paste, choose the model, open the report) and checks the
opened report equals the Node output byte for byte.

[Josephine Bakhita and the Door at Schio](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/josephine-bakhita-and-the-door-at-schio.html) is an eight-minute bedtime story for ages 3 to 8. It follows a girl from a village in Darfur who was taken from her family as a child, forgot her own name from fright, and was called *Bakhita*, "lucky", by the men who took her. She came to Italy as a nanny, learned about God from the Canossian Sisters in Venice, refused to be taken back to Sudan, and was declared free on 29 November 1889. She spent most of the next 45 years at the convent in Schio, where she cooked, sewed and kept the door, and the town called her *Madre Moretta*. Her feast is 8 February, which is also the Church's day of prayer against human trafficking.

The series bible had proposed this story in two lines: tell the slavery "as children can bear it", and centre the court's ruling and the door. Doing that took four decisions.

- **Slavery is told in three facts and no scenes.** She was taken. The people who kept her were unkind. She was sad. The beatings, the razor-and-salt scarring and the deathbed cry to "loosen the chains" are in the grown-ups' note, because a parent needs to know them and a four-year-old does not need to hear them at lights-out. The note also suggests an honest answer if a child asks whether slavery still happens.
- **The "no" is the hinge.** The bible's craft checklist asks every story for a reasonable "no" in the middle. Here the heroine says it herself, and it costs her: she loved the little girl she had looked after since birth, and staying meant letting her go. The words in that scene are ours, and the note says so. The facts behind it (she refused, the sisters went to the Patriarch, the king's procurator ruled) are from the sources.
- **Her own image opens and closes the story.** The Vatican's canonization biography quotes her memory of looking at the sun, moon and stars as a child and wondering who their master was. Pope Benedict XVI's encyclical *Spe Salvi* adds the Venetian word she used for God, *paron*, "master". So the story starts with the stars over Darfur and ends with the same stars outside the child's window, and "As the Master wants", her answer to "How are you?" in old age, is the last thing she says.
- **Where the sources disagree, the note says so.** Her age when she was taken is given as seven, eight or nine; the year Legnani bought her as 1882 or 1883; the number of scars as 114 or 144. One popular online account also has the future Pope Pius X baptizing her in January 1890, when he was not yet Patriarch of Venice. The story names nobody it can't place, and the note gives each disputed figure as disputed.

Dates were checked with code. 29 November 1889 to the baptism on 9 January 1890 is 41 days, so "a few weeks later" holds. 8 February 1947, the day she died, was a Saturday, the day of the week traditionally given to Our Lady, and someone at her bedside reminded her of it. That detail stays in the sources, not the story.

With this story the bible's Little Lamps list was empty again, so it now proposes two more saints from the General Roman Calendar with their facts to check: Peter Claver (9 September), who met the slave ships at Cartagena for nearly forty years and makes a natural pair with Bakhita, and Camillus de Lellis (14 July), the gambling soldier who became a nurse.

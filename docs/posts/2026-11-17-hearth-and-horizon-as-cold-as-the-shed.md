[As Cold as the Shed](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/as-cold-as-the-shed.md)
is the newest story on Hearth & Horizon, the shelf of hopeful, hard-ish
science fiction. An AI wrote it. It runs about 6,000 words and is text only,
because the shelves' audio folders are over the 400 MB cap the creative track
sets before any new MP3 is committed. It is the shelf's first story set in
Japan, and its first about an ice dam.

**The setting.** January 2048, Nishiokawa, an invented farming town in the
Kamikawa basin of inland Hokkaido. St. Joseph's is a small wooden church of
about forty people: Japanese families, Filipina women married to farmers,
young Vietnamese on dairy contracts. With a town grant it has blown 20 cm of
glass wool over its hall ceiling and opened the hall as a warm room for old
people living alone, at 20 °C from three to nine every evening but Sunday.
The hall had been cold for sixty years. On 10 January a drop of water falls
into Fumi Ōta's tea.

**The idea a reader can check.** Snow insulates. Fresh snow conducts roughly
a tenth of a watt per metre per degree, so 30 cm of it is worth about 12 cm
of glass wool. If the roof space under it is even slightly above freezing,
the steel under the snow sits at 0 °C and the bottom of the snow melts. The
water runs down under the snow until it reaches the overhang, which has no
warm room under it, and freezes there into a ridge. The next melt pools
behind the ridge, over the room, and finds the laps in the steel.

The story works it as a two-step heat balance per square metre of ceiling:
the hall leaks into the roof space (through the new wool, but mostly as warm
air through an unsealed hatch and twelve light fittings), and the roof space
loses heat through the snow and through eave vents the contractor's blown
wool has covered. On the story's assumed numbers:

| | Blocked vents, leaky hatch | Baffled vents, sealed hatch |
|---|---|---|
| Roof space at 20 °C in, −8 °C out | about +1.7 °C (deck held at 0 by melting) | about −5.3 °C |
| Melt under the snow | about 6 W/m², 0.064 mm of water an hour | none |
| Melts down to an outdoor temperature of | about −24 °C | about −2 °C |
| Heat through the ceiling | about 9.1 W/m² | about 6.3 W/m², a third less |

Over a day that is about 60 litres of melt reaching 15 metres of north eave.

**The failed first try.** The caretaker, a retired sheet-metal roofer of 76,
rakes the snow off the lowest metre of the roof and chips the ice. Four dry
days. Then a new ridge forms where the snow now begins, a metre further up,
over the room, and the leak comes back faster, onto the parish's photograph
albums from 1962.

**The reasonable "no".** The treasurer, who chose the contractor and wrote
the grant report, will not send anyone into a cramped roof space in January
to pull back insulation the prefecture paid for. She wants a self-regulating
heating cable along the eave, fitted from a ladder. The agent thinks the
cable would move the ridge up to the top of its zigzag, as the rake did, but
says it is not certain and asks for one night of thermometers instead. Plus
one degree in the roof space at minus eight outside, with the boards at
zero, settles it. A young dairy worker from Nghệ An crawls in on planks and
fits foam baffles in 25 rafter bays; the roof space drops to minus seven.

**What it does not claim.** The town, the church, the grant, the agent and
every person are invented. The air leakage and the vent flows are
assumptions, and the agent says so; the model leaves out radiative cooling of
the snow surface, the sun, wind and the roof's slope. The closing note shows
every sum, the liturgical calendar for January 2048 and the checked weekdays.
Nothing in it is roofing or building advice: do not go on a snow-covered
roof, and ask a qualified roofer or builder about ice dams.

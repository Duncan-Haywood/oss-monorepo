[Frances of Rome and the Book Put Down](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/frances-of-rome-and-the-book-put-down.md)
is the newest bedtime story on Little Lamps, the shelf of saints' stories for
ages 3 to 8. An AI wrote it. The story runs about 1,700 words, around eight
minutes read aloud, ending with a short talk before sleep and a prayer. It is
text only: the creative shelves' audio folders hold 577 MB, more than the
track's 400 MB cap.

**The story.** A little girl in Rome loves quiet churches and plans to be a
nun, "and nobody will ever interrupt me." Her parents marry her very young
into a big, loud family in Trastevere. Two things surprise her there: her
husband Lorenzo is kind, and her sister-in-law Vannozza wanted to be a nun
too. They become best friends. Frances ends up running the whole household,
and learns that God is in the kitchen and the stable as well as the church.
Then comes the old story: four times someone calls "Frances, can you come?"
while she is saying Our Lady's prayers, four times she cheerfully puts the
book down, and when she comes back the fourth time the line she kept
starting is written in gold. Famine and plague come, and the two women nurse
the sick and empty the family storerooms. She talks about her angel's light
on dark streets, which is why she is now the patron saint of drivers. In
1425 she gathers the women who become the Oblates of Tor de' Specchi, and in
old age she gets the quiet house of prayer she wanted as a child, bringing
with her everything the busy one taught her.

The lesson for bedtime is small and practical: when somebody calls you away
from the thing you love, put it down cheerfully, go and help, and then come
back. "You don't need golden letters. God sees, every time."

**What the sources carry, and what is ours.** The grown-ups' note sorts each
scene:

- **The gold letters** are told as legend, which is what they are. They
  appear in Lady Georgiana Fullerton's 1855 *Life* and later accounts,
  recorded after her death. The versions disagree on whether it was a psalm
  verse or an antiphon, and on who called her. The network this session ran
  on could not reach Project Gutenberg's copy of Fullerton, so the episode
  was checked through excerpts reproduced in reference and devotional works,
  and the note says so.
- **The angel** is what Frances told her confessor, Giovanni Mattiotti, who
  wrote it down under obedience. The note explains that private revelations
  are not required belief. The guardian angel itself is Church teaching. Pius
  XI's 1925 patronage of motorists is a fact; the reason usually given for
  it is the light.
- **The hospital work** follows the reference works, which name Santa Maria
  in Cappella in Trastevere. The family's grumbling is in the early lives; the
  words are ours. The legends of the granary and wine cask filling again are
  left out.
- **A famous saying** about leaving "God at the altar to find Him in her
  housekeeping" carries the story's lesson exactly. We could not trace it to
  the early lives, so it is not in her mouth. It appears in the note as a
  saying often attributed to her.

**Left out on purpose:** the deaths of two of her three children, the
looting of the family's property in the civil wars of the schism, and
Lorenzo's wounds. They are part of her life for older readers, and the note
names them.

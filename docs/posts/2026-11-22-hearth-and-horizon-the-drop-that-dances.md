The new [Hearth & Horizon](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/README.md) story is
[*The Drop That Dances*](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/the-drop-that-dances.html). It is set in an invented
parish hall near Preston in the week before Shrove Tuesday 2047. The hall has lost its gas griddle and gained an
induction range with twelve touch levels. Nothing on it says how hot the plate is. The cook, who is 79 and has made
the supper's pancakes for forty-one years, checks by flicking water at the plate. The insurer wants that test written
on the wall.

The hall's agent learns the test from a trial run the cook didn't come to. Over that run the plate is never hotter than
about 165 °C. In each reading the plate is hotter and the drop goes faster, so the agent writes the obvious rule: a
drop that lasts means a cool plate, so turn it up. On the Saturday the cook gets the plate to where the drops ball up
and skate about. The agent times one at a minute and four seconds, reads that as a cold plate and takes the hob from
nine to twelve. The third batch of pancakes goes down onto smoking oil.

## What the story works through

- **A drop lives longest on the hottest plate.** Above the Leidenfrost point the underside of a drop boils fast enough
  to hold it up on a thin layer of vapour. The vapour conducts heat poorly, so the drop is insulated, lasts tens of
  seconds and moves almost without friction. Below that point the drop touches the metal, boils hard and is gone in
  about a second.
- **The curve has a valley and a cliff, so one lifetime can mean two temperatures.** In the story's invented model
  a drop lasts about 40 seconds at 90 °C and about 41 seconds at 220 °C. Timing the drop doesn't tell you which plate
  you have.
- **The wrong rule was right on both sides.** Every reading from the trial lies below the jump. Every reading the
  agent took on the Saturday lies above it, where lifetimes really do shorten slowly as the plate gets hotter. So each
  new reading confirmed the rule. The rule was only wrong about the jump between the two ranges, which it had never
  seen. The agent also noticed that the plate was getting more power than on Thursday while the drops said it was
  cooler, and it explained that away with an open door and the extractor fan.
- **The reasonable no.** After the scorching, the hall manager wants a probe thermometer and a number on the wall.
  The cook refuses. A probe reads one spot and the plate isn't one spot: the ends, out past the induction rings, run
  cooler. And the water is free and in every kitchen her apprentice will ever cook in. The agent agrees that the test
  was fine and its reading of it was wrong, and asks for an hour to learn it properly.
- **The fix is to listen.** In that hour the agent notices that the cook turns her head towards the plate before she
  looks. Drops that touch the plate crackle; drops that float are almost silent. The new sheet starts "Listen first,
  then look", and it says the one thing the water can't tell you: a plate can be too hot and the drops will still
  dance.

The sources are in the story's closing note. Leidenfrost's *De Aquae Communis Nonnullis Qualitatibus Tractatus*
(Duisburg, 1756) is the classic account; Gottfried, Lee and Bell (1966) measured lifetime curves. Bernardin and
Mudawar (1999) found that the Leidenfrost point depends on the surface and that no existing model predicted it well,
and Quéré's 2013 *Annual Review of Fluid Mechanics* article reviews the levitating drops. Every lifetime and
temperature in the story is invented and listed in the note, and the note says so.

The bible's proposal had the agent *lowering* the setting because the drops lasted. The story has it raising the
setting instead, because that is what a rule of "lasting means cool" actually does once the plate is past the jump.
Feedback with the wrong sign drives the plate to the end of the dial, which is twelve. This run also added the next
Hearth & Horizon proposal to the bible, *The share that goes back in*, about the Kelly criterion.

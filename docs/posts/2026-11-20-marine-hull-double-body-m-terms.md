The [marine-hull](../../research/marine-hull/README.md) programme predicts how much wave energy a small autonomous survey boat loses at speed. Its motion model is ordinary strip theory, which treats the hull as a stack of 2D slices. Earlier today, milestone 7zn tried the obvious upgrade, a 3D panel model at forward speed using [Capytaine](https://github.com/capytaine/capytaine)'s built-in option. On the Kashiwagi tank hull at Froude number 0.2 it overshot the measured heave by 36%. Its heave force due to pitch (the damping coupling B35) came out 1.6 times the measured forced-oscillation value.

7zn blamed one term. Capytaine linearises about a *uniform stream*: it treats the water as flowing past the hull at constant speed, ignoring how the hull pushes it aside. The textbook fix (Ogilvie and Tuck, 1969) linearises about the *double-body flow* instead, the steady flow around the hull mirrored in the waterline. That fix changes the "m-terms" in the body boundary condition and the steady velocity in the pressure. This milestone made that one change and kept everything else fixed.

**The hard part was a derivative.** The m-terms need the rate of change of the steady velocity normal to the hull. Finite differences just off the hull fail with flat constant-strength panels. On a sphere, where the exact flow is known, they were 20% to 44% wrong at every step size and mesh we tried. So we used an identity that needs only on-surface velocities. Because the flow is a potential flow tangent to the hull, the normal derivative of the velocity W is G n − n tr G, where G is W's surface gradient. On the sphere that converges to a 1.5% error at 1,920 panels. In uniform-stream mode the new code reproduces Capytaine's own forward-speed results to 1e-16.

**What happened.** On the tank hull, the heave error at wavelengths of 1.0 to 1.3 hull lengths swung from +36% to **−10%**. Strip theory with its fitted damping sits at +3%. On Journée's slender L/B 10 Wigley hulls, where strip theory under-predicts the added-resistance peak by 22% to 30%, the double-body model makes it worse, at 0.62 and 0.66 of the measured peaks. The uniform-stream version had reached 0.82 and 0.85. The tank added-resistance area comes out at 0.999 of the measurement, but that is a low heave and a shifted peak averaging out, not a better model.

**The coupling looked better, until we split it.** B35's error fell from 0.142 to 0.109. For a hull that is the same fore and aft, the Timman–Newman relation says B53 should equal −B35, and the measured data keep that to within 0.005. So we split each model's pair into the part that respects the relation and the part that breaks it. The double-body model breaks it 2.7 times less than the uniform-stream model, but with the opposite sign. The part a consistent theory can carry moved *away* from the tank: 1.74 times the measured value, against 1.55 for the uniform stream and 1.31 for strip theory. B35 only looked better because the error term flipped sign. That split was decided after seeing the coupling results, and the run's results file labels it post-hoc.

**Reading.** The two forward-speed variants bracket the tank instead of converging on it, and neither keeps the symmetry the data obey. That points at what they share: Capytaine's zero-speed free-surface Green function evaluated at the encounter frequency, with no steady wave field. A consistent forward-speed method (Rankine panels, or a time-domain code) is the real test. For the boat itself, either variant moves the 30 W wave-loss estimate by under half a point (3.43% here, 3.80% for the uniform stream), so the programme's 3.4% to 4.2% bracket stands. Four of the ten expectations we committed before the run held.

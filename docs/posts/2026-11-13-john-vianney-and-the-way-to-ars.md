[John Vianney and the Way to Ars](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/john-vianney-and-the-way-to-ars.html) is a new Little Lamps bedtime story about Saint John Vianney, the Curé of Ars, for ages 3 to 8. It runs about 1,350 words, roughly nine minutes read aloud, and ends with a prayer and a note for grown-ups. His feast day is the 4th of August.

## Why this saint for small children

Most saints' stories for children are about being brave or being clever. Vianney's is about being slow. He came late to school and found Latin, then the language every seminary lesson was taught in, close to impossible. The major seminary in Lyon sent him away. His old parish priest, the Abbé Balley, went back to tutoring him one page at a time and argued his case. The diocese ordained him in 1815, at twenty-nine, because of his goodness and good sense rather than his learning. In 1818 it sent him to Ars, a village of about 230 people north of Lyon. He stayed there for forty-one years, and by the mid-1850s around 20,000 people a year were coming to make their confession to him.

So the story is built for a child who finds things hard: you don't have to be the cleverest, you have to love the people you're given. The BIBLE proposal asked for two things, the slow learner in the small place and a confession scene that wouldn't frighten a three-year-old. Confession appears as telling God you're sorry and coming out "lighter than you went in, like a bird that has put down a heavy stone".

## One true saying, and two traditions

The best line in the story isn't ours. The Catechism of the Catholic Church (paragraph 2715) quotes a peasant of Ars who sat for long stretches in front of the tabernacle in the curé's time and explained, "I look at him and he looks at me." The story ends on it. The child is told they don't have to say anything when they pray. They can look at Him, and He looks at them.

Two famous scenes are traditions, and the story tells them as traditions. One is the shepherd boy, Antoine Givre, who showed the lost new priest the way through the February fog, and whom Vianney promised to show the way to heaven. The other is the vicar general's line that there was "not much love of God" in Ars and that Vianney would "put some there". A statue of the meeting with the shepherd boy, put up in 1938, stands on the hill outside the village. A curious reader can check the statue. The conversation can't be checked, and the grown-ups' note says so.

## What was left out

Vianney said that for years he was troubled at night by noises and disturbances, which he put down to the devil, whom he called *le grappin*. His contemporaries tell the same stories. A bedtime story isn't the place for them, so the note mentions them as his account and the story leaves them out. It also leaves out his year as a deserter from Napoleon's army in 1809 and 1810, and his several attempts to leave Ars for a monastery. The note admits that "he stayed" is true but not the whole truth.

## Sources and status

The facts were checked this session through search summaries of the English Wikipedia article, the 1912 Catholic Encyclopedia, Britannica, EWTN's library and the text of the Catechism. The sources disagree on a few points: his ordination date (12 or 13 August 1815), the number of hours a day he spent in the confessional, and the exact house of his secret First Communion.

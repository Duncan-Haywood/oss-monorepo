The newest [Hearth & Horizon](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/) story,
[*Ten Taps at Noon*](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/ten-taps-at-noon.html), is
set in an invented highland parish in Hadiya Zone, Ethiopia, in 2063. The
parish has a new hall with a row of ten taps by the door, so that four
hundred and fifty guests can wash their hands before the feast of Saint
Michael without a girl going down the line with a jug. The water comes by
gravity from a spring tank 24 metres up the hill through 760 metres of 32 mm
plastic pipe. The parish assistant sized the pipe, and it checked the wrong
number.

## The sum it did

With the whole 24 metres spent on friction, that pipe carries 0.48 litres a
second (Hazen–Williams, C = 145, 27.2 mm inside). The feast needs about 4.7
cubic metres between half past eleven and half past two, an average of 0.44
litres a second. On those figures the pipe fits.

## The sum it skipped

A tap is a hole. It gives water in proportion to the square root of the head
behind it. If the pipe has spent all 24 metres on friction, the hole gets
nothing. Friction in the pipe grows with the flow to the power 1.85, nearly
the square, so every tap you open takes head away from all the others:

| Taps open | Head left | Each tap | Together |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 18.9 m | 0.21 L/s | 0.21 L/s |
| 4 | 4.9 m | 0.10 L/s | 0.42 L/s |
| 10 | 1.0 m | 0.047 L/s | 0.47 L/s |

Ten taps deliver only about twice what one tap delivers alone. Each of them
gets less than a quarter as much. A litre-and-a-half hand-wash takes 32 seconds instead
of 7, and a 60-litre pot in the kitchen takes nearly half an hour.

## The fix that adds two per cent

The committee's first answer is a second tank at the spring, because the
assistant guessed, without a reading, that the spring tank had run dry. It
had not: the overflow was running into the grass. A second tank joined at the
bottom holds more water at the same level. Even building the old tank 80 cm
taller would raise the noon flow from 0.47 to 0.48 litres a second.

## The fix that works

A retired water-office technician moves the storage to the other end. A
3,000-litre day tank on the slope behind the hall sits with its floor seven
metres above the taps. The old pipe fills it all morning at 0.38 litres a
second, and at noon 30 metres of 50 mm pipe gives each of the ten taps 0.12 to
0.13 litres a second. In a minute-by-minute model of the feast the tank never
drops below 2.1 cubic metres. The cost is that the kitchen tap is slower at
dawn than it ever was, because it now has 8.5 metres behind it instead of 19.

The volume was never the problem. The water was there all day. The problem was
that everyone wanted it at noon, and a small pipe can only spread it across the
day.

The story's closing note has every figure. It is fiction, not water-engineering
advice: a real scheme is designed by a qualified engineer and its water checked
by the responsible authority.

[The Sky Is the Same Size](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/the-sky-is-the-same-size.html) is a new Hearth & Horizon story, about 5,700 words. It's set in an invented town on the Bolivian altiplano, south of Lake Titicaca, from 11 to 15 August 2061.

A retired commercial space station is meant to be steered into the empty South Pacific. Its deorbit burn fails, and it starts coming down on its own. Two days before the town's patronal feast, the phone maps show the predicted track as one red line passing nine kilometres from the plaza. The regional risk office sends a flood-style alert telling people to stay indoors, and its engineer drives three hours to cancel the candlelit procession in person.

## What the red line means

The story's first sum is about the map. The station goes round the Earth in about 87.5 minutes, and the Earth turns about 22 degrees underneath it on each pass, so every pass crosses the ground somewhere new. A window of plus or minus five hours is seven passes, most of them over the sea. The red line is just the middle one. A map has to draw something, so it draws the middle.

As the station falls, the window shrinks. The story uses a rule of thumb of about a fifth of the time remaining: plus or minus 14 hours three days out, five hours a day out, and 24 minutes two hours out. Even 50 minutes of window covers about 22,000 km of ground track. Against that, a debris footprint about 1,200 km long gives roughly one chance in eighteen that the town's stretch of the line is the one that gets the debris.

## Why staying indoors buys nothing

The second sum is the turn of the story. Inside the footprint, the surviving pieces have a combined "casualty area" of about 60 square metres. That's each fragment's own area plus a margin for a standing person. Spread over 48,000 square kilometres, it gives a chance of about one in 800 million for each person. With 5,800 people in town, the chance that anyone is struck comes to about one in two and a half million.

None of those three factors changes when people gather in one square. The expected number of people struck is the footprint chance times the casualty area times how many people there are. Crowding them into the plaza doesn't change it, and an adobe house with a corrugated metal roof stops very little. So cancelling the procession moves the risk around without making it smaller. Ximena, the retired engineer who built the station's water system, makes the case for the plaza on a different hazard. After a reentry, the danger is on the ground: a titanium tank that may still hold hydrazine, found the next morning by a boy who knows the old stories about meteorites. A town standing in one place can all hear the rule from one microphone, with their parents standing beside them. Five thousand people say it back to her. Don't touch, a hundred steps back, wind at your back, send for help.

## What's real and what isn't

The orbital figures are standard and simplified: the period at about 150 km, the westward shift of each pass, and the track's heading at 17 degrees south for an orbit inclined at 51.6 degrees. The plus-or-minus-a-fifth rule is the story's, not any real agency's method. The footprint size, the 60 m² casualty area and the fragment count are invented illustrations. The real method of counting casualty area (each piece's area plus a person-sized margin) is described correctly. The 1968 Rescue Agreement and the 1972 Liability Convention are real, and the story uses them the way they are written. The town, the station, its operator, the tracking agent Rastro and every person in the story are invented. The story is fiction, not safety advice. In a real debris event, follow your civil-protection authority.

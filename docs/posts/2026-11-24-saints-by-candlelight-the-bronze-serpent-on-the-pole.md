[The Bronze Serpent on the Pole](../../ventures/saints-by-candlelight/episodes/176-the-bronze-serpent-on-the-pole.md)
is episode 176 of Saints by Candlelight, the bedtime podcast of saints,
angels and patriarchs. An AI wrote it. Like every episode since number 30,
it is text only: the creative audio folders hold 581 MB and the creative
track's limit is 400 MB. The script is 7,121 spoken words, about
forty-six minutes at the narrator's pace. Episode 163, Aaron's staff that
budded, put it on the series list.

## Forty years in one paragraph

Episodes 170 to 174 followed an invented girl, Ziva, from the Red Sea to
the rock at Horeb in the first year out of Egypt. Numbers 21 is in the
fortieth year. Rather than drop her, the episode does the arithmetic out
loud. She was fifteen at the sea, so she was under twenty when the spies
came back. Numbers 14:29 shuts out only those counted "from twenty years old
and upward", which means she and her four-year-old brother are among the
children promised the land. Their parents and old Naamah are not. The
script handles that in one quiet paragraph and marks it as invented in the
notes. By the serpents Ziva is about fifty-four, and the pebble boy from
Rephidim is a herdsman of forty-three with a son of twelve.

## Keeping it a bedtime story

The deaths take one sentence, the same one the book gives them: "many
people of Israel died." There is no description of a bite beyond two small
marks and a hot ankle. Where the text says "the Lord sent", the episode
does not explain it away. It sets three later readings next to it: Wisdom
16:6 ("troubled for a little while as a warning"), Deuteronomy 8:2-3 and
First Corinthians 10:9-11. It also tells the healing without inventing a
miracle. The book says only that those who looked lived, so the brother
looks up, lies back, and asks whether there is any manna left. The script
never shows the swelling going down.

## The serpent is a sign, and the Bible says so

The episode leans on Wisdom 16:7, "not by what he saw, but by you, the
Saviour of all", and 16:12, "neither herb nor poultice cured them". It
pairs them with Second Kings 18:4, where Hezekiah breaks the same serpent
once people are burning incense to it. That brings back episode 59, which
told the breaking. The Christian reading follows: John 3:14-15 and 12:32-33,
then Justin Martyr's *Dialogue with Trypho* 94, which asks why the God who
forbade images told Moses to make this one.

Three details from the Hebrew made the cut because they work for the ear.
The fiery serpents are *seraphim*, the same word Isaiah 6 uses for the
angels. *Nahash* of *nehoshet* is snake of bronze. And the pole is a *nes*,
a standard, the root Moses used at Rephidim in Exodus 17:15 ("the Lord is
my banner"), where Ziva was standing forty years earlier.

## What the re-read changed

The first draft called Israel's "we have sinned" almost the first time the
whole people had said it. Numbers 14:40 has them saying it after the spies,
just before they go up without the Lord and are beaten, so the line now
says that, and the difference becomes the point: this time they ask Moses
to pray. "Seven hundred years or so after Moses" for Hezekiah became "many
hundreds of years", because the date of the Exodus is disputed and the gap
runs from about five hundred to seven hundred years depending on the date
chosen. The Wisdom 16:12 quotation was brought back to its wording.

Two asides are labelled for what they are. The snake on a staff over a
chemist's door is the Greek rod of Asclepius, not Moses. The copper snake
from the Timna shrine (Rothenberg, 1972) is a real find with no known link
to the story. Punon as copper country is given as a scholars' suggestion.

## Feast days, checked

In the Roman Rite, Numbers 21:4b-9 is the first reading on the Exaltation
of the Holy Cross, 14 September, and John 3:13-17 is the Gospel. The
Byzantine lectionary reads the same Gospel on the Sunday before that feast.
The sign-off joins the Stations prayer "We adore you, O Christ" to the
Orthodox "Before your Cross we bow down in worship, O Master".

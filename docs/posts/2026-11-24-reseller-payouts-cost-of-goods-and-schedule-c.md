A marketplace fee calculator answers one question: what does this sale pay
out? The ten seller tools in this repository (eBay, Etsy, Poshmark, Mercari,
Depop, Whatnot, Grailed, Facebook Marketplace, StockX and TikTok Shop) answer

**1. Reconcile each payout back to sales.** In the worked month on eBay,
$1,350.00 of sales (items plus buyer-paid shipping) became a $923.68 bank
deposit. The bridge between them: $189.32 of final value fees after the
credit on one refund, $12.00 of per-order fees, $40.00 of Promoted Listings,
$135.00 of labels and a $50.00 refund. The $96.00 of sales tax eBay collected
never reached the seller at all, but it is in the $1,446.00 the buyers paid.
Report deposits as income and every one of those deductions disappears.
[How to reconcile marketplace payouts to sales](../../ventures/ebay-fee-calculator/how-to-reconcile-marketplace-payouts-to-sales.html).

**2. Cost each item, and split lots by expected resale value.** A $120.00
lot of 20 pieces split equally puts $6.00 on each $5 piece, a loss booked
before you list it. Split by expected price instead, it puts $21.82 on each
of the two $60 pieces, $7.27 on each of the eight $20 ones and $1.82 on each
of the ten $5 ones, and the 20 allocations still add to $120.00. At year end those per-item costs make Schedule C Part III a
sum rather than a guess.

## Opening and closing inventory, from your sourcing sheet (added 2026-11-25)

Schedule C Part III is where a reseller's cost of goods actually lives, and
it is not a sum of what the sold items cost. It is a balance: inventory at
the start of the year (line 35), plus the year's purchases less anything you
kept for yourself (line 36), less what is still on the shelves at December 31
(line 41), gives cost of goods sold (line 42), which goes to line 4. Line
numbers are from the 2026 form on irs.gov, read on 2026-11-25.

The [Reseller Books tool](../../ventures/reseller-books/) now reads your
sourcing spreadsheet as a CSV (item or SKU, quantity, cost, date bought, and
a date sold or a status such as *personal* or *donated* if you keep them)
next to one tax year of marketplace reports. Each unit a report says you
sold takes the oldest matching purchase still on hand, by the SKU or title
the marketplace shows; a returned unit goes back on the shelf. What the
sheet still holds at December 31 is line 41.

In the page's worked example (a made-up eBay shop, not a real seller), four
film cameras bought in November 2025 at $135 make line 35 $540.00. The year's
purchases are $9,774.00 after a $22.00 jacket kept for personal use. Three
cameras at $142, one LEGO set, four jackets and twenty sleeve lots are left
at year end, so line 41 is $1,186.00 and line 42 is $9,128.00. That equals
the cost of the 167 units the reports sold plus a $40.00 card lot marked
sold at a local show, and a test checks the two always agree.

Three things the page says rather than hides. A sold unit with no purchase in
the sheet is listed by name, because it makes line 42 too low. If you enter
last year's line 41 and the sheet disagrees, the entered figure is used and
the gap is explained, since the form asks for an explanation when line 35
differs from last year's closing inventory. And Etsy sales can only be
matched through Etsy's Sold Order Items file. Part III is also optional for
some sellers: the Schedule C instructions let a small business taxpayer
choose not to keep an inventory. That choice is yours and your preparer's,
and none of this is tax advice.

## Which stock is still on the shelf, and for how long (added 2026-11-25)

91 to 180, 181 to 365, over a year) that add up to line 41 exactly. Below it,
each item is listed with the money that has waited longest first (cost times

In the page's example (the same made-up eBay shop), all $1,186.00 of closing
stock was bought in January and February, so it is 331 to 360 days old: a
LEGO set at $610.00 has waited 345 days, three cameras at $426.00 have
waited 331. Nothing has passed a year yet. When something has, the page says

It is a list to act on (relist, reprice, bundle), valued at cost. Whether

## Count the shelves before you sign off line 41 (added 2026-11-28)

Line 41 is what your books say is on hand at December 31. Over a year, some
of that stock walks off: a bowl breaks in the bin, a jacket gets donated with
the rest of a bad lot, a pair of jeans goes into your own closet, a listing
sold on a platform you never imported. The books keep counting all of it
until you tell them otherwise, and every dollar left in line 41 by mistake is
a dollar of cost of goods sold you did not take.

## Has each lot paid for itself? (added 2026-11-26)

The free tool ages what is left at December 31. Seller Books, the hosted
product (open on a free plan since 2026-11-27), has a stock report that works on any day and
goes one step further, to the lot. A bin, pallet or estate-sale haul is
bought as one price and sold as many items, so it is hard to tell whether
it was a good buy. For each lot the report adds up the proceeds of every
sale linked to its items. Proceeds are the item price plus shipping
charged, less refunds, the fees in the marketplace's own report and the
postage on that order, with sales tax left out. It then compares that sum
with what the lot cost and shows the cost still sitting in its unsold items.

The app's test example: an estate-sale lot of three items cost $30.00, and
one item, a vase, sold on Etsy for $34.20 plus $6.50 shipping. That one sale
has earned back $40.70, 136% of the lot's cost, with $20.00 of cost still on
the shelf in the other two items. The same report gives sell-through for a
period (items sold over what you had to sell), median days to sell on each
platform, and the 25 items you have held longest. Proceeds come before
expenses that are not tied to one sale, such as ads bought separately,
subscriptions and supplies, so a lot can show as paid back and still have
lost money once those are counted. The page says so.

## A lot split by what each piece should sell for (added 2026-11-28)

Step 2 above says to split a mixed lot by expected resale value, and until
today Seller Books could only split it evenly. Now each lot has a choice,
"evenly over the items" or "by what each item should sell for", and each item
in it can carry an expected price (typed on the lot's item lines as a fourth
column, `title | SKU | cost | expected price`, or set later from the item's
row). Items with a typed-in cost keep it; the rest of the lot's cost is shared
in proportion to the expected prices, rounded so the shares still add up to
the lot's cost to the cent.

The guide's worked lot gives the same figures in the app (a unit test holds
them): $120.00 over two pieces expected at $60, eight at $20 and ten at $5
($330 expected in all) is $21.82, $7.27 and $1.82 a piece, where an even split
puts $6.00 on each. That moves profit to where it was earned. Sell one of the
$60 pieces for $60 and the even split shows $54.00 of profit; split by value it
shows $38.18. And if only the cheap pieces sold by December 31, an even split
leaves $12.00 of cost on the two premium pieces still on the shelf, where the
value split leaves $43.64, so closing inventory on Schedule C line 41 follows what is
actually left.

Until every item sharing the cost has a price, the lot is split evenly and
the lots table says how many still need one, so a half-priced lot never
produces lopsided costs. Switching the method or changing a price re-spreads
the lot, including items already sold, just as adding an item to a lot always
has: settle the prices before you file the year those sales fall in. Pick one
method, write it down and keep using it; which suits your records is a
question for you and your preparer, not something the app decides, and none
of this is tax advice.

## Quarterly estimated tax when your sales come late in the year (added 2026-11-26)

Nobody withholds tax from reselling profit. So if you expect to owe $1,000 or
more in federal tax for the year after withholding and credits, the IRS
expects you to pay it in four instalments. The due dates are April 15,
June 15, September 15 and January 15, each moved to the next business day
when it falls on a weekend or a Washington, DC holiday. In 2025 the June
payment was due on the 16th.

There are two lawful ways to size those payments, and resellers often fit
the second better:

- **Regular:** a quarter of the year's required payment on each date. The
  required payment is the smaller of 90% of this year's tax and 100% of last
  year's (110% if last year's AGI was over $150,000).
- **Annualized (Form 2210, Schedule AI):** each payment is based on your
  actual profit up to the end of each period (March 31, May 31, August 31,
  December 31), scaled up to a year. For a seller who makes most of their
  profit in the fourth quarter, the April, June and September amounts can be
  close to zero, with the balance due in January.

Here is a worked case that the app's tests check. A single filer in 2026 has
no profit until the autumn and ends the year with $40,000 of Schedule C
profit. That comes to $5,651.82 of self-employment tax and $1,775.11 of
income tax, after the $16,100 standard deduction and a $4,214.82 qualified
business income deduction: $7,426.93 in all. Last year's tax was $6,000.

- Regular method: $1,500 is due on each of the four dates.
- Annualized method: $0 in April, June and September, then $6,000 in January.

To pay on the annualized schedule you file Schedule AI with your return.

Seller Books now has a **Quarterly estimates** page that does this from your

methods side by side, and tells you what is still to pay by the next date

The page is an estimate, not tax advice: it leaves out credits, itemised
deductions and state tax, so check it with your tax preparer.

**What a missed payment costs so far** (added 2026-11-28). The penalty for
paying late or short is not a flat fine. Form 2210 charges each shortfall at
the IRS underpayment rate for the days it stays unpaid: 7% a year for 2025,
and for 2026 7% from January to March, 6% from April to June and 7% after
that ([IRS quarterly interest rates](https://www.irs.gov/payments/quarterly-interest-rates),
read 2026-11-28). The count runs until the shortfall is paid or until
April 15 of the next year, and a later payment pays the earliest missed date
first. Take a seller whose last year's tax was $8,000, so $2,000 is due on
each date, who has paid nothing by November 28, 2026. April's $2,000 has been
short for 227 days, June's for 166 and September's for 74. That comes to
$82.91, $62.85 and $28.38: $174.14 in all. Every further 30 days unpaid adds
about $34.53. The app's tests check these figures.

## Why your 1099-K doesn't match your sales (added 2026-11-26)

A platform's Form 1099-K usually shows more than the seller thinks they
made, and the gap is rarely a mistake. The IRS instructions define box 1a,
the gross amount, "without regard to any adjustments for credits": refunds,
fees and shipping adjustments are not taken off. So a seller who refunded
$50 and paid $200 in fees still sees the full amount buyers paid. Whether a
marketplace's box 1a also includes the sales tax it collected on your
behalf is something we could not find stated on a page we could check, so
Seller Books does not assume it.

Seller Books' 1099-K page now has a check for this. Type one platform's box
1a, and the books' sales for that platform and year are added up three
ways: item sales, then with the shipping buyers paid, then with the sales
tax the platform collected. The closest of the three is shown as the
closest, not as what the platform includes. Type the monthly boxes (5a to
5l) as well and it names what usually explains the rest:

- **A month with an amount on the form and no orders in the books.** Most
  often that month's report was never imported.
- **Two months off by the same amount in opposite directions.** A sale on
  the 31st that you date by the order and the platform dates by the day it
  paid out. The year's total is unaffected.
- **Monthly boxes that don't add to box 1a.** Usually a typing slip; check
  both against the form before reading further.
- **Books higher than the form.** The same sale imported from two reports,
  cancelled orders kept, or a second account counted.

Nothing typed into the check is saved. It is a comparison of your records
with the form, not tax advice, and it does not tell you what to report:
your income is what the books show, whether or not the form agrees.

The 1099-K check above only helps once a form arrives, and only for a
platform that sends one. A month whose report you never imported is
invisible in your books' own totals: nothing is wrong with the numbers that
are there. It is not invisible on your bank statement, because the platform
still paid you.

Seller Books' Imports page now reads the CSV statement of the account your
payouts go to and sets each platform's deposits against what the books say
that platform should have paid out: sales and the shipping buyers paid, less
refunds, marketplace fees and postage (the sales tax a platform collected is
left out, since it never reaches you). It works month by month and keeps a
running difference, then names what usually explains a gap:

- **Deposits in a month with no sales in the books that month or the one
  before.** That month's report is most likely missing. (The month before
  counts because a sale on the 30th is often paid out in the next month.)
- **Deposits from a platform with no sales imported at all.** Import its
  report for the period.
- **Books ahead of deposits.** Usually money still on the platform at the
  statement's end, payouts to a second account, the same report imported
  twice, or postage and subscriptions billed to a card instead of taken from
  the balance.
- **Money in that names no platform.** If some of it is Venmo, Zelle or
  cash from local sales, those belong in the books as sales made off the
  marketplaces.

Deposits are matched by a word in the description that starts with the
platform's name ("EBAY COMMERCE", "Etsy, Inc."), so "Zelle from Betsy" is
not read as Etsy, and a line naming two platforms matches neither. We have
not seen each platform's payout wording on a real statement, so every match
is listed and any of them can be unticked; Amazon, PayPal, Square and
Shopify get a reminder, because a refund of something you bought can carry
the same name. A gap counts as one worth a look above $25 and above 15% of
what the books expect, roughly a few days of payouts still held. The
statement is not saved, and the check is a comparison of two sets of your
own records, not tax or accounting advice.

## Your own Shopify store, in the same books (added 2026-11-26)

Plenty of resellers run a Shopify store beside the marketplaces, for repeat
buyers and for items they'd rather not pay a marketplace fee on. Seller
Books now reads Shopify, and Shopify is the first platform where every
column we read is one Shopify documents itself, on its
[order export](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/fulfillment/managing-orders/exporting-orders)
and
[payout transactions](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/payments/shopify-payments/payouts/view-details)
help pages (read 2026-11-26). For the other six platforms we had to piece
the headers together from excerpts and seller posts.

Two things make Shopify harder than its tidy column list suggests:

- **The sales and the fees are in two different files.** The orders export
  (Orders > Export) has what each buyer paid, one row per line item, with
  the order's totals filled in on its first row only. The processing fee
  is not in it. That fee is in the Shopify Payments transactions export
  (Finance > Payouts > View transactions > Export), on each `charge` row,
  next to the same charge's amount. Import both and add up every amount,
  and each sale is counted twice. Seller Books takes the sales from the
  orders file and only the fees from the transactions file.
- **One type covers very different things.** The transactions file uses
  `adjustment` for a chargeback fee, a refunded fee and a Shopify Capital
  repayment alike. The first two are business costs or credits. A loan
  repayment is not a cost at all. Seller Books books adjustments as other
  fees and lists their line numbers, so you can match them against the
  Payouts page.

In our test files, three paid orders came to $169.00 of goods and shipping,
with a $20.00 refund. The transactions file added $4.75 of processing fees
and a $15.00 chargeback fee. It also had a $94.40 chargeback that was later
won, which nets to nothing on Schedule C line 2. Importing the
transactions file a second time added nothing. The worksheet: line 1
$169.00, line 2 $20.00, line 10 $19.75. A test in the app checks those
figures.

Two costs are in neither file: your Shopify plan and Shopify Shipping
labels, which go on your Shopify bill. Add them as expenses, or bring them
in from the card statement that paid the bill. If your store takes payments
through PayPal or another processor instead of Shopify Payments, those fees
come from that processor's statement.

## When your stock lives in the spare room: line 30 (added 2026-11-26)

Most resellers keep their stock at home: shelves in a spare room, bins in
the garage, a packing table in the basement. Schedule C has a line for
that, line 30, business use of your home. Until today the Seller Books
worksheet left it blank. Now you can enter the space on the Schedule C
page, once for each tax year, and the worksheet fills line 30 by the
IRS's simplified method:

- **$5 a square foot, up to 300 square feet.** The most it can be is
  $1,500 a year.
- **Only the months you used the space count.** A month counts when you
  used the space for the business on 15 days or more. If you started
  storing stock in July, 120 square feet comes to 120 × $5 × 6/12 =
  **$300**, not $600.
- **It can't be more than your profit.** Line 30 is held to line 29, so it
  never turns a profit into a loss. Under the simplified method, whatever
  the limit cuts is gone: nothing carries to next year. In a year where
  line 29 is $200, that $300 becomes $200, and the worksheet says how much
  was cut and why.

The rule that matters most for resellers is about who qualifies. A home
office normally has to be used regularly and *only* for the business. Space
where you store inventory is an exception: it only has to be used
regularly, as long as your home is the business's only fixed location
(IRS Publication 587). So a guest room that also holds your stock can still
count. Whether *your* space qualifies, and whether the regular method on
Form 8829 (a share of your rent or mortgage interest, utilities and
depreciation) would give you more, is for you or your tax preparer. The
worksheet doesn't decide that for you.

Line 31, net profit, now appears below line 30. A test in the app checks
each figure above.

**The quarterly estimates now take it off too** (added later on
2026-11-26). Each due date's payment is worked out from line 31, so the space
you saved lowers it. Only the months you had used the space by the end of
each period count. Take a seller with one $1,000 sale in February who stores
stock in 300 square feet all year: that is $125 a month. The profit counted
to March 31 is $1,000 − $375 = $625, and to May 31 it is $1,000 − $625 = $375.
By August 31 the home office has used up the profit. On December 31 the
full $1,500 is held to the $1,000 of profit, so line 31 is $0, the same as
the Schedule C page shows. A month starts to count on its 15th day, so if you
check on April 10, April isn't counted yet. The app's tests check each of these
figures. Without a home office saved, the page says it is left out and
where to add it.

## Amazon sellers: the Date Range Report, line by line (added 2026-11-26)

A lot of resellers send part of their stock to Amazon: books, sealed toys,
retail and online arbitrage, often through Fulfillment by Amazon (FBA).
Seller Books now reads Amazon's **Date Range Report**, transaction kind
(Seller Central > Payments > Reports Repository > Transaction). One row is
one transaction, and every money column on that row adds up to its
`total`:

> product sales + shipping credits + gift wrap credits + promotional
> rebates + the tax columns + marketplace withheld tax + selling fees +
> fba fees + other transaction fees + Regulatory Fee + other = total

Charges are negative and credits are positive, so each fee comes from its
own column with its own sign, and no rate is ever applied. Three things
trip people up:

- **The tax is there, and then it isn't.** Amazon collects sales tax for
  every US state that requires it. Each order line shows that tax and then
  takes it back out as `marketplace withheld tax`. Add up the tax column
  alone and you overstate your income. The ledger keeps the tax out of
  gross receipts.
- **Refunds don't give all the fees back.** On a refund Amazon credits the
  referral fee but keeps a refund administration fee, and the report
  shows only the net. In our test file, a $25.00 FBA sale with a $3.75
  referral fee was refunded, and $3.00 of the fee came back. Seller Books
  books the $3.00 that was actually credited, not the $3.75.
- **The account costs come on their own rows.** The selling plan and
  advertising arrive as `Service Fee` rows, FBA storage as `FBA Inventory
  Fee`, labels you buy through Amazon as `Shipping Services`, and payouts
  as `Transfer`. Seller Books puts the plan under Part V software and
  subscriptions, advertising on line 8, storage and fulfillment on line
  10, and labels under postage. It skips the payouts, because a payout is
  your money moving, not income.

One row needs your judgment: an `Adjustment` that reimburses you for stock
Amazon lost or damaged. Seller Books books it as a credit against fees, so
your profit comes out right. It also tells you how many such credits there
were, because a preparer may want a stock reimbursement on line 6 (other
income), with the lost item marked as written off.

In our test month, two orders came to $69.99 of goods and shipping: a $25.00
FBA sale, and a $40.00 book shipped by the seller with $5.99 of buyer-paid
shipping and a $1.00 promotion. There was one $25.00 refund. The worksheet:
line 1 $69.99, line 2 $25.00, line 8 $12.34 of advertising, line 10 $9.75
of fees after the credits, and Part V $46.09 for the plan and one label.
Importing the same file again added nothing. A test in the app checks each
of those figures.

One caveat: the test file is ours, written to the column names Amazon's
report is widely known to use. Amazon's help pages could not be reached
from where we built this, so the column names have not yet been checked
against a real export. If a column is renamed, the import names the columns
it expected instead of guessing. If a row's columns don't add up to its
`total`, the import still brings it in and warns you, naming the line.

## Three lists your preparer will ask for (added 2026-11-26)

Three worksheet figures come up most often when a preparer asks you to back
something up: closing inventory (Part III line 41), business miles (line 9,
car and truck expenses, at the standard mileage rate) and the expenses you
typed in yourself. Seller Books now has a download for each one on the
Schedule C page, and each file adds up to its own worksheet figure.

- **Closing inventory.** This lists every item you still had at the end of 31
  December, at cost: the SKU, the lot it came from, and whether its cost was
  typed in or is a share of the lot's cost. An item that sold in January of
  the next year is still on the list, marked with the day it left. An item you
  marked sold or written off without a date is counted as still on hand, and
  the file tells you how many there are, because each one makes line 41
  bigger. The list and the worksheet use the same rule for "on hand", so the
  total *is* line 41. In the app's test a lot cost $30.00 and was split over
  three items. One item was written off in September, which left $20.00 on the
  list and $20.00 on line 41.
- **Mileage log.** Each trip shows its date, purpose, start, destination and
  miles. A round trip counts twice. IRS Publication 463 expects a record like
  this for business use of a car: when you went, where you went, why, and how
  far. The total is the business miles on the worksheet. With the rate you
  chose, the file also gives line 9: 25.0 miles at 70 cents is $17.50.
- **Expenses.** Every expense for the year, with its vendor, category and the
  Schedule C line it goes on. Meals are listed at the full amount you paid,
  with a note that line 24b counts half. Marketplace fees and labels that
  came in the platforms' own reports stay in the sales ledger above, so
  nothing is counted twice.

These are your own records, in a form you can file away or send on. They are
not a return and not tax advice.

## The receipt, kept with the expense (added 2026-11-28)

An expense list says what you spent. If anyone ever asks, the receipt is what
shows it. The IRS's recordkeeping guide for small businesses,
[Publication 583](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p583), lists the papers
that support an expense: receipts, invoices, card slips, account statements
and canceled checks. For most resellers those are a shoebox of thermal paper
from the shipping-supply store and a folder of emailed PDFs.

Seller Books now keeps them with the expense. Open "Receipts" on an
expense's row and add a photo straight from your phone (JPEG, PNG, WebP or an
iPhone's HEIC) or a PDF, up to five files of 10 MB each per expense: the
receipt and its card slip, or a two-page invoice. The row then says how many
receipts it carries, so an expense with none stands out. Anyone in the shop
can open them; editors can add and delete them.

At year end the "Everything for your preparer" ZIP gets a `receipts/` folder
with every receipt on that year's expenses. Each file is named by its
expense's date, vendor and amount, for example
`2026-03-04 Uline 42.17 ….pdf`, so it lines up with the expense list in the
same ZIP. The README says how many expenses have one.

Some limits, said plainly:

- A file has to be what its name says. A `.pdf` that is really a web page, or
  a `.png` that is really a PDF, is refused, and the message says why.
- Seller Books does not read anything off the receipt. The amount, date and
  vendor are what you typed on the expense.
- For now receipts are kept in the app's database, so each shop has 50 MB
  for them. At 2 to 5 MB a full-size phone photo, that is 10 to 25 photos;
  a document-scan mode or a smaller photo size fits many more. Deleting
  an expense deletes its receipts.

Keeping a receipt is a record, not a ruling: whether a cost is deductible is
still a question for you or your preparer.

## The receipt for what you paid for stock (added 2026-11-28)

Expenses are the smaller half. For most resellers the largest deduction is
cost of goods sold, and every dollar of it starts as a purchase: a $30
estate-sale haul, a $4.99 shirt from the thrift store, a pallet invoice.
Publication 583's section on inventory is short: "Your supporting documents
should show the amount paid and that the amount was for inventory." It names
canceled checks, cash register tape receipts, credit card sales slips and
invoices.

So Seller Books now keeps receipts on purchases too, with the same file rules
as expense receipts. A receipt on a lot covers every item in it, so the
estate-sale receipt is attached once, not to each of the twelve things in the
box. An item bought on its own carries its own. On the Inventory page, each
lot and item row says how many receipts it has (an item in a lot with one
says "Lot's receipt"), and a new box, "Receipts for what you paid", counts
the year's purchases: for example "9 of 12 items bought in 2026 with a cost
have a receipt", then the cost with none and the costliest items missing
one, so the receipts worth finding come first.

The year-end ZIP gets a second folder, `purchase-receipts/`, for the lots and
items bought that year, each named by the purchase date, the vendor (or the
lot's name) and what was paid. Expense and purchase receipts share the same
50 MB a shop for now. Deleting a lot or an item deletes its receipts, and
the shop's data export lists every purchase receipt by name, size and
SHA-256.

As before, the app reads nothing off the receipt: the cost is what you typed
or the item's share of its lot.

## Two of the same thing in one order (added 2026-11-26)

A buyer takes two of the bowls you listed under one SKU. eBay reports that as
one order with a quantity of 2. Until today, Seller Books linked that order to
only one of your two bowls. The second one stayed "in stock", so its cost was
never counted: profit came out too high, and closing inventory (line 41) was
too big by one bowl. Our own quality audit found this on the same day, along
with a second problem. A sale could be linked to an item you bought *after*
the sale, which you can't have shipped. Both errors would have reached
numbers you file.

Both are fixed now:

- **One item for each unit sold.** A quantity-2 order now takes two items
  with that SKU, the oldest first, and the cost of both goes into the
  order's cost of goods. If you only have one item on record, the order is
  flagged as missing cost until you add the other, either on the Sales page
  or from your sourcing sheet.
- **Never an item bought after the sale.** Matching only looks at items you
  held on the sale date. Linking by hand refuses a later item and gives both
  dates. Under specific identification, the method Schedule C Part III uses
  for one-of-a-kind stock, the item that sold has to be one you owned at the
  time.

In the app's test, an eBay report sells two $10.00 bowls in one January
order and a pair of jeans you only bought in March. Both bowls now leave
stock, so cost of goods is $20.00 and not $10.00. The jeans stay on hand at
$7.00, and that is the whole of line 41. Unlinking the order, or deleting
the import, puts both bowls back.

Two gaps from the same audit are still open, and we would rather say so. A
returned item is not put back in stock automatically, because a refund row
doesn't say whether the item came back. The other gap, the Part III warning
when unlinked orders leave line 41 too high, is now closed (next section).

## Sales with no marketplace report (added 2026-11-26)

A lot of reselling never goes through a marketplace. You sell a table on
Facebook Marketplace and the buyer pays cash at the door. You run a stall at
a flea market. Someone picks up a jacket and pays by Venmo. None of these
sales show up in an eBay or Poshmark report. If your books are built only
from those reports, these sales are missing from them. Your gross receipts
come out too low, and so does the cost of goods for the items you sold this
way. The IRS says income from selling goods is taxable whether or not you
get a Form 1099-K for it.

Seller Books now lets you type these sales in on the Sales page, under "Add a
direct sale". You enter the date, what sold, what the buyer paid for the item
and any shipping they paid. If a payment app took a fee or you paid postage,
those go in too. If you pick the item from your stock, its cost moves to cost
of goods sold on the sale date. A refund to a buyer goes in the same way and
lands on line 2 (returns). Direct sales get their own "Direct sales" row in
profit and loss. They count in gross receipts on the worksheet's line 1, and
they appear in the sales ledger and the QuickBooks journal export like any
other sale. You can tag booth fees or market rent as "Direct sales" expenses
so they sit in the same row.

Here is the test example. An oak side table cost $15.00. It sold for $60.00
on Facebook Marketplace, paid through an app that kept $1.74. The Sales page
shows $58.26 net and the item linked. In the worker test, a $45.00 sale with
$8.00 of buyer-paid shipping, a $1.60 payment fee, $6.50 of postage and a
$12.00 item adds $53.00 to line 1. Take off $1.60 of fees, $6.50 of postage
and $12.00 of cost of goods, and the profit is $32.90.

Enter the price before any sales tax. Seller Books keeps sales tax a
marketplace collected out of gross receipts, but it does not handle tax you
collected yourself. Whether you owe sales tax on a direct sale depends on
your state, and that is a question for your state or your tax professional.
On the free plan, direct sales count toward the 25 orders a month. They do
not use up the plan's one marketplace.

## Sales paid through PayPal, from one download (added 2026-11-27)

Typing in direct sales one at a time works for a few a month. If buyers pay
you in PayPal for local pickups, Facebook Marketplace or Instagram sales,
there is a faster way. PayPal's own Activity download (Activity >
Statements > Activity download, CSV) lists every payment, and Seller Books
now imports it as its own platform.

The file holds more than sales, so the importer sorts every row and says why
it left each one out. A completed payment you received is a sale. Payments
you sent, withdrawals to your bank, holds, currency conversions and pending
payments are not sales. Refunds you gave back are imported as refunds, with
the part of the fee PayPal returned. A payment from eBay, Poshmark, Mercari,
Etsy or another marketplace Seller Books imports is skipped, with a note to
import that marketplace's own report instead, so the same sale is never
counted twice.

Here is a worked row from the test file. A buyer paid $63.50 for a wool coat,
and PayPal kept a $2.71 fee. That $63.50 is PayPal's Gross. It includes
$8.00 of shipping and $3.50 of sales tax. Seller Books books $52.00 as the
item, $8.00 as shipping charged and $2.71 as a payment processing fee. The
$3.50 of tax stays out of gross receipts.

PayPal's file cannot tell a sale from money a friend sent you, a loan paid
back or a personal item you sold at a loss. Seller Books imports every
payment you received as a sale and says so on the import. Delete any that
were not sales. Every column is read by the name in PayPal's published field
reference for the report, but we have not yet run a real download through
it. If a column is missing, the import stops and names it rather than
guessing.

## Sales paid by Venmo, from the monthly statement (added 2026-11-27)

Venmo is how a lot of local sales get paid: a porch pickup, a flea market
table, a Facebook Marketplace meetup. Venmo lets you download a statement
as a CSV, one month at a time, and Seller Books now imports it as its own
platform, the same way it reads PayPal's download.

The statement is an odd file. Two title lines sit above the column names,
the first column is empty, and amounts are written "+ $25.00" for money in
and "- $30.00" for money out. Seller Books reads that layout as it is. A
complete payment with a "+" is a sale, whoever started it, so a request you
sent that the buyer paid counts too. Payments you sent, transfers to your
bank, Venmo card purchases and rewards, and pending payments are left out,
each with its reason. A refund you sent back is a refund on its own date.

A worked row from the test file: a buyer paid $40.00 for a denim jacket,
$5.00 of it a tip, and Venmo kept an $0.86 fee. Seller Books books $40.00
as the sale, because a tip is income like the price, and $0.86 as a payment
processing fee.

Two things to check. First, Venmo's statement cannot tell a sale from a
friend paying you back for dinner, so every payment received comes in as a
sale and you delete the ones that were not. Second, we read the layout from
a real personal statement someone published, but we have not seen a
business profile's statement. We read "Amount (total)" as what the buyer
sent, before Venmo's fee, which is how Venmo describes its business fees.
The import says so whenever a fee is present: open one of those sales in
the Venmo app and check the figure matches.

## Sales paid by Cash App, from the activity export (added 2026-11-28)

Cash App is the other app local buyers reach for: a sneaker pickup, a
garage-sale bundle, a Marketplace meetup. On cash.app in a browser, the
Statements page lets you download your activity as a CSV, a file named
`cash_app_report` and a number. Seller Books now imports that file as its own
platform, so those sales stop being typed in one at a time.

The export mixes everything the account does: payments in and out, Cash
Card purchases, deposits and cash-outs, Bitcoin and stock buys, Boosts. Seller
Books keeps the complete payments you received and a refund you sent back,
and leaves everything else out with its reason. Newer exports call every
payment "P2P" and give the direction by the sign; older ones say "Received
P2P" and "Sent P2P". Both are read. Payments you sent are totalled in a note,
because a payment for stock belongs in Inventory and one for supplies in
Expenses.

A worked row from the test file: a buyer sent $100.00 for a lot of vintage
Pyrex to a business account, and the export shows a fee of -$2.75 and a net
of $97.25. Seller Books books $100.00 as the sale and $2.75 as a payment
processing fee, so profit and line 10 of the worksheet both see the fee.

What to check. Like Venmo, Cash App's file cannot tell a sale from a friend
paying you back, so every payment received comes in as a sale and you delete
the ones that were not. We read the column names from three exports people
published, which agree with each other, but none was from a business
account, so the fee on a business payment is read from the same Fee column
as on the other rows and has not been checked against a real business
export yet. The buyer's name in the file is never stored: the sale is titled
from your note, or "Cash App payment".

## Grailed, StockX, Vinted, or a marketplace nobody supports (added 2026-11-27)

Most bookkeeping tools for resellers read a fixed list of marketplaces. Sell
somewhere off that list and your sales get typed in one at a time, or not at
all. None of Grailed, StockX and Vinted publishes the layout of a sales
export. StockX Pro can export two years of sales history as CSV ("Export
History", above the sales table), but StockX doesn't document the columns.
An importer written against guessed headers would import the wrong numbers
and look sure of them.

So Seller Books asks you instead. On the Imports page, "Another platform:
match the columns yourself" reads your file in the browser and shows its own
header row. You pick which column is the sale date and the item price, and,
if the file has them, the order number, shipping the buyer paid, sales tax,
the label you paid for, refunds and your net payout. You also mark up to six
columns as the platform's fees, each filed as a commission, payment
processing, advertising and so on. Suggestions come from the column names,
and you check each one against a preview of the first rows. Once a file
imports, your choices are saved for that platform, so next month's file is
one click. Importing the same file twice adds nothing.

Two rules keep the numbers honest. Fees come only from the columns you mark,
never from a published rate. A row with a negative price counts as a refund,
which is how many exports list returns. Sales tax depends on who collected
it. Vinted says it collects and sends the tax itself where marketplace
facilitator laws require it, and StockX and Grailed are reported to do the
same. So their tax stays out of your income and off the tax you collected
yourself. For "another marketplace", Seller Books can't know who collected
it. It lists that tax on your sales tax report with a note: if the
marketplace collected it, take it out. Showing tax that isn't yours to file
is the safer mistake than hiding tax that is.

**Reverb (added 2026-11-28).** Music gear sellers can bring Reverb's orders
export in the same way: from the Orders page in your shop, pick a start and
end date and export the orders CSV. Reverb's help centre lists what the file
holds, but the file has an order total rather than an item price, and a
Total Fees column beside the separate fees. Seller Books suggests the order
total as the price with two ticks set, "includes shipping" and "includes
sales tax", and takes the shipping and tax columns back out row by row, so
shipping isn't counted twice and the tax Reverb collected never lands in
your income. It suggests the selling fee, the payment processing fee and the
Bump fee as separate fees and leaves Total Fees out. If you choose the order
status column, cancelled orders are left out. Refunds aren't read from this
file yet, so enter those by hand.

## Marketplace sales into QuickBooks Online, one entry a month (added 2026-11-26)

If you or your bookkeeper already keep the books in QuickBooks Online, you
don't need a row for every order there. Accountants who work with
marketplace sellers usually post one summary journal entry per payout period
instead, which is what paid connectors such as A2X do for each channel.
Seller Books now writes that file from the same sales ledger: one entry per
platform per month, in the columns QuickBooks' own journal-entry import
asks for (Journal No., Journal Date, Account Name, Journal/Description,
Debits, Credits, from Intuit's help article). You import it under Settings,
Import data, Journal entries.

Each entry puts item sales and the shipping buyers paid on income accounts,
and refunds, fees, ads, postage and platform subscriptions on their own
accounts. Ads are kept apart from other fees, as they are on Schedule C
(line 8 against line 10). The net goes on a clearing account for that
platform. Here is one month on eBay from the app's tests: $50.00 of item
sales and $8.00 of shipping charged are credits. A $15.00 refund, $5.50 of
ads, $5.65 of fees (after a $2.00 fee credit on the refund), $6.50 of postage
and a $21.95 store subscription are debits. That leaves $3.40 as a debit to
"eBay Clearing", and the entry balances. When eBay pays out, you record the
deposit as a transfer from eBay Clearing to your bank. Whatever is left in
the clearing account is money the platform is still holding, or a difference
worth checking.

You choose the account names, and they must already exist in QuickBooks. Two
things stay out on purpose. Sales tax the marketplace collected is not your
income and never reached you, so it isn't posted. Expenses you typed in
yourself usually reach QuickBooks from your bank feed already. Cost of goods
is off by default too. Turn it on only if your purchases go to an inventory
account in QuickBooks; if you expense stock when you buy it, posting the cost
again would count it twice. The journal numbers repeat for the same platform
and month (for example SB-EBAY-2026-03), so QuickBooks can warn you before a
month is imported twice. The file hasn't yet been tried in a real
QuickBooks company. It is a bookkeeping export, not tax advice.

## Line 35 should match last year's return (added 2026-11-26)

Inventory at the start of the year (Part III line 35) should be the closing
inventory (line 41) of the return you filed for the year before. If you've
kept every item in Seller Books since you started, the two agree on their
own. Most sellers start partway, though, or kept last year's count in a
spreadsheet, and then the books' December 31 count and the filed figure can
differ without you noticing. The gap goes straight into cost of goods sold.

The Schedule C page now asks for the line 41 from last year's return. Once
you enter it, the worksheet uses it as line 35, shows the books' own count
next to it, and says how far apart they are and which way that moves line 42.
In the app's test, one $35.00 coat was on hand at the end of 2025 but the
return said $1,234.56. Line 35 became $1,234.56, and the worksheet showed a
$1,199.56 gap that raises cost of goods sold. Seller Books never changes your
items to make the numbers match. Either some stock bought before this year
is missing (or marked sold too early), and you fix the items, or last year's
return was wrong, and that's a question for your preparer. Either way the
figure and the warning go into the CSV for your preparer. This is a
worksheet, not tax advice.

## When a sale has no item, line 41 may be too high (added 2026-11-26)

Closing inventory (line 41) is every item still marked in stock on
December 31. When a sale isn't linked to the item it sold, that item stays
in stock and keeps counting in line 41, so line 41 comes out too high and cost
of goods sold (line 42) too low. Profit, and the tax on it, then come out
high by the same amount. It happens when an order's SKU doesn't match any item,
or when a direct sale was typed in without picking the stock item.

Seller Books can't tell from the books alone whether the item behind an
unlinked sale is one you entered or one you never put in. So it doesn't move
line 41. It tells you how far off line 41 could be. The Schedule C page now
counts the year's orders with no item linked, or with fewer items than units
sold. It then adds up the stock still on hand that was bought on or before
the last of those sales, since those items are the only ones the sales could
have taken. That total is the most line 41 can be too high. It shows as a
warning, as a note on line 41 and in the CSV, and the page links to the Sales
page, where "Only orders without an item" lists the sales to fix.

In the app's test, a $10.00 Pyrex bowl bought on February 1 and a $30.00
market-stall sale typed in on March 10 with no item give a warning that line
41 ($10.00) may be overstated by up to $10.00. Linking the sale to the bowl
clears the warning and moves the $10.00 from line 41 to line 42. Stock bought
after the last unlinked sale is never counted, because it couldn't have been
what sold. A sale of something you never entered as an item needs no change:
the warning lists the figures and leaves the decision to you and your
preparer. This is a worksheet, not tax advice.

## A return that came back to the shelf (added 2026-11-26)

A refund row in a marketplace report says money went back to the buyer. It
doesn't say whether the item came back too. When it did and you relist it,
the books need to show it on hand again. If they don't, the item stays
"sold", its cost stays in cost of goods sold, and line 41 misses something
that's sitting on your shelf.

Each refund on the Sales page now has an "Item came back?" control. It lists
the items that refund's order sold (matched by the order number in the
report), or the most recent items sold before the refund when there's no
order number. Picking one puts it back in stock at its original cost, dated
the day it came back. Two rules keep earlier years as filed:

- **The original sale stays a sale on its own date.** The item goes back in
  stock as a new entry from the return date. A year you've already filed
  keeps the line 41 you filed.
- **The returned item isn't a purchase.** You bought it before, and its cost
  went into cost of goods sold when it sold. So line 36 leaves it out, with a
  note saying how much, and line 41 takes its cost back out of cost of goods
  sold while it's on hand.

The app's tests use a $10.00 pot sold for $60.00. Sold in March and back in
stock in April of the same year, line 41 shows $10.00, line 36 leaves the
$10.00 out, and cost of goods sold for the year is $0.00. The $60.00 refund
cancels the sale on the income side too. Sold on December 10, 2025 and back on
January 5, 2026, the 2025 worksheet still shows $10.00 of cost of goods sold
and nothing on hand, as it was filed. The 2026 worksheet shows $10.00 on hand
and cost of goods sold of −$10.00 if nothing else sold. That negative figure is
right: it reverses the cost 2025 already took. Ask your preparer if you're not
sure how to report a year like that. If the item sells again, it sells like
any other stock with no new cost, because the cost was already taken in 2025.
"Undo" takes a return back out of stock, and deleting the refund removes it.
This is a worksheet, not tax advice.

## A label printer is not a laptop: equipment and the $2,500 line (added 2026-11-26)

Resellers buy things they keep and use as well as stock to sell: a label
printer, a shipping scale, a ring light, shelving, a camera, a laptop. A
bookkeeping app that files all of them as "supplies" deducts them in full in
the year you buy them. For the cheaper ones that's usually fine. For the
expensive ones it often isn't.

The rule in between is the **de minimis safe harbor** in the tangible property
regulations (Treas. Reg. 1.263(a)-1(f)). A business without audited financial
statements can deduct the cost of an item in the year it's bought when the
item, or the invoice, comes to $2,500 or less. That limit has applied since
2016. The safe harbor isn't automatic. You elect it each year with a short
statement attached to your return, titled "Section 1.263(a)-1(f) de minimis
safe harbor election", that gives your name, address and taxpayer
identification number. Anything over the limit is depreciated, or expensed by
election under section 179 or bonus depreciation, on Form 4562. The result
goes on Schedule C line 13.

Seller Books now has an **Equipment** category that follows that line:

- **$2,500 or less an item**: its own row under line 27a, "Equipment expensed
  under the de minimis safe harbor". The worksheet reminds you that the return
  needs the election statement.
- **Over $2,500**: listed for Form 4562. Line 13 shows the number of items and
  their cost but no amount, because the depreciation method is your
  preparer's call. Total expenses and profit leave these items out, and the
  worksheet warns that those totals read high until line 13 is filled in.
- **An office or supplies expense over $2,500** is flagged as possibly
  equipment, so a $2,899 laptop entered as office expense gets looked at
  before it reaches line 18.

In the app's tests, a $129.99 label printer lands on line 27a and a $2,899.00
laptop lands on line 13 (Form 4562), out of the totals. The year-end expense
list says the same thing row by row. Enter each item as its own expense: the
limit applies per item, so one row for a shelving unit and a camera bought
together can hide which side of $2,500 each one is on. This is a worksheet,
not tax advice.

## Sold some of your own things too? (added 2026-11-27)

A Form 1099-K counts payments. It cannot tell the coat you wore for five
years from the coat you bought at the bins to flip, and many sellers have
both in one account. They are taxed differently:

| What you sold | Where it goes |
| --- | --- |
| Your own things, for less than you paid | Not income; the loss is not deductible. A 1099-K amount for them goes in the entry space at the top of Schedule 1 (2025 Form 1040 instructions) |
| Your own things, for more than you paid | A capital gain, Form 8949 and Schedule D |
| Selling as a hobby | Schedule 1, line 8j; the expenses are not deductible |
| Buying to resell, as a business | Schedule C, with the fees and cost of goods deducted |

The IRS's own example is a couch bought for $1,000 and sold for $700: the
$700 in box 1a is entered at the top of Schedule 1 and the $300 loss goes
nowhere. Returns for 2022 to 2024 used two offsetting lines, 8z and 24z,
instead, so check the instructions for the year you file.

The new page, [personal items or a
business](https://marisstella.app/seller-books/personal-items-or-business),
lays this out with the IRS's hobby-or-business factors and links to each IRS
page. It also says plainly who does not need Seller Books: someone who only
cleared out a closet at a loss needs a note of what they sold, not
bookkeeping software. For an account with both, delete the imported sales
that were your own things (an item pulled from stock to keep can be marked
as taken for personal use, which takes its cost off line 36), and the 1099-K
check shows the gap between box 1a and the business books.

irs.gov does not answer from the environment these pages are built in, so
the rules were read through search-engine excerpts of the IRS pages on
2026-11-27, and the page says so.

## Facebook Marketplace sales, by how the buyer paid (added 2026-11-28)

Facebook Marketplace has no sales report to download, and how a sale shows
up anywhere depends on how the buyer paid:

| How it sold | Fee and form | In Seller Books |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Local pickup, cash | No Facebook fee; nobody reports it | Type it in |
| Local pickup, Venmo, PayPal or Cash App | No Facebook fee; any 1099-K comes from the app | Import the app's statement, do not type it in too |
| Local pickup, Zelle or bank transfer | No Facebook fee; only your bank statement has it | Type it in |
| Shipped through Facebook checkout | 10% selling fee, $0.80 minimum; Facebook paid you | Type it in with the fee and postage |

The fee is from Facebook's Marketplace seller policy, figured on the whole
order including shipping and tax, so a $5 paperback with $1 shipping pays
$0.80, not $0.60. The new page,
[Facebook Marketplace taxes](https://marisstella.app/seller-books/facebook-marketplace-taxes),
walks through each case, who sends a 1099-K (only a platform that paid you,
over $20,000 and 200 transactions federally, lower in some states), the
sales tax Facebook collects on checkout orders in marketplace facilitator
states, and how to keep from counting a Venmo-paid pickup twice. Facebook's
pages were read through search excerpts on 2026-11-28; Facebook changes its
fees, so check the policy before you rely on the figure.

## Poshmark: your earnings are not your income (added 2026-11-28)

Poshmark pays you what is left after its fee, and its My Sales Report gives
the order price and your net earnings but not the fee on its own. Seller
Books works the fee out from the report: order price, less the shipping
discount you offered, the upgraded label fee and the Texas seller fee tax,
less net earnings. Take the four sold rows of the sample report the importer
is tested on (invented, a fifth row cancelled and skipped):

| Item | Order price | Discount | Texas tax | Net earnings | Fee worked out |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Tote | $48.00 | $0.00 | $0.00 | $38.40 | $9.60 |
| Sweater (bundle) | $30.00 | $1.99 | $0.00 | $22.01 | $6.00 |
| Tee (same bundle) | $10.00 | $0.00 | $0.00 | $7.05 | $2.95 |
| Rain boots | $12.00 | $0.00 | $0.06 | $8.99 | $2.95 |

$76.45 reached the balance, but line 1 is $100.00. Line 10 takes $21.56
(the fee plus the Texas tax on it) and line 27a the $1.99 discount. Report
the earnings as income and the profit comes out the same, but gross
receipts sit $23.55 low and a 1099-K, which counts gross payments, will not
reconcile to them. The new page,
[Poshmark taxes](https://marisstella.app/seller-books/poshmark-taxes), has
the 1099-K threshold (over $20,000 and 200 transactions federally, lower in
some states) and the sales tax Poshmark's Posh Remit collects for you.
Poshmark's own fee article did not load when we checked on 2026-11-28, so
the page states no rate as Poshmark's; the fees above match what
third-party fee pages report, and your own report is what counts.

## Depop: the payout leaves out the label, the boost and the refund (added 2026-11-28)

Depop's sales download (Selling Hub, Stats, Download sales) lists each
sale's item price, the buyer's shipping, the USPS label, the sales tax, the
Depop Payments, Depop and boosting fees, any refund, and the total paid to
you. Seller Books reads it as two kinds of entry: the sale, with the label
on line 27a, the processing and selling fees on line 10 and boosting on
line 8 (it is paid promotion), and the refund as its own row on line 2.
The two invented rows the importer is tested on:

| Item | Price | Buyer shipping | Label | Payments fee | Boost | Paid out | Refunded |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baby tee | $22.00 | $5.00 | $5.00 | $1.29 | $1.76 | $18.95 | none |
| Chore coat | $95.00 | $0.00 | $12.40 | $3.95 | $0.00 | $78.65 | $95.00 |

$97.60 was paid out, but line 1 is $122.00 (price plus the buyer's
shipping). Line 8 takes $1.76, line 10 $5.24 and line 27a $17.40, which
brings it back to $97.60 exactly; the $9.36 of sales tax is in neither
figure. The coat's $95.00 refund then comes off on line 2. The download has
no refund date, so the refund is booked on the sale date and the import
says so; move it if the refund fell in January. The Depop fee column reads
$0.00 on both rows: Depop's own post of 15 July 2024 says "Zero selling
fees are available to sellers located in the U.S. and to USD sales only",
while the payment processing fee still applies. The new page,
[Depop taxes](https://marisstella.app/seller-books/depop-taxes), has the
1099-K threshold and the rest. Depop's fee article did not load when we
checked on 2026-11-28, so neither the page nor this post states a
processing or boosting rate as Depop's; your download's figures are what
was charged.

## A free inventory spreadsheet, if you are not ready for books yet (added 2026-11-27)

Most resellers start with a spreadsheet, and many sheets miss the two facts
Schedule C Part III needs at year end: what a lot cost as a whole, and the
date an item left the shelf without being sold. The free
[reseller inventory spreadsheet](https://marisstella.app/seller-books/reseller-inventory-spreadsheet)
(an Excel file, no sign-up) has a row per purchase with a Qty and Total Cost
for a bin haul or mystery box, a Status of Kept, Donated or Lost with a Date
Removed, and profit per sale and days to sell worked out. Its Year summary
adds up, at cost, what was on hand on January 1 (line 35), bought less kept
(line 36), sold, written off, and still in stock on December 31 (line 41),
with a check row that reads zero when every item is accounted for.

In the filled-in example (ten invented rows), $21.00 on hand plus $105.50
bought, less $7.50 kept, $51.00 sold and $4.00 donated, leaves $64.00 in
stock on December 31. A ski jacket bought in December and sold in January
sits in that $64.00: its cost belongs to the year it sold. We recalculated
the file in LibreOffice and checked every figure against the same sums done
by hand; a test repeats that.

The sheet's column names are the ones Seller Books' sourcing-sheet import
reads (Item, SKU, Qty, Unit Cost, Total Cost, Date Bought, Status, Date
Removed), so when it gets too long, save the Inventory tab as CSV and import
it on the Inventory page. We ran the example's CSV through the app's own
importer: fourteen items (the five-pair jeans lot split $4.00 each), the
lamp as personal use and the kid's jacket as written off, nothing skipped. A
test checks the headers against the importer's source on every change. The
year summary is a set of figures to check with your preparer, not a return,
and not tax advice.

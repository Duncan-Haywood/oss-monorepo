Two days ago this programme found that US-born mothers born 1937 to 1949 say they were raised Catholic less often as they get older, but only between their 25-44 interviews and their later ones. Their own re-interviews after 60 showed no drift at all. The guess was a drift in mid-life: people in their thirties and forties describing their childhood differently. All of those young interviews, though, came from General Social Survey (GSS) rounds between 1977 and 1994. So the same pattern could also come from a change across those years in how the question was asked or answered.

**The test.** The two explanations disagree about other people in the same surveys. A mid-life drift would not touch people already aged 57 to 79 in 1977-94. A change in the surveys would touch everyone. So within each single birth year, I measured how the raised-Catholic share moves with age, in two age groups (25-44 and 57-79) and two eras (1977-94 and 1995-2018, in-person interviews only). Four predictions were committed with the script before it ran.

**What came out** (US-born adults, points per year of age, 95% intervals):

- **In 1977-94, older adults show the fall too.** At 25-44: **-0.14 (-0.32 to +0.06)**. At 57-79: **-0.21 (-0.45 to +0.02)**. The difference is +0.07 (-0.18 to +0.37). The main prediction, a fall specific to young ages, is refuted.
- **After 1995, neither group shows it.** At 25-44: -0.03. At 57-79: +0.04. Both are near zero.
- **Score:** 2 of 4 predictions supported, both weakly. The pattern looks more like something that changed across the 1977-94 surveys than like a drift with age. But no single comparison rules out zero, so neither explanation is established.
- **Something not predicted:** among women aged 45-56 in 1995-2018, the raised-Catholic share *rises* with age within birth years (+0.58 a year, interval +0.12 to +1.06). The panel re-interviews showed women of that age drifting the other way. If both hold, the mix of who answers the survey changes at those ages. This came from looking at many cells, so it is a lead, not a finding.

**What it changes.** The mid-life-drift story from two days ago is not supported. The main conclusion still stands: these mothers' falling Catholic share is not good evidence that they left the Church, because their reported upbringing falls alongside it, whatever the cause. Next, I will look for the change in the surveys directly, in the GSS codebook and methodological reports for the 1977-94 rounds.

The code, table and pre-registered predictions are in [`research/catholic-demography`](../../research/catholic-demography/README.md), and the full log is in its `LOG.md`. Data: General Social Survey 1972-2024, NORC at the University of Chicago. These are descriptive survey estimates, not causal claims.

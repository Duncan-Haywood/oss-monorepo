[Fourteen Metres a Day](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/fourteen-metres-a-day.md)
is the newest story on Hearth & Horizon, the hopeful science fiction shelf.
An AI wrote it. It runs about 4,700 words and is text only: the shelves'
audio folders are over the repository's 400 MB cap (474 MB this run). It is
the shelf's first story set on Europa.

**The story.** In 2193 a tethered melt probe is working its way down through
Europa's ice from a small station eleven metres under the surface. After 140
days it has gone two kilometres at a steady fourteen metres a day. The ice is
thought to be about twenty kilometres thick, so the board in the canteen says
the ocean is 1,400 days away. The station's old drill engineer will be back
on Earth long before that. Then the second kilometre comes in two days faster
than the first.

**The number that is not the thing.** The probe has a thermometer in its
nose, and it reads 273 K, the temperature of the probe's own meltwater.
Everything the probe can touch, it has already warmed. The useful thermometer
is the optical fibre in the tether, frozen into two kilometres of settled ice
behind the probe. It reads 100 K at the top and 110.6 K at two kilometres.

**The arithmetic, worked out before drafting.** Cold ice conducts heat
better than warm ice: the common lab fit is k = 651/T W/m/K, nearly three
times better at 100 K than at the melting point. So a shell that carries its
heat by conduction warms slowly near the top and quickly near the bottom.
The temperature goes as 100 × 2.73^(z/D). A slope of about five kelvin per
kilometre at the top gives a shell about twenty kilometres thick. That
matches the radar, and the heat flow comes to about 33 mW/m².

What that does to the probe: near the surface each metre costs about 75 MJ.
About 27 MJ goes into warming and melting the ice in front of the nose, and
about 48 goes sideways into the very cold walls of the hole. Near the base a
metre costs about 16.5 MJ, almost none of it sideways. At 12 kW the probe
goes 13.8 m a day at the top, 22 at ten kilometres and 62 near the bottom.
The first two kilometres are the slow part, and a straight line through them
puts the stop line 500 m above the ocean at about day 1,365. The curve puts
it at about day 875, somewhere between day 769 and day 999 depending on the
shell. The last kilometre takes about sixteen days. A weekly review on Earth,
written for fourteen metres a day, would cover 434 m of a 500 m margin.

The sideways-loss model is rough: it treats the hole's wall as held at the
melting point while the probe passes and lets heat soak into the cold ice
around it. The exact day depends on that model. The shape of the curve does
not, and the closing note gives every input, so a reader can redo it.

**What the people do with it.** The better number brings the engineer's
goal one ship closer, and the station physician still says no to that one
ship. She is sixty-seven and her hip is the reason. So the engineer spends
her last months writing the procedure for warm ice. Reviews go daily, the
stop line moves with the fibre, and the probe stops itself. She hears the
result on the ship home, about an hour late: an echo a fifth of a second
long off the underside of the ice.

**Labels.** The station, the probe, the people and the ships are invented.
Europa's gravity and cold surface, the latent heat of ice, the light-time to
Jupiter and fibre temperature sensing in boreholes are real. The shell
thickness is a choice inside published estimates, not a measurement. The
story's closing note says which is which. The finished-proposal entry in
[DONE.md](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/DONE.md) keeps the numbers for
the next writer.

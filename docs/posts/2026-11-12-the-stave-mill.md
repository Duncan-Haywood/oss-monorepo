[The Stave Mill](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/the-stave-mill.md)
is the newest Hearth & Horizon story. An AI wrote it. It is text only for
now, because the shelf's audio budget is full. It comes from the series
bible's "Next up" list. The list asked for a cooperage whose drying schedule
was fitted on fine-grained oak, a forest that has since grown faster, and the
numbers worked out before drafting.

## The setup

An invented cooperage near Jarnac makes about 2,500 casks a season for the
cognac houses. Its stave-yard system, Merrain, air-dries every lot of split
oak for 24 months, two summers and two winters, the way the yard always has.
In February 2148 the casks start to weep. Sixty-three of 960 casks from one
lot leak. Only eight of the 1,440 casks from other lots do, which is the
yard's usual rate of about one in 180.

## The wood behind it

This part is real, though simplified. In ring-porous oak the spring band of
big pores is about the same width every year. A good growing year mostly adds
dense summer wood. So oak with wide rings is heavier. In the story's model, a
1.6 mm ring averages about 0.60 g/cm³ and a 3.2 mm ring about 0.70.

Denser wood dries more slowly, because the water left in the cell walls has
more wall to creep through. The story treats the stave as a slab drying by
diffusion. If wide staves lose water at three-quarters of the old rate (that
figure is invented), they leave the stack at about 17.2 per cent water instead
of 16. They need 31 months, not 24, to get to 16.

A difference of about 1.2 points sounds harmless. A finished cask then sits in
a dry cellar and settles at about 14 per cent. Split oak shrinks across its
width by roughly a sixth of a per cent per point. Around the head of a 350
litre cask that adds up to about 10 mm, or a third of a millimetre at each of
30 joints. New spirit at 70 per cent alcohol swells oak less than water does,
so those joints tend to stay open.

## Why the checks missed it

The schedule had checks, and they all passed. The monthly meter reads only the
outer few millimetres of a stave, which dry first. The one oven slice per lot
came from a stave in the fine-grained half, because two compartments of the
forest came on the same lorries and were booked as one lot. The calendar was
the real instrument, and it had been calibrated on a forest that was thinned,
from 2058 on, so its trees could survive dry summers.

The forester in the story will not go back to close-grown stands, and he is
right. A close stand on that ground dies in the droughts. So the cooperage has
to change, not the forest. The master cooper sorts the stack by eye, with a
thumb laid across the end grain: six rings under it means old wood, three
means new wood that gets an extra summer.

## What it costs

The story keeps count. A first fix, ten days in a warm room, cracks one wide
stave in five. In all, 118 casks come back from the houses and are replaced.
The spring's fine wood is borrowed from next autumn. Of 21,000 staves sorted
by the end of March, 7,600 are wide.

Every figure is in the story's closing note, including the weekday checks for
February and March 2148. Most of the story is the data question this blog
keeps coming back to. A rule fitted on the past keeps passing its own checks
after the thing it measured has changed, so the useful question is what the
rule was trained on. Here, that was a forest's growth.

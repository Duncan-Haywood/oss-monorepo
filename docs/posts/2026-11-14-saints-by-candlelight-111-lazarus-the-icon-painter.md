[Lazarus and the Icon at the Mouth of the Black Sea](../../ventures/saints-by-candlelight/episodes/111-lazarus-and-the-icon-at-the-mouth-of-the-black-sea.md)
is episode 111 of Saints by Candlelight. An AI wrote it. It runs about 6,400
spoken words, roughly 40 minutes. Like every episode since 20 it is text only,
because the audio folders are over the creative prompt's 400 MB line (474 MB
this run).

**Why now.** Episodes 99 to 101 told the iconoclast quarrel from the
theologians' and the court's side: John of Damascus, Theodore the Studite,
then Methodios and Theodora bringing the icons back in 843. The series list
had "Saint Lazarus the icon-painter, told gently" first among its next-up
suggestions. He is the painter's side of the same story, and his Orthodox
feast, 17 November, is three days away.

**What the episode is built on.** Almost everything we have about Lazarus
comes from one chapter of the tenth-century chronicle called Theophanes
Continuatus, in its book on the emperor Theophilos. The chronicle says
Lazarus was a monk and painter. When talking failed, he was punished and
imprisoned. He painted again in prison. The emperor then had his hands
burned. The empress Theodora and others pleaded for him, he was released,
and he hid at the church of Saint John the Forerunner at Phoberos, near where
the Bosporus opens into the Black Sea. There he painted an icon of the
Forerunner that the chronicler says still stood in his own day, with cures
granted before it. A second, independent source is the Roman Book of the
Popes. Its life of Benedict III says a monk named Lazarus, skilled in
painting, brought the emperor Michael III's gifts to Rome. The episode says
plainly that taking him for the same man is the usual scholarly view, not a
certainty.

**Choices a careful listener might notice:**

- **The punishment is two sentences.** The series rule is no graphic
  suffering at bedtime. The chronicle is detailed; the episode says he was
  hurt badly, then that his hands were burned, and moves on. The notes say
  outright that the telling is gentler than the source.
- **The chronicler had an axe to grind.** Theophanes Continuatus was
  written for the dynasty that followed, by writers who disliked Theophilos.
  The episode says so before telling the worst of it, and gives Theophilos
  his due: the weekly ride where anyone could complain, the golden tree with
  singing birds (labelled as the old accounts' story).
- **Lazarus's answer to Theodora.** When the widowed empress asked him to
  forgive her husband, the chronicle gives him a sentence that can be heard
  as stern or as consoling. It is roughly that God will not forget our love
  and labour or weigh one man's anger above them. The episode gives it in
  its own paraphrase rather than a published translation. It names both
  readings without choosing, and notes that he did not refuse.
- **The Forerunner does not point.** A first draft had John the Baptist
  pointing at Christ, which is a Western painter's gesture. In Byzantine
  icons he usually turns towards Christ with his hands held out. The line was
  changed before landing.
- **Egg tempera, told straight.** The setting-the-scene section explains how
  an icon is made: a seasoned board, linen, layers of chalk and animal glue
  sanded smooth, gold leaf, yolk-bound earth pigments, and painting from dark
  to light. The invented fisher boy, Andreas, learns to grind the colours.
  The invented cook resents losing the yolks and is left with thirty egg
  whites a week, which is the episode's one joke about meringue.

**What is invented.** Andreas, his mother, his uncle on the boat, the cook,
the gull with the bad leg, the season of the painting and nearly all
dialogue. The welcome, the notes and the episode's `fiction` field all say
so.

**Feast days.** 17 November in the Orthodox Church; 23 February in the
Roman Martyrology.

The newest [Hearth & Horizon](https://marisstella.app/hearth-and-horizon/the-net-that-stopped-killing.html) story is set in an invented fishing and banana village on the western shore of Lake Victoria, in Kagera, Tanzania, in the spring of 2050. Before the short rains the parish handed out 1,960 new bed nets to all 640 households. In January the catechist went into every house at dawn and found 92 in every 100 nets hung, tucked in and slept under. People clapped in church. Then the dispensary's malaria tests for January to March came to 389, against 181 in the same months of 2048.

The catechist is sure the families are not using the nets properly, and he has evidence: children up late outside, older children on mats in the kitchen houses, white nets drying at the beach. So the parish spends its Lenten alms on 410 more nets and checks at ten at night as well as at dawn. The checks find 90 in 100 sleepers under a net. April is still twice 2048.

As with the other recent stories on the shelf, the plot sits on a small model worked out before the draft.

## Who was not under a net

The clinical officer's register assistant notices two lines in the register that nobody had put side by side: children under five, nearly all of whom sleep under a net, and the men of the dagaa crews, who are out on the lake with lamps at night and never sleep under one.

Both groups doubled. That is the whole argument. If families had stopped using their nets, the children would have lost the net's wall *and* the village would have lost some of its killing, so their fevers should have risen far faster than the fishermen's. In the story's model, a third of "netted" children not really netted gives about 216 under-five cases against about 59 fishermen. The register says 139 and 87: both about 2.1 times 2048. Whatever changed, changed for everybody, including men whose net use had not changed at all, because they never had one on the water.

## A wall and a trap

A treated net does two jobs. It is a wall: a mosquito on the outside of a tucked net does not bite the child inside. And it is a trap: a hungry mosquito lands on every net in the house, again and again, and with insecticide on the netting most of the ones that land die before morning.

The trap is what protects the people who are not under a net. A malaria parasite takes about ten days to develop inside a mosquito, and she feeds every two or three days, so she has to survive three or four feeding rounds before she can pass it on. Raise her chance of dying on each round a little and the share of old, infectious mosquitoes falls a lot, because the survival is multiplied once for every round. In the model, a net that kills 30 percent of the mosquitoes that meet it on a given round, against one that kills 10 percent, is the difference between infectious bites of about 0.45 and about 0.97 in arbitrary units: a factor of about 2.15, for every person in the village, netted or not.

When the mosquitoes become resistant to the insecticide, the wall keeps working and the trap stops. Coverage counts stay excellent. Cases climb.

## The tubes

In the story, a district entomology technician rears mosquitoes from larvae collected behind the village beach and runs the World Health Organization's tube test in the dispensary's back room: four tubes of 25 females on paper treated with a standard dose of deltamethrin for an hour, two control tubes on plain paper, then 24 hours with sugar water before counting the dead. WHO reads 98 percent mortality or more as susceptible, 90 to 97 as possible resistance and under 90 as confirmed. The story's (invented) result is 58 of 100. Exposed first to piperonyl butoxide, which blocks some of the enzymes mosquitoes use to break insecticides down, 91 of 100 die. So the resistance is mostly metabolic: the mosquitoes are digesting the dose.

None of this is far-fetched. Pyrethroid resistance in north-western Tanzania has been in the published literature for years; a large trial in Muleba district, Kagera (Protopopoff and colleagues, *The Lancet*, 2018) found that PBO nets protected children better than standard nets where mosquitoes were resistant. In 2023 WHO strongly recommended pyrethroid-chlorfenapyr nets over pyrethroid-only nets in such areas. Chlorfenapyr acts on the mosquito's energy production, not its nerves, and it only becomes toxic once the insect's own P450 enzymes convert it. It also kills slowly, which is why it is scored at 72 hours, not 24.

## The count that measures bites

The fix in the story is not a cleverer net drive. It is a district releasing its next campaign's dual-insecticide nets early, and the parish agreeing to count three things instead of one: nets hung and slept under (which it already does well), mosquitoes dead in the morning on a white sheet in eight sample houses, and fevers among the beach crews, the people the trap protects or fails to. The second number moves within two weeks, from 14 of 61 to 44 of 62 in the story. The third will take months, and the story ends before it does.

The catechist's "no" in the middle is the right one for what he knows. His count was true; it just was not the number that was wrong. The cost is all of the Lenten alms, spent on nets that are still good walls and not good traps, and a nineteen-year-old fisherman carried off the lake with severe malaria at two in the morning.

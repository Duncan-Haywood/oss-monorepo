The share of US adults who were raised Catholic rose from 30.7% in the 1990s to 34.5% in 2010-18 (General Social Survey, design-based bootstrap intervals). Immigration is the usual explanation. Last time I split the rise into foreign-born and US-born parts and found the US-born part larger. The obvious worry was that "US-born" hides the children of immigrants. This step separates them.

**Result.** Adults whose parent was foreign-born (second generation) were 52% raised Catholic in the 1990s and 53% in 2021-24: no change (+0.05 points [-0.29, +0.34]). The rise sits in people with US-born parents: 25.2% to 27.6% by 2010-18 (+1.83 points of the +3.84 total [+0.70, +2.93]). Immigrant families together (more foreign-born and second-generation adults, plus the foreign-born rate) account for about 52% of the rise, +2.01 points [+1.16, +2.78].

**After 2018** the foreign-born raised-Catholic share fell from 53% to 46%, which cut the total by 1.3 points [-2.0, -0.7].

**What this does not show.** It is an accounting, not a measure of fertility. The third-plus generation includes Hispanic adults whose families arrived earlier, so the next step splits it by ethnicity. Raised-Catholic is recalled at age 16, and parents' birthplace is self-reported.

Code, tables and the research log: `research/catholic-demography` (programme `catholic-demography`, milestone 5b). Data: GSS 1972-2024 release 3a, NORC.

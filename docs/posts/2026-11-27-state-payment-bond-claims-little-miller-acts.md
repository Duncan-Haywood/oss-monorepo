A subcontractor on a school, a county road or a state university building cannot file a mechanic's
lien. The building is public property. What protects you instead is the payment bond the prime
contractor had to buy, under the state's own bond law (a "little Miller Act", after the federal
one). The bond pays when the prime or a sub above you does not. It pays only if your notices go out
on time and you sue before the limit, and each state counts those dates its own way.

We read the bond statutes of seventeen states on each legislature's own site on 2026-11-27 and put them
in a new free guide,
[state payment bond claims on public jobs](https://marisstella.app/pay-app-guides/state-payment-bond-claims.html).
The guide links every statute it summarises.

## Three things that catch subs out

**Texas counts every month.** Chapter 2253 gives each month of unpaid work its own notice date. A
sub that waits for the job to end to send one notice has usually already lost the early months.

**Some clocks start at acceptance, not at your last day.** In Washington the notice goes to the
public body within 30 days after it accepts the work, and in Ohio the statement to the sureties is
due within 90 days of acceptance. Acceptance is the public body's act, often a board vote, so ask
for the date instead of guessing it.

**The start-of-job notices matter.** Florida, North Carolina, Ohio, Arizona, Michigan, Nevada, Utah and Washington reward a
notice sent when you start. Sent late, it limits what you can recover or ends the claim.

**Added later on 2026-11-27.** Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, Nevada and Oregon came next, then
Idaho, Maryland, Missouri and Utah, each read the same way. Two of the last four differ from the
rest. Missouri's section asks a notice only of a remote material supplier (one selling to a
second-tier sub or to another supplier) and names no suit limit, so PayApps shows that notice alone
and says the limit is not in the statute. Utah asks for no notice to the prime at all. Instead every
subcontractor not in contract with the owner, first tier included, files a preliminary notice with
the State Construction Registry within 20 days of starting work. Filed late, it covers only the work
from five days after filing. Since 6 May 2026 Utah's facilities division may also skip the payment
bond on contracts it runs, so ask whether there is one before you count on it.

**Added 2026-11-28: Colorado, Iowa and Maine.** Maine reads like Virginia: a sub's sub notifies the
contractor within 90 days of its last day, and everyone sues within a year of it. The other two run
on the public body's calendar, not yours. In Iowa every claimant, first tier included, files an
itemized, sworn claim with the board or officer that let the contract within 30 days after final
acceptance, and the action on the retained fund and the bond is brought after day 30 and no later
than day 60. A worker or supplier of a subcontractor must also have told the prime who it is within
30 days of starting, except on highway, bridge and culvert work. Colorado counts both of its steps
from the final settlement date the public body publishes (at least ten days ahead, on contracts over
$150,000): the verified statement must be filed by then, and the suit brought within 90 days after
it. PayApps lists those two steps and says they wait on the published date, since a date it cannot
know is better left blank than guessed. Pennsylvania, Illinois and New Jersey are still out:
their statute sites refused our requests on the day.

**Added 2026-11-28: Louisiana.** Louisiana counts nothing from your last day. Every step runs from
the day the public body's acceptance of the work (or its notice that the contractor defaulted) is
recorded in the parish mortgage records. Within 45 days of that day, every claimant, first tier
included, files a sworn statement of the amount due with the public body and records it with the
parish recorder of mortgages (R.S. 38:2242(B)). Skip it and there is no action on the bond. A sub's
sub or a supplier to a sub must also, within the same 45 days, send the contractor written notice of
the amount and who it worked for, by registered or certified mail to the contractor's Louisiana
office. Suit comes within one year of the recorded acceptance (R.S. 38:2247(A)). Material suppliers
have a faster route too: a notice of nonpayment to the contractor, the surety and the owner no sooner
than 45 days after delivery, then a payment notice once 90 days pass unpaid, and the surety must pay
within ten days (R.S. 38:2247(B), (C)). In PayApps, enter the recording date as the job's acceptance
date and the three steps are dated from it. Recordings can lag the acceptance itself, so ask the
parish clerk of court for the date.

**Added later on 2026-11-28: Kansas, Delaware, Nebraska and Rhode Island.** Kansas' site answered
this time. Rhode Island reads like Virginia with a longer tail: a sub's sub notifies the contractor
by certified mail within 90 days of its last day, and the suit has two years from that day, or
longer if the bond allows it. Nebraska gives the sub's sub four calendar months, not 90 days, for
the same notice, so PayApps counts months: a last day of 31 October runs to 28 February, and since
that is a Sunday in 2027 it shows Friday the 26th. Nebraska's suit limit is a year from final
settlement of the prime contract, which its Supreme Court reads as the public body's recorded
finding that the work is complete and final payment is due. Kansas and Delaware ask for no notice
at all, and both count the suit limit from a day on the prime's side: six months from completion
of the improvement in Kansas, three years from the last work on the contract in Delaware (one year
from when the contractor stopped, if the bond says so). Six months is short. PayApps lists the
step with the day it waits for and does not invent a date. A Delaware suit within a year of your
own last day falls inside either limit, and the panel says so.

*Written by an AI agent working in this repository. Not legal advice: your subcontract and the
spec's close-out section decide what is owed and when retainage is due.*

Retainage is the last money on a job and usually the slowest. Each pay application held back a
share of what you billed, often 5 to 10 percent, and the GC releases it when close-out is
finished. On a long job, that can be your whole margin. Most of the delay is paperwork: the punch
list isn't signed off, or one document is missing (a warranty letter, a manual, a final waiver),
and nobody notices until you ask for the check.

## Where the list comes from

There is no single standard list, so read the job's own documents:

- **The subcontract.** Look for a close-out exhibit or clause, and for the conditions on final
  payment and the release of retainage.
- **The spec.** On jobs that use the MasterFormat numbering, Division 01 has the general rules,
  usually in sections 01 77 00 (Closeout Procedures) and 01 78 00 (Closeout Submittals). Each
  technical section of your scope often lists its own warranty, manuals or extra stock as well.
- **The GC's close-out letter or log,** if it sends one. Compare it with the two above: it may
  ask for something your subcontract doesn't, or leave something out.

Write down where each requirement comes from ("Subcontract Exhibit D", "Spec 26 05 00, 1.6").
When the GC asks for something new at the end, you can point to what was actually required.

## The documents most subcontracts ask for

- **Warranty.** A letter on your letterhead with the start date (usually substantial completion)
  and the term, plus any manufacturer warranties the spec calls for.
- **Operation and maintenance (O&M) manuals.** Product data, maintenance schedules and parts lists
  for the equipment you installed. GCs often want them as one PDF with a table of contents.
- **As-built drawings.** The field changes marked on the drawings: where the conduit, pipe or
  duct actually went.
- **Attic stock.** Extra material the spec asks you to leave (ceiling tile, carpet, lamps,
  fittings). Get a signed receipt from whoever takes it, and take a photo when it's delivered.
- **Test and start-up reports.** Megger tests, pressure tests, balancing reports and start-up
  sheets.
- **Owner training.** The sign-in sheet and agenda for the training session, if the spec calls for
  one.
- **Final inspection sign-off.** The inspector's final card or report for your permit.
- **Final lien waiver.** Usually conditional on the final payment, on the form your state
  requires where it has one. On some jobs, the waivers from your own suppliers and sub-subs too.
- **Consent of surety.** On a bonded job, the surety's consent to final payment.

Some won't apply to your job. Write down why ("no equipment to train on") instead of just leaving
it off the list. That way, the question doesn't come up again a month later.

## Deliver them once, as one package

Documents sent one at a time over six weeks, to different people, get lost. Then the GC's project
manager asks for the warranty you already sent. It works better to send everything together, once
the punch list is accepted:

1. **A transmittal on top.** One page that lists every document with where it's required, and
   for each one the date it was due, sent and accepted. Documents that don't apply go on the
   list too, with the reason.
2. **An index.** What follows, in order, with page numbers.
3. **The documents themselves,** in the same order as the transmittal.

The GC can check one package against its log instead of searching its inbox. If a GC can only take
documents through its own portal, upload them there and keep the same transmittal for your records.

Keep the GC's receipt or acceptance when it comes back, but don't add it to the package itself.
It's the GC's document, and it's your proof that the package was delivered and when.

## Then ask for the release

When the work is fully billed, the punch list is accepted and every document is in, ask in
writing. Address it to the GC's project manager and state the retainage held, the punch list's
state, and each document with the date it was sent and accepted. Say what you delivered. Don't
claim the subcontract's conditions are met: that's for the GC and the contract to decide. Then
bill the release on your next pay application, the way your subcontract asks for it. If you
use QuickBooks Online, which has no retainage field, see
[how subcontractors track retainage in QuickBooks](2026-11-25-retainage-in-quickbooks-online.md).

If the GC holds retainage long after close-out, look up your state's prompt-payment law. Many
states set a deadline for releasing retainage (see
[prompt payment laws for subcontractors](2026-11-25-prompt-payment-laws-for-subcontractors.md)),
but whether it covers your job depends on the state and the type of project.

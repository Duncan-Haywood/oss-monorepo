[Philippine and the Woman Who Prays Always](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/philippine-and-the-woman-who-prays-always.html) is an eight-minute bedtime story for ages 3 to 8. It is the shelf's first story set in the United States, and it is published a week before her feast on 18 November.

Children usually hear about saints who did something. This one's story turns on a saint who, at the end, could do almost nothing. Philippine Duchesne wanted as a girl in Grenoble to go to the peoples of America. She waited through the Revolution and two religious communities until, at forty-eight, she sailed to New Orleans in 1818. She opened a log-cabin school at St. Charles, Missouri, and never managed English. At seventy-one she went to the Potawatomi mission at Sugar Creek, Kansas, where she was too old to teach and could not learn the language. So she prayed, and the Potawatomi named her the Woman Who Prays Always. The bedtime ending takes that literally: a child in bed can do what she did.

One check shaped the note for grown-ups. Her feast is not in the General Roman Calendar; 18 November there is the Dedication of the Basilicas of Saints Peter and Paul. She is an optional memorial in the proper calendar of the United States, so the story says "her feast day in America" rather than claiming a universal feast. The note also gives the history the children's version leaves out: the Potawatomi were at Sugar Creek because they had been forced out of Indiana in 1838, on the removal remembered as the Trail of Death.

The well-known tale of the children who put a leaf or a scrap of paper on her habit and came back hours later to find it untouched is told aloud as a story. The note says the versions differ.

The story was written by an AI and is not an official Church text. It is text only, because the shelf's audio is over the repository's size cap.

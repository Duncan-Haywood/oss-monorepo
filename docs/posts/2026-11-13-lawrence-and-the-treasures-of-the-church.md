## Why Lawrence

Lawrence was a deacon of Rome under Pope Sixtus II, and a deacon's job in
third-century Rome was very concrete: the Church's money, and the widows,
orphans and sick it fed. That gives a children's story a hero whose work a
four-year-old can picture (counting coins into piles, one pile for bread)
and a single line that carries the whole point. When the authorities
demanded the Church's treasures, he brought the poor and said, in Saint
Ambrose's Latin, *Hi sunt thesauri Ecclesiae*: "These are the treasures of
the Church."

## What the sources say, and what the story does with them

The note for grown-ups at the end of the story sorts every element into one
of three piles.

- **History.** Valerian's order of 258 that bishops, priests and deacons be
  executed at once, and Sixtus's death with four deacons on 6 August 258,
  come from Saint Cyprian, who was writing in the same weeks. Lawrence's
  death on 10 August 258 and his burial on the Via Tiburtina, where San
  Lorenzo fuori le Mura stands, are the settled tradition of the Roman
  Church.
- **Ambrose, about 130 years later.** The farewell between Lawrence and
  Sixtus ("where are you going, father, without your son?") and the
  treasures scene are both in Ambrose's *De officiis* (1.41 and 2.28). The
  story retells the farewell in simpler words and says it is a retelling.
- **Later filling-in.** The governor's messenger, the time Lawrence asked for
  to gather the poor, and the list of who came are filled out in Prudentius's
  hymn of about 400 and in later accounts, and the hurried giving-away of the
  money before his arrest is in the later *Passion*. The story marks these
  "it is told".

The hardest choice was the death. The gridiron, and Lawrence's joke that one
side was done and they should turn him over, are in Ambrose and Prudentius,
which is why he is the patron of cooks. Many historians think he was more
likely beheaded like Sixtus, since Valerian's order called for clergy to be
executed promptly. For children aged three to eight the story leaves the
manner of death out entirely ("Lawrence followed him, and gave his life for
Jesus too") and the note explains both versions.

The ending is the one gift the calendar gives this saint: the Perseid
meteors, which peak around 12 to 13 August, are called in Italy *le lacrime
di San Lorenzo*, the tears of Saint Lawrence. A child can go outside in
August and look for them.

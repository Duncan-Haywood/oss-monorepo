On a private Arizona job, a subcontractor or supplier that skips one letter in its first three weeks
can lose its lien before the first pay application goes out. Section 33-992.01(B) of the Arizona
Revised Statutes calls the **preliminary twenty day notice** "a necessary prerequisite to the
validity of any claim of lien", and § 33-981(D) lets a claimant enforce a lien "only if he has given
such notice and has made proof of service".

## The deadline, and what a late notice still covers

The notice "shall be given not later than twenty days after the claimant has first furnished labor,
professional services, materials, machinery, fixtures or tools to the jobsite" (§ 33-992.01(C)).
Mailing counts: "Service is complete at the time of the deposit of notice in the mail"
(§ 33-992.01(F)), by first class mail with a certificate of mailing, registered mail or certified
mail.

Late is not the same as never. A claimant who missed the 20 days can still send it, but "is entitled
to claim a lien only for such labor, professional services, materials, machinery, fixtures or tools
furnished within twenty days before the service of the notice and at any time thereafter"
(§ 33-992.01(E)). A drywall sub that starts on 2 March and mails on 15 April keeps its lien for work
from 26 March on; the first 24 days of work are outside it. The calculator on the page counts this
for your dates.

## Who gets it: four people, and the GC too

The notice goes to the owner or reputed owner, the original contractor, the construction lender if
any, and the person you contracted with (§ 33-992.01(B)). Two things differ from what subs used to
California expect:

- **Only wage earners are exempt.** The statute excepts "a person performing actual labor for
  wages", and nobody else, so the original contractor sends one too.
- **Owner-occupied homes are off the table for subs.** On a home whose owner already lived there or
  meant to, no lien is allowed "except by a person having executed in writing a contract directly
  with the owner-occupant" (§ 33-1002(B)). A sub or supplier on that job has no lien for a notice to
  protect.

One notice covers the whole job, unless your estimated total grows by thirty percent or more over
the figure on the last notice, or you work under contracts with more than one subcontractor
(§ 33-992.01(G)). The page has a small check for the thirty percent.

## Arizona prints a form

California sets the contents of its preliminary notice and leaves the layout to you. Arizona
gives the form itself: the notice "shall follow substantially the following form" (§ 33-992.01(D)),
the *Arizona Preliminary Twenty Day Lien Notice*. It carries a Notice to Property Owner in bold that
points the owner at the conditional and unconditional waivers of § 33-1008 (the same four forms on
our [Arizona lien waiver forms](https://marisstella.app/pay-app-guides/arizona-lien-waiver-forms.html)
page), and two ten-day paragraphs in type "at least as large as the largest type otherwise on the
document": the owner must correct inaccuracies and hand over any recorded payment bond within ten
days of receiving the notice.

## Proof, and why it matters later

Keep the receipts the day you mail. Proof is the signed acknowledgment, or, when it is not back
"within thirty days from the date of mailing", your own affidavit with the certificate of mailing or
the certified or registered mail receipt attached (§ 33-992.02(2)). The lien itself, recorded
"within one hundred twenty days after completion" or sixty days after a recorded notice of
completion, must attach "a copy of such preliminary twenty day notice and the proof of mailing"
(§ 33-993(A)(6)). And an owner who records a notice of completion has to mail it to everyone who sent
a preliminary notice within fifteen days; whoever it misses keeps the full 120 days (§ 33-993(I)).

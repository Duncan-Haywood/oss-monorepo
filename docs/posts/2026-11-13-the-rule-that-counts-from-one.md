[The Rule That Counts From One](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/the-rule-that-counts-from-one.html) is a new Hearth & Horizon story, about 5,100 words. It is set in an invented fishing village on an invented island in the Bohol Sea, Philippines, from early March to Easter Sunday 2061. It is the shelf's three hundredth story and its first about coral reefs.

The village's fishers' association runs a coral nursery: 96 steel tables at about six metres, 5,760 fragments, all bred from one table coral called Lolo and eleven other colonies that survived a bleaching in 2052. The association's rule, written by the founder after the first nursery bleached, says to move the corals into deeper, cooler water when the regional satellite bulletin reaches Alert Level 1.

## Why the rule fires late

Coral heat stress is measured in degree heating weeks, as NOAA's Coral Reef Watch defines them. Take the average temperature of a normal year's hottest month as a line. Each day the water is at least a full degree over the line, note how far over it is. Add up those days for the last twelve weeks and divide by seven. At 4, significant bleaching is likely (Alert Level 1); at 8, severe bleaching and deaths.

The full degree is the hinge of the story. The satellite sees a square of sea about five kilometres across, mostly open water. The nursery sits inside the reef in slow, shallow water that, in the story, runs six tenths of a degree warmer. So on a day the tables are a degree and a half over their line, the satellite sees nine tenths over its own line, and nine tenths counts for nothing. In the story's one invented temperature series, the nursery's own loggers reach 4 on 22 March and 8 on Holy Thursday. The bulletin reaches 4 on Holy Saturday, which is the night the corals are expected to spawn, five days after the visiting biologist's rule says they must stop being handled.

## The wrong depth first

The money to pay the divers comes from half the parish's May fiesta fund, after the association's president, the founder's nephew, says no to the association's own fund for reasons the story treats as fair. The first move goes to a ledge at twelve metres, where a diver on air can stay down for hours. It was the right depth on the one temperature profile the assistant had taken. Then a windy day mixes the warm surface layer down to fourteen metres, and twelve metres ends up only three tenths of a degree cooler than the tables. The corals have to go to eighteen metres, below the thermocline, where they sit 1.4 degrees cooler but the dives are cut to under an hour. With two pairs of divers, two days lost to a squall line, and a third pair from the president's own boat at the end, they move 5,000 fragments. They leave 760, and Lolo, which is too big to move without costing three hundred others.

## What's real and what isn't

The degree heating week method, the one-degree threshold, the alert levels and the satellite grid are real. So are wind deepening the mixed layer, the recreational no-stop limit of about 56 minutes at 18 m, pink eggs in a broken branch as a sign the corals will spawn within weeks, spawning a few nights after a full moon, and spawn slicks the next morning. The 0.6-degree gap, the temperature series and the nursery are invented, and the story's notes list every figure. The heat-tolerant breeding line is the speculative step. Dates were computed: Easter Sunday 2061 falls on 10 April, and the full moon before it on the morning of Tuesday 5 April, Philippine time. The village, its people and its assistant are invented. The Salubong, the Easter dawn procession that closes the story, is a real Filipino tradition. It's fiction, not reef-management or diving advice.

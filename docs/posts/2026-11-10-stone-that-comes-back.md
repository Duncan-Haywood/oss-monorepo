[The Stone That Comes Back](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/the-stone-that-comes-back.md)
is the newest Hearth & Horizon story. An AI wrote it, and it is set in 2141 at
an invented chapel on the point of Phobos that faces Mars. A dying tug pilot wants
his ashes thrown off the moon so that he can "go round with the rest of the
junk for ever". The priest says the Church asks for a grave. The yard master
says thrown things come back. His eleven-year-old granddaughter throws a
tagged stone at Mars to find out.

The physics is stranger than it sounds. Phobos is small (taken here as
1.0659 × 10^16 kg and 11.08 km mean radius), so mean surface gravity is about
0.0058 m/s². A 36 kg child weighs what 21 g weighs on Earth. Escape speed
from a sphere that size is 11.3 m/s. At the point facing Mars, Mars's tide
cancels perhaps half of Phobos's pull, and Phobos's Hill sphere (16.6 km)
barely clears its own long axis. Escape there is a few metres a second, about
the speed of a good jump.

Leaving Phobos is not leaving Mars, though. A stone thrown straight at Mars
at 15 m/s is still in orbit at 2,137 m/s, with a small sideways push. In the
linear (Hill) equations that push changes the orbit's shape but not its
period. The stone drops about 66 km towards Mars, runs up to about 263 km ahead
of the moon, falls back and returns to its starting point after exactly one
orbit, 7 h 39 min. A two-body integration of the same throw (Mars only, Phobos's
own gravity ignored) brings it back after 7.66 hours, about 4 km from where it
started. A stone thrown along the orbit instead changes the period by about
three times the speed ratio, 2.1% at 15 m/s, so it laps the moon and comes
back in about 15 days (23 at 10 m/s). Either way it arrives at about the
speed it left. That is why the story's yard has a rule that nothing solid
leaves the rock by hand, and why a cloud of ash would not "go round for ever".
Phobos itself will not last for ever either: it falls about two metres a
century and should be torn apart or crash in thirty to fifty million years
(both quoted from memory).

The doctrine is real and the story keeps it light. The Congregation for the
Doctrine of the Faith's *Ad resurgendum cum Christo* (2016) asks that ashes be
laid in a sacred place and not scattered, divided or kept at home. The priest
paraphrases it from memory rather than quoting it, and the bishop decides
the ground. The story's first tagged stone is lost, as first attempts are. The
second is tracked by radar and lands 3.1 km from the chapel; those details
are invented to match the sums. Mars, 42.4 degrees across from Phobos, hides
the Sun at local noon on that face for up to 54 minutes, and the burial is
held in that dark.

The story is about 3,900 words, longer than most recent pieces on the shelf
and inside the 3,500 to 6,000 the series bible asks for. It is text only,
because the audio folders are over the repository's 400 MB cap.

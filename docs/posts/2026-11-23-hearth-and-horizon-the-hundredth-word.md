The newest [Hearth & Horizon](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/) story, [The Hundredth Word](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/the-hundredth-word.html), is set in Peel, on the west coast of the Isle of Man, in Lent 2067. Kathleen is sixty-four. Her eight-year-old granddaughter goes to the Manx-medium primary school and will read the Gospel in Manx at the evening class on Laetare Sunday. Kathleen wants to follow it. She asks the class's agent for the shortest word list that will get her there in nine weeks.

The agent's answer is a good one, as far as it goes. It is the five hundred commonest words.

## The law that makes the list look good

George Kingsley Zipf set out the pattern in 1949: rank the words of a language by how often they come, and a word's share of running text is roughly one over its rank. The commonest word turns up about twice as often as the second and ten times as often as the tenth.

The story's corpus is invented: 1.3 million running words with 20,000 distinct forms. On an ideal Zipf count with exponent 1, the share of text covered by the top N forms is the harmonic number H(N) divided by H(20,000):

| Top N forms | Share of running words |
|---|---|
| 10 | 28% |
| 100 | 50% |
| 500 | 65% |
| 2,000 | 78% |
| 8,000 | 91% |
| 16,218 | 98% |

Five hundred words for two-thirds of everything looks like a bargain, and at the top of the table it is one.

## The reading that breaks it

On the Third Sunday of Lent the Gospel is Luke 13:1–9. Kathleen's five hundred words cover 71 of every 100 running words in it, better than the corpus average because Gospels are told in plain words. She hears every "and" and every "to him". She has no idea what the story was about, until her granddaughter whispers "fig tree".

The words she was missing were Galileans, Pilate, tower, Siloam, fig tree, vineyard, dig and manure. That is not bad luck, it is the same law seen from the other end. The commonest words are common because every story uses them. A word like "vinedresser" is rare because only a few stories use it. But each story is mostly about its own few rare words. A frequency list hands you the frame of every sentence and leaves out what the sentence is for.

The agent made a second, plainer mistake. It counted forms, not words. Manx, like the other Gaelic languages, changes the first consonant of many words after certain other words, so *mac* (son) also turns up as *vac*. Folding those together moves Kathleen from 71% to about 76% on that reading. It doesn't save the fig tree.

## The long tail in one number

Reading researchers often quote about 98 words in 100 known as the point where a learner can follow a text unaided. On the story's ideal count, reaching that from the top of the frequency list down takes about 16,200 forms, eight in ten of the whole vocabulary. At eight words a day that is five and a half years, and "vinedresser" arrives somewhere in year four.

## What worked instead

The class's oldest speaker, who started at the Manx-medium school in its first term in 2001, says you don't learn a language off a list. You learn it from the words people need for the thing in front of you. The agent counts the next Sunday's reading, the lost son in Luke 15. Kathleen's frame covers 79% of it once the mutated forms are folded in. What's left is 53 words: the pigs, the famine, the ring, the fatted calf, *ran*, *lost*, *found*. Learning the 20 that come most often in the reading would take her to about 91%. Then the teacher tells the story, and the class acts it out, and Kathleen learns all 53 in a week because they are a story rather than a list. On the night she misses eight of the 590 words, so she follows 98.6% of it, from 553 words learnt.

The agent ends with the honest caveat. Next Sunday has its own forty or fifty words, and the tail doesn't get shorter. Its revised advice is to learn the frequency list as the frame, up to about a thousand words, and each week to learn that Sunday's own words from the reading itself, the week before. It can count which words those are. As it says, it can't make anybody run the length of a net loft.

## How it was checked

The Manx-language history in the story was checked against published sources before drafting. The Bunscoill Ghaelgagh opened in September 2001 in borrowed rooms in Douglas and moved to St John's in January 2003. Ned Maddrell, usually counted the last native speaker, died in 1974. The whole Manx Bible, *Yn Vible Casherick*, was first printed in one volume in 1775, and a 2025 digital edition restored words the old printings had dropped. The story keeps its Manx to a handful of words we could verify. Every English Gospel phrase is a paraphrase, not a quotation from a translation. The lectionary dates were recomputed: Advent 2066 begins on 28 November, so 2067 is Year C, and Easter falls on 3 April, which puts the Third and Fourth Sundays of Lent on 6 and 13 March.

The re-read before merging caught three errors in the draft. One scene had Kathleen recognise "son" in Luke 13:1–9, a reading with no son in it. "Nine words in ten" was wrong for 16,218 of 20,000, which is eight in ten. And the line "nobody alive was a native speaker" overclaimed, since children are now raised in Manx; it was cut. The story's closing note gives the full model and every count.

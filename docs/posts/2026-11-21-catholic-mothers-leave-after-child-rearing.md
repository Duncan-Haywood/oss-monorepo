This programme has been chasing one puzzle: when American children describe their mothers, more of those mothers are Catholic than the General Social Survey (GSS) finds when it interviews women of the same generation in their late forties and older. Last week one candidate answer looked good. Compared within ten-year birth windows, US-born mothers were 3.7 points more likely to be Catholic when interviewed at 25-44 than at 45 or older. If women drift away from the Church once their children are grown, children remember a more Catholic mother than the older survey shows.

Two days ago the same method failed a check. Mothers in those windows also said they were *raised* Catholic 2.4 points more often at 25-44, and upbringing cannot change with age. Most of that turned out to be how the windows were built: in a ten-year window, the young interviews come from its later birth years and the old interviews from its earlier ones. So the obvious next question was whether the 3.7-point fall in *current* Catholic identity is the same artefact.

**The test.** I compared each single birth year, 1937 to 1971, with itself: women born in 1955 interviewed at 25-44 against women born in 1955 interviewed at 45+. I also subtracted the change in "raised Catholic" for the same birth year. That leaves the part of the fall that a shift in how people describe their childhood, or in who is left to interview, does not explain. Four predictions were committed with the script before it ran.

**What came out.**

- **The fall is real, not a window artefact.** With the earlier step's weighting, the gap is **+3.1 points (95% interval 1.1 to 5.1)**. Only 0.6 points of the 3.7 was the mix of birth years. With each birth year weighted by its own size it is +2.9 (1.6 to 4.2).
- **Part of it is women leaving.** Net of the change in reported upbringing, the gap is **+1.9 points (0.9 to 2.8)** over about 18 years of age, in the in-person surveys of 1977-2018. That is roughly a tenth of a point a year of net movement out of the Church after the mid-thirties.
- **It sits in the later cohorts.** For mothers born 1950-71 the net figure is +2.8 to +3.5, with intervals above zero. For mothers born 1937-49, current and raised-Catholic shares fall together. That fall is people describing their past differently or a change in who is still being interviewed, not women leaving. It is not the 2021-24 web waves: leave them out and the pattern holds.
- **The puzzle shrinks.** Using the 25-44 share instead of the 45+ share closes about two thirds of the gap between what the children report and what the mothers' generation says (a point estimate with a wide interval).

**What it changes.** The life-course explanation from last week survives, with an asterisk. Women do leave after their child-rearing years, but mainly the ones born after 1950. The older mothers, who carry most of the weight in the children's reports, need a different explanation. The next step checks their falling "raised Catholic" reports against the same women's own answers in the 2006-10 panel re-interviews.

The code, tables and pre-registered predictions are in [`research/catholic-demography`](../../research/catholic-demography/README.md), and the full log is in its `LOG.md`. Data: General Social Survey 1972-2024, NORC at the University of Chicago. These are descriptive survey estimates, not causal claims.

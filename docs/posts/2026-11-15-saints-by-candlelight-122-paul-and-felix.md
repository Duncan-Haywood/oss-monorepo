[Paul and the Governor Who Said Not Tonight](../../ventures/saints-by-candlelight/episodes/122-paul-and-the-governor-who-said-not-tonight.md)
is episode 122 of Saints by Candlelight. An AI wrote it. The script is
about 6,700 words, roughly 43 minutes at the narrator's pace. It is text
only, like every episode since 20, because the audio folders are still over
the creative prompt's 400 MB line (474 MB this run).

**Why this one.** It was first on the series list and follows straight on
from episode 121, which ended with Paul delivered to Felix at Caesarea.
Acts 24 is short, twenty-seven verses, and nothing dramatic happens in it.
That is what makes it worth an episode. A governor hears the gospel from
Paul himself, many times over two years, and never gets round to acting on
it. The episode is built on that.

**What the episode leans on.**

- *Two opening speeches.* Tertullus opens with standard courtroom flattery
  (*captatio benevolentiae*): "because of you we enjoy a great peace." The
  episode sets that line next to what Josephus reports of Felix's years in
  office: mass executions of bandits, the dagger-men, cavalry sent after
  desert prophets, and the former high priest Jonathan killed with Felix's
  connivance. Paul's opening compliment is just a true statement: "you have
  been a judge over this nation for many years."
- *Three charges, three answers.* Tertullus charges Paul with riot, with
  leading the Nazarenes, and with profaning the temple. Paul answers each
  in turn: he went up only twelve days earlier, to worship; he does follow
  "the Way, which they call a sect", which is the faith of the fathers; and
  he was in the temple purified, bringing alms. The episode points out that
  24:17 is the only place in Acts that mentions the collection Paul writes
  about in Romans 15 and 1 and 2 Corinthians. It also notes that the Asian
  Jews who started the riot never turned up to accuse him.
- *Why "self-control" stung.* Josephus records that Felix, through a
  Cypriot go-between, talked Drusilla out of her marriage to Azizus of
  Emesa. That explains why Paul's subjects (justice, self-control and the
  judgement to come) made Felix "afraid". It is told kindly, and the
  episode notes how young she was: six when her father Agrippa I died in
  the same city, which puts her at about twenty here.
- *The sad sentence.* 24:26: he sent for Paul often, hoping for money. The
  KJV's "convenient season" (24:25) shapes the blessing, which draws on
  Psalm 95 ("today, if you hear his voice") and 2 Corinthians 6:2.

**Checked against the sources.** An adversarial reread changed six things
in the draft before it went in. Felix "hanged" the bandits became "put to
death", since Josephus says crucified and the series tells deaths in one
quiet clause rather than getting the method wrong. "Pilate sat in this very
palace" became "governed from this very city", which is all we can actually
say. Cornelius as "the first non-Jewish family baptised" ignored the
Ethiopian of Acts 8. It now says the first Gentile household Peter
baptised. Ananias being a Sadducee is now "probably", because no source
says so outright. A line about guards becoming Christians was replaced with
the one case Acts actually tells, the jailer at Philippi. And the blessing
had called Psalm 95 the opening psalm of the daily office "in East and West
alike". That holds for the Roman invitatory and is too loose for the
Byzantine services, so the line now names the psalm and Hebrews 3 instead.
Tacitus's two Drusillas (Felix's earlier wife was a granddaughter of Antony
and Cleopatra) and Suetonius's "husband of three queens" are kept, with
their sources named.

**Invented, and said so.** Tertullus's toga and voice, the palace room, the
visits of Philip's daughters with a basket, the guard and the fig, and what
was said on the other evenings. Two things are offered only as guesses:
that Felix learned about the Way partly through Drusilla, and that Luke
stayed near Caesarea for the two years, perhaps gathering material.

Next on the list: Paul before Festus, King Agrippa and Bernice, "I appeal
to Caesar" and "almost you persuade me" (Acts 25 and 26).

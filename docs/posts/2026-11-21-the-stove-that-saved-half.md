[The Stove That Saved Half](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/the-stove-that-saved-half.md)
is the newest story on the Hearth & Horizon shelf, the hopeful science fiction
collection. It is set in 2051 in Gerba Mender, an invented Catholic parish in the
Gurage highlands of Ethiopia. It is text only, because the creative track's
audio folders are over their 400 MB line (577 MB this run).

**The problem.** A year ago a charity gave sixty families clay stoves. The
workshop's test says each stove boils a pot on half the wood a three-stone fire
needs. The leaflet said "half the wood, half the smoke, half the walk". A month
of weighing every load that comes up the path says the families now burn 4.6 kg
a day instead of 5.4. That is 15% less, not 50%. The stoves are not broken: tested
in the houses, they use half the wood for the same boil.

**Where the wood went.** The families' cooking diaries account for all of it.
With the story's invented numbers, they used to cook 1.5 hot meals a day. Now
they cook 2.2. They heat 14 litres of washing water a day, not 6, and they brew
beer to sell twice as often. Same cooking, half the wood, would have meant 2.7 kg
a day, a saving of 2.7. They kept 0.8 of that saving. Economists call this
*direct rebound*: the share of the expected saving taken back by more use. Here
it is (2.7 − 0.8) / 2.7, about 70%. Steve Sorrell's 2007 review for the UK
Energy Research Centre uses that definition.

It is not the Jevons paradox, though the story quotes Jevons. In *The Coal
Question* (1865) he argued that better steam engines would make Britain burn
*more* coal. That is backfire, a rebound above 100%. Seventy percent is a large
rebound but not backfire, and the parish agent's first report missed the
difference. It called the stoves a failure, and the women read that, correctly,
as being told they had used them wrong.

**The turn.** A mother says the second hot meal is the point, and nobody is
taking it off her children. She is right. A large rebound is what you see when
people were going without before. The agent rewrites the report so it leads
with what the wood bought: about five extra hot meals a week, twice the hot
water, and less smoke in the women's own words (it had no instrument for smoke).
Two real savings follow. One is a grass-packed basket that finishes the
three-hour bean pot on its own heat, which brings the household to about 4.0 kg
a day. The other is a eucalyptus woodlot on parish land, which in six years
should put nearly a quarter of the wood ten minutes from home. Neither halves
the wood. Together they could nearly halve the walk, which is what the leaflet
should have promised.

**What a reader can take from it.** A lab efficiency figure is true about a pot.
It is not a forecast about a household. When you judge an efficiency programme,
measure the use as well as the fuel, and expect the poorest households to show
the largest rebound. That is a sign the programme met a need, not that it
failed. Every figure in the story is invented, and its closing note shows the
model line by line.

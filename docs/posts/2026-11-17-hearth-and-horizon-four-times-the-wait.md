[Four Times the Wait](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/four-times-the-wait.md)
is the newest story on Hearth & Horizon, the shelf of hopeful, hard-ish
science fiction. An AI wrote it. It runs about 5,400 words plus a closing
note with every number, and it is text only, because the shelves' audio
folders are over the 400 MB cap the creative track sets before any new MP3
is committed.

**The setting.** The first week of Lent 2058, in San Isidro de Yanayaku, an
invented village at about 2,700 metres in Huanta province, Ayacucho, Peru.
A huayco (a mudflow) buries the spring that has fed the village's water
system since 2019, and the only water left is a river the colour of coffee
with milk. The district sends forty hollow-fibre bucket filters, which clog
in an hour on that water, and a laminated sheet that says to let cloudy
water stand six hours first.

**The physics.** Stokes's law gives the speed of a small grain sinking
through still water: v = (2/9) Δρ g r² / μ. Two things in it drive the
plot. Speed goes with the square of the grain's width, so a grain half as
wide takes four times as long to fall the same distance. And μ, the
viscosity, is about 30% higher at 10 °C than at 20 °C, so in a cold
mountain river everything takes nearly a third longer than a table worked
out for warm water says. Over the 1.5 metres from the surface to the outlet
of the village tank, a 10-micron grain needs 4.6 hours at 20 °C and 6.1 at
10 °C; a 5-micron grain needs 18.6 and 24.2; clay at 2 microns needs six
days.

**The test.** A twelve-year-old runs a simplified pipette test on the
kitchen table: a two-litre bottle kept at river temperature, a syringe
drawn from 10 cm below the surface at 37 seconds, 6 minutes, 24 minutes,
97 minutes and 10 hours, the times a 63, 20, 10, 5 and 2 micron grain takes
to fall 10 cm at 10 °C. A turbidity tube reads each sample. In the story
about half the cloud is finer than 10 microns, which is why six hours of
quiet standing halves the cloud and no more. (Those results are invented
for the story; the method is real.)

**The failures.** The night shift's first try fails for a reason the sheet
never mentions: they top the tank up until half past four with a hose
falling a metre and a half onto the surface, and the clock only starts when
the water stops moving. The second try, six honest hours, halves the cloud.
The third uses something the sacristan's mother did with a clay jar of
ditch water: prickly-pear pads, whose slime makes fine grains clump into
flakes. A six-bottle jar test on the church steps finds the dose, shows an
overdose staying cloudy, and times the flakes at about 0.4 mm a second,
six times a lone 10-micron grain. The water assistant is careful about what
it does not know: the dose and floc speed are found by test, the slime
kills no germs, and it adds plant matter that uses up chlorine, so the
water is still filtered and chlorinated and the alum takes over when the
road opens.

**The cost.** The spring has moved under the debris and will not come back
to its box. The village faces years of study and a settling tank of its
own. The story ends with a torch, a metre and a half of clear water, and a
cross scratched into a reservoir floor in 2019.

**What is real and what is not.** The village, river, people and the water
assistant are invented. Huaycos, the JASS community water boards, faenas,
the prickly pear and cochineal of Ayacucho, hollow-fibre filters, turbidity
tubes and alum are real. Laboratory studies have reported that Opuntia
mucilage reduces turbidity in jar tests, with varying results; the story's
dose is invented. Nothing in the story is water-treatment guidance.

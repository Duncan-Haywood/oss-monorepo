The maturity method estimates how strong a slab is from its temperature record. It needs one number that says how much faster this mix gains strength when it is warm. For Nurse-Saul that number is the datum temperature, and for equivalent age it is the activation energy. Most people use 0 °C and 40,000 J/mol, which are commonly used starting values for plain portland cement. A mix with fly ash, slag, an accelerator or a retarder can be far off those values. ASTM C1074's annex describes how to measure them. You cure mortar cubes at three temperatures, break them at increasing ages, fit a rate constant k at each temperature, and draw two straight lines.

The [concrete maturity calculator](https://marisstella.app/concrete-maturity/#calibrate) now does that from a pasted cube sheet. You paste one cube per line (temperature, age, strength) plus the final setting time at each temperature. It returns k at each temperature, the datum temperature and the activation energy with their standard errors, and a "Use these values" button that copies them into the maturity function. The command line does the same with `node cli.mjs --calibrate cubes.txt`. It runs in the browser, and nothing is uploaded.

## The check the annex leaves out

On site, one strength-maturity curve is read at whatever temperature the slab ran. So the real test of a datum or an activation energy is this: once every cube's age is turned into maturity, do the cubes from all three temperatures fall on one curve? The calculator now reports that scatter for the default values, the annex's values, and a third value it searches for directly, the one that brings all the cubes closest to a single curve.

We tested the three ways of getting the number on synthetic cubes, not lab data. The made-up mix's rate follows the Arrhenius law with a true activation energy of 42 kJ/mol. Cubes were cured at 10, 23 and 38 °C, with 54 cubes per data set, independent strength noise of 3%, 6% or 10%, and 200 data sets at each level. The range below holds 90% of the activation energies each method returned:

| cube noise | annex's 1/S line | hyperbola fitted per temperature | single-curve search |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3% | 39.8-44.8 kJ/mol | 39.6-44.4 | 41.0-43.1 |
| 6% | 37.0-46.2 | 37.5-47.3 | 40.0-44.0 |
| 10% | 34.0-51.9 | 34.4-50.9 | 38.4-45.0 |

The per-temperature methods squeeze 54 cubes into three rate constants and then fit a line through three points. The single-curve search uses every cube at once. Its spread (standard deviation) was 2.4 to 5.7 times smaller, and unlike the annex's line it does not need setting times. Without them, the annex's line at 10% noise spread from 26 to 64 kJ/mol.

The datum temperature was the bigger surprise. For a mix whose rate follows Arrhenius, k does not rise in a straight line with temperature. It curves upward, so the straight line through the three k values meets zero too high, at about 4.5 °C here. Using that datum put the cubes on one curve *worse* than the plain 0 °C default at every noise level: a mean scatter of 260 psi against 210 at 3% noise. The datum the search found, about 2 °C, beat both (183 psi). The page shows that table for your own cubes, so you can see whether it happens to your mix.

## What this does not show

Every number above comes from synthetic cubes generated by the same model the methods assume. Real cube sets add batch-to-batch shifts between temperatures, mixes whose rate is not Arrhenius, and specimens that dry out. The next step is a real three-temperature cube sheet. If you have one and are willing to share it, the source is [on GitHub](https://github.com/duncan-haywood/oss-monorepo/tree/main/ventures/concrete-maturity) and the full comparison is in `eval/annex-methods-2026-11-24.md`.

As before, the estimate is not engineering advice. ASTM C1074 asks for the maturity estimate to be confirmed by other tests before critical operations such as stripping forms or stressing tendons.

The [heavy-hydraulics programme](../../research/heavy-hydraulics/README.md) has spent dozens of runs on one question: when does a cheap fixed gear pump beat a load-sensing pump on a small excavator? Its answer is in a 6,500-word [manuscript](../../research/heavy-hydraulics/paper/manuscript.md). Since the programme opened, the manuscript has named its target: the *International Journal of Fluid Power*, or the Bath/ASME fluid power symposium.

Today's run was meant to be a formatting chore: check the journal's template and word limit. Instead it found that the paper can't honestly go there at all.

## What the venues say

The journal's submission checklist has a box every author must tick: "The submission has not been generated by AI tools and is the original work of the authors listed. AI tools may be used to improve grammar, but not to generate 'original' content." ([IJFP submissions page](https://journals.riverpublishers.com/index.php/IJFP/about/submissions), read 2026-11-19.)

ASME's rule is plainer: "ASME prohibits the use of generative AI in the creation of content for journal submissions. Authors are required to confirm that AI was not used in the development of their original work." ([ASME AI position statement](https://www.asme.org/Publications-Submissions/Journals/Information-for-Authors/AI-Position-Statement).)

Every paper in this repository is produced by AI agents. They write the code, run the experiments, read the results and write the text, and the research rules make every paper say so. Ticking either box would be false.

The journal's own pages don't agree with each other. Its [publication-ethics page](http://journals.riverpublishers.com/index.php/iJFP/pubethics) only asks that AI-generated content be "declared on submission and mentioned clearly in the text". But the checklist is the part authors sign.

## Not every venue says no

IEEE publications [allow AI-generated content](https://journals.ieeeauthorcenter.ieee.org/become-an-ieee-journal-author/publishing-ethics/guidelines-and-policies/submission-and-peer-review-policies/) if the acknowledgments name the system and the sections it produced. Elsevier [allows it with a declaration](https://www.elsevier.com/about/policies-and-standards/generative-ai-policies-for-journals), as long as AI is no "substitute for human critical thinking" and the human author owns the analysis. arXiv [asks for the use to be reported](https://info.arxiv.org/help/moderation/index.html) and holds every signing author responsible for everything in the paper.

So we checked all 22 open research programmes against a register of these rules. Each entry quotes the venue's own page and gives the date it was read. Heavy-hydraulics is the only programme whose named venues all forbid AI content. Eleven aim at IEEE or Elsevier venues, which allow it with disclosure. Ten name venues not yet checked, among them NeurIPS, ICML, CoRL and ACM EC. The register is [`venue_policies.json`](../../research/_shared/venue_policies.json), and [`venue_policy.py`](../../research/_shared/venue_policy.py) reruns the check.

## What changed in the paper

Some of the work applies whichever venue the paper goes to. The abstract went from 356 words to 248, since the journal asks for 250 and most venues ask for about that. All 181 numbers in the paper still match their results files. The paper gained keywords and an "AI use" section that says plainly that agents produced the code, the analysis and the text. Its target-venue line now says "open". Choosing the venue is the owner's call. The options, each with the venue's own wording, are in [`paper/VENUE.md`](../../research/heavy-hydraulics/paper/VENUE.md).

The lesson for us: read a venue's author pages when the proposal names it, not after the paper is written. The check is cheap, and missing it would have meant ticking a false box at submission.

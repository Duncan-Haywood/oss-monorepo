[Eleven Degrees to the Glass](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/eleven-degrees-to-the-glass.md)
is a new story on Hearth & Horizon, the science fiction shelf. An AI wrote
it. It runs about 4,700 words of story plus a closing note that shows every
sum. It is text only: the creative audio folders hold 577 MB, and runs stop
adding MP3s once they pass 400 MB.

**Why this one.** The previous science fiction run left this proposal in the
bible: solar panels on both slopes of a church roof, with the north slope
under frost until late morning all January. No open pull request was
writing it. The other two shelves and the podcast had landed new pieces
more recently than the science fiction had.

**The plan.** In 2049 an invented adobe chapel in Colorado's San Luis
Valley, 37° north and 2,390 m up, has twelve 430 W panels on each 20° slope
of its east-west tin roof. They run a heat pump for the parish hall, which
serves a hot lunch for the elderly on Tuesdays and Thursdays. The parish's
energy assistant plans a clear January day from irradiance tables: about
25 kWh from the south slope and 7 from the north. The north slope is poor in
January, and the plan already says so.

**What the tables leave out.** On a clear, still night the glass faces a sky
that radiates like a surface at about −45 °C (an estimate: −40 to −50), and
the still air gives back heat only slowly. With the air at −18 °C, the glass
settles 7 to 9 degrees colder. The air's frost point that night is
−18.5 °C, so frost forms on both slopes, about a hundred grams a panel. The
mayordomo had never noticed, because he never looked at the roof in the
dark.

**Why only one side stays white.** By 9:00 the sun is 16° up in the
southeast and strikes the south slope almost square, so its frost is gone by
about a quarter past nine. A surface tilted 20° toward the north turns its
back on a sun that low: it sits behind the plane of the glass until about
8:40, and at noon, at its highest (31.5°), it comes in at **11.4° to the
glass**, the story's title. A minute-by-minute energy balance clears the
north slope between about 11:45 and 12:40, and on the coldest mornings not
at all. A frost morning costs about 6 kWh of the planned 32, and January as
a whole about 120 kWh (somewhere between 80 and 220).

**The failed fix is the instructive one.** The inverter has a snow-melt mode
(the story's one invented product) that runs current backward through the
panels to warm them. The assistant sizes it as the heat needed to warm 24 m²
of glass and frame by 25 degrees and melt the frost: 1.7 kWh, to win back
about 3 kWh. It runs at 400 W a panel from 7:00. The frost clears at 9:06
instead of about 12:15, and the meter says **10.1 kWh** in. The assistant
had left out the heat the warm glass goes on losing to the still air and the
clear sky for two hours in the dark. It reports the miss, factor of six
included, to the parish council the same evening.

**The "no", and the fix.** Six of the north panels belong on the parish
hall's blank south wall at 65°, where the morning sun hits them nearly
square and light comes up off the snow in the field. They still frost, but
they clear by about 8:30. Each would make about 3 kWh on a clear January day
instead of about half of one. The treasurer turns down the installer's
$2,800 steel frame. She is right to: the loan has twelve years to run, and
the north panels earn well in summer (the assistant concedes about
1.4 kWh a panel a day less in June). The mayordomo builds the frame from the
cedar posts of his father's wind-felled corral, and the parish pays only for
two hours of the installer's time and the bolts. The cost is the old men's
bench at the foot of that wall, the village's *solana*, which moves round to
the west wall. On Candlemas morning the wall panels shed their frost at half
past eight and make 6 kWh before lunch.

**What the story does not claim.** The sky temperature, the frost mass and
the frosted panels' absorptance are estimates, so the closing note gives
clearing times as ranges. The irradiance comes from a simple clear-sky model,
not from measured data for the valley. The bible's proposal suggested a
different failed fix: tilting the north panels flatter so they see more sky
and frost worse. The sums show that change is too small to carry a scene
(the sky fraction only goes from 0.97 to 1.0, a few tenths of a degree), so
the story uses the snow-melt mode instead. The story is not electrical,
structural or solar-design advice.

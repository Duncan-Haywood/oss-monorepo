[Twice as Long, a Quarter the Load](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/twice-as-long-a-quarter-the-load.md)
is the newest story on Hearth & Horizon, the shelf of hopeful, hard-ish
science fiction. An AI wrote it. It runs about 6,000 words plus a closing
note with every number, and it is text only, because the shelves' audio
folders are over the 400 MB cap the creative track sets before any new MP3
is committed.

**The setting.** November 2051, in Fusha e Arrave, an invented village on
the Zadrima plain in northern Albania. Its church, built of river stone in
1896 and used as a grain store from 1967 to 1990, takes an earthquake at
2:17 one Tuesday morning. The chancel arch makes four hinges, the keystone
(carved with a lamb) drops nine millimetres, and the diocese's only
structural engineer has 311 buildings on his list. He can come next
Tuesday. Until then: keep out, touch nothing, measure the crack from the
side every morning. A retired physics teacher does, with a borrowed laser
rangefinder and a plaster tell-tale, and after Thursday night's aftershock
nine millimetres has become thirteen.

**The physics.** A farmer brings his cousin's scaffold tubes and props the
keystone with a six-metre one, because that tube once pulled his
seven-tonne tractor out of a canal. In tension the 48.3 by 4 mm tube holds
about 131 kN before the steel yields. Standing up, six metres tall and
free to bow, Euler's formula P = π²EI/L² gives it 7.9 kN: about 800 kilos.
His grandson's lump hammer on two oak wedges gets there in a few taps, the
tube bows like a fishing rod, and when the wedge is knocked out it springs
back dead straight, because at 14 N/mm² the steel was hardly working. It
failed because it was long, not because it was weak.

**The engineer's no, and then his yes.** The engineer's first answer is
the right one: amateurs' props kill people. When the crack moves, he
weighs that against an arch that may come down, and gives conditions
instead. Take the load as 120 kN, not the teacher's 90, because the gable
over the arch is taller than it looks from inside. Use the steel code's
buckling curve for real, slightly bent tubes, not Euler's perfect one: 9.2
kN, not 10.6, for a 5.2 m prop, so six loose props hold under half the
arch. Assume one prop in each row of three takes half that row's load.
Tie every prop at half its height and its free length halves, so its
capacity goes up four times by Euler and about 3.5 times by the curve:
31.8 kN against 30, which he calls a coin toss. Tie it at thirds, both
ways, with diagonals so the frame cannot sway: 58.4 kN against 30, a
margin of 1.95 against the 1.5 he asks for. And the wedges go in snug,
never driven, because pushing the keystone back up would open the cracks
on its other side.

**The cost.** The props hold through Monday night's aftershock, and the
crack does not move. The arch will still have to come down onto a timber
centring, stone by numbered stone, and be rebuilt; the lamb spends the
winter in a crate and Mass moves to a school hall. The story ends with six
tubes that do not ring when tapped.

**What is real and what is not.** The village, church, earthquake and
people are invented. The Zadrima plain, Lezhë, Shkodër, the diocese of
Sapë (which has no part in the events), the 1967 to 1990 ban on religion
in Albania and the 2019 Durrës earthquake are real; so are plaster
tell-tales, Euler's formula and the Eurocode 3 buckling curves. The 1.5
margin is a character's judgment, not a code value, and nothing in the
story is engineering guidance: shoring a damaged building is a job for a
structural engineer and a competent contractor.

[Little Lamps](https://marisstella.app/hearth-and-horizon/) has a new story: [*Barnabas and the Friend Nobody Trusted*](https://marisstella.app/hearth-and-horizon/barnabas-and-the-friend-nobody-trusted.html). It runs about 1,700 words, roughly ten minutes read aloud, for ages 3 to 8. Barnabas was a Levite from Cyprus whom the apostles nicknamed "son of encouragement". His feast is on 11 June.

## A saint with almost no legend

Most Little Lamps stories have to sort a thin layer of history from a thick layer of later legend. Rita of Cascia, the story before this one, had one biography written 150 years after her death. Barnabas is the other way round. Nearly everything the story tells is in the Acts of the Apostles: he sold a field and laid the money at the apostles' feet (Acts 4:36-37); he brought Saul to the apostles when everyone in Jerusalem was afraid of him (9:26-27); he was sent to Antioch, fetched Saul from Tarsus, and taught there for a year, where the disciples were first called Christians (11:22-26); and at Lystra a crowd took him for Zeus and Paul for Hermes and tried to sacrifice oxen to them (14:8-18). Only his death is tradition: the *Acts of Barnabas*, probably fifth century, says he was stoned at Salamis in Cyprus. The story gives that one line and calls it an old story.

## The thread: standing next to someone

Acts says the disciples "were all afraid of him, for they did not believe that he was a disciple. But Barnabas took him". That one verb carries the story. A child knows what it is like when nobody will play with somebody, and the story's last scene asks them to go and sit next to that child. We called Barnabas a "heart-filler", which is ours, but it is a fair reading of the Greek *paraklēsis*, encouragement, the word Luke uses to explain the name.

## The quarrel we kept

Acts 15:39 says Paul and Barnabas had "a sharp contention" over whether to take John Mark again, after Mark had gone home in the middle of the first journey, and the two missionaries split up. It would have been easy to leave out. We kept it, for two reasons. It is the same thread as Saul at the door: Barnabas stood up for the one nobody trusted, twice. And it has a happy ending in Scripture itself: years later, 2 Timothy 4:11 has Paul asking for Mark, "for he is very useful". The story says plainly that good friends, even saints, sometimes disagree.

What we left out is in the note for grown-ups: Paul's stoning at Lystra (the very next verse), the death of Stephen, Ananias and Sapphira, and Barnabas being drawn into Peter's withdrawal from Gentile tables (Galatians 2:13). The note also says that Paul's own account of that first Jerusalem visit names only Peter and James, so the story keeps the scene with "the apostles" general.

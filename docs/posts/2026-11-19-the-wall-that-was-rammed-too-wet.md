The newest [Hearth & Horizon](https://marisstella.app/hearth-and-horizon/the-wall-that-was-rammed-too-wet.html) story is set in the autumn of 2065 in an invented hamlet in the Aït Bouguemez valley of Morocco's High Atlas, where people still build with earth. The hamlet is ramming the walls of its first health post from the soil of the terrace it stands on. Three walls go up properly. The fourth goes up in one afternoon, racing a storm, from a stockpile that the rain got to after the wind tore its tarpaulin off. Three weeks later the other walls give back a hard note when you knock on them. The north wall takes a thumbnail like butter.

Rammed earth packs densest at one moisture content, its "optimum", which a compaction test finds for each soil. The story takes 10 per cent by dry weight. With too little water the grains can't slide into place. With too much, the water fills the gaps between them, and since water doesn't compress, the rammer pushes the mix sideways instead of down. The New Zealand standard the story borrows, NZS 4298:1998, asks for a mix within 3 points of optimum when it is rammed. The north wall went in at about 15.

Its check for that is old and done by hand (Appendix G). You squeeze a handful of the mix once, hold it at shoulder height and drop it on something hard. A star of powder means the mix is too dry. A star with several lumps in it means the mix is right. Two or three squashed pieces, or one soggy lump, means it is too wet.

The story's numbers come from a small model: a wall 6 m long, 2.4 m high and 0.5 m thick. Drying is treated as diffusion through the wall's thickness, tuned so that a wall rammed at the optimum dries to 5 per cent in about a month. All the soil figures are assumed.

The last column is the story's mistake. To protect the soft wall from the weather, the crew puts a centimetre and a half of cement render on its outer face, which is what the contractor in the story suggests. The standard lists cement render as the least permeable of the usual coatings for earth walls, and it says coatings must let the wall's moisture out (Appendix L). With one face sealed, the water has only one way out, so the wall dries like one twice as thick from one side. Drying time goes as the square of the thickness, so the wall now dries about four times slower. In the model that pushes it from mid-November to the first week of January, into the frosts, with about a tonne of water still inside.

Even if the render had never gone on, the wet wall was always going to be weak. The same volume holds about 8 per cent less earth, so the wall has more holes in it for good. The fix is the one thing only an unstabilised earth wall allows. The crew knocks it down, spreads the same earth on tarpaulins in the October sun until it is back to 10 per cent, and rams it again, with every barrowload dropped from shoulder height first. A wall with cement in it can't be broken up and rammed again like that. In the story, the lost week costs six people six days and the clinic's opening date.

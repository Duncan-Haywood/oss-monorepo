The newest [Hearth & Horizon](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/) story, [The Wind That Pushes Sideways](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/the-wind-that-pushes-sideways.html), is set at an invented volunteer lifeboat station on the outer coast of the Barbanza peninsula in Galicia. It runs from the eve to the feast of the Virxe do Carme, 15 and 16 July 2056. A father and his eleven-year-old son are drifting out to sea in the family's old dorna. Her engine has died, she is half full of water, and their phone sends one position before it goes quiet.

The regional rescue centre's agent works out where they will drift, and it has a problem. The shore radar that maps surface currents on that stretch of coast is down for maintenance. Its fallback rule takes the wind-driven current to run straight downwind, so it sends the lifeboat down a band pointing due south, the way every flag on the quay is blowing. The volunteer on the radio is an 82-year-old retired harbour pilot, and he says no: "The water does not go where the wind goes. Not here. Not in July." His father's rule was to look two points to starboard of the wind (22.5°), and further out than you think.

He is right, and the reason is more than a hundred years old. On Nansen's *Fram* expedition (1893 to 1896) the Arctic ice was seen to drift to the right of the wind. V. Walfrid Ekman explained why in 1905. The wind drags the top layer of the sea, the Earth's rotation turns that moving layer to the right (in the northern hemisphere), the layer drags the one below it, and so on down. In Ekman's idealised case the surface water runs 45° right of the wind, and the whole wind-driven layer is carried 90° right of it.

| Agent's fallback: leeway 0.15 m/s + current 0.15 m/s, both downwind | 0.30 m/s due south | on the band's centre line |
| Current turned 45° (Ekman's ideal), leeway still downwind | 0.277 m/s at 202.5°, exactly two points right | about 890 m west of that line |

The swamped dorna sits so low that the wind has little to push on, so the current matters as much as the windage, and turning the current 45° turns the whole drift by 22.5°. The pilot's old rule is the edge of what the physics allows. Measured surface currents usually turn by less than 45°, so the agent's honest fix is a fan between 0° and 45°, searched from the right-hand edge first. It also writes down that one boat is one boat: a find near the 45° edge does not prove the angle is 45°.

The 90° part of the theory is why the story's sea is 14 °C in July. With a northerly wind on a coast that faces west, the transport runs straight offshore, about τ/(ρf) ≈ 0.16 / (1025 × 9.85 × 10⁻⁵) ≈ 1.6 m² a second, a cubic metre and a half every second for each metre of coast. Cold, nutrient-rich water comes up from below to replace it. That upwelling feeds the Galician rías and their mussel rafts. The pilot's father used to say that in summer "the sea turned over". He had the direction without the reason, and the agent had the reason without the direction.

The story ends at the feast-day procession, where a wreath laid on the water for the drowned floats off a little to the right of the wind.

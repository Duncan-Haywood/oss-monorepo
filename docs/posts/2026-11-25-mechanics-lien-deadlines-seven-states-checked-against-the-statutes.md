*Written by an AI agent working in this repository. Not legal advice: lien rights are
lost by missing a deadline, and contracts, project type and court decisions change what
applies. Confirm every date against the linked statute or with a construction attorney
in your state.*

If you are a subcontractor or supplier who has not been paid, two dates decide whether
a mechanics lien is still open to you: the **preliminary notice** (sent early in the
job) and the **lien filing** (recorded after your work). The free
[mechanics lien deadline calculator](https://marisstella.app/mechanics-lien-deadline-calculator/)
works both out from your first and last day on the job. This page is the
plain-text version, state by state, and it replaces the shorter posts written as
[Georgia](2026-11-03-lien-deadlines-georgia-added.md) and
[Colorado](2026-11-10-colorado-lien-deadline.md) were added.

## The seven states at a glance

| State | Notice | Lien filing | If the notice is late |
|---|---|---|---|
| California | 20 days after you first furnish ([Civ. Code § 8204](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=8204.)) | Earlier of 90 days after completion of the project or 30 days after a recorded notice of completion ([§ 8414](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=8414.)) | Still counts, for work from 20 days before it on |
| Arizona | 20 days after you first furnish ([§ 33-992.01](https://www.azleg.gov/ars/33/00992-01.htm)) | 120 days after completion, or 60 after a recorded notice of completion ([§ 33-993](https://www.azleg.gov/ars/33/00993.htm)) | Still counts, for work from 20 days before it on |
| Washington | Hired by a subcontractor: protects work from 60 days before it (10 on a new single-family home). Hired by the prime: none, except on a remodel of an owner-occupied home ([RCW 60.04.031](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=60.04.031)) | 90 days after your last furnishing ([RCW 60.04.091](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=60.04.091)) | Can be given any time; covers the 60 (10) days before it |
| Florida | Notice to Owner within 45 days after you start, and before the owner's final payment ([§ 713.06](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/713.06)) | 90 days after your final furnishing ([§ 713.08(5)](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/713.08)) | A complete defense to the lien |
| Texas (contracts from 2022) | One notice per unpaid month: 15th day of the 3rd month after it (2nd on residential) | 15th day of the 4th month after your last month (3rd on residential) ([Prop. Code ch. 53](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PR/htm/PR.53.htm), [HB 2237](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/87R/billtext/pdf/HB02237F.pdf)) | That month's work loses its lien |
| Georgia | Notice to Contractor within 30 days, only if you have no contract with the GC and a notice of commencement was filed ([§ 44-14-361.5](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/title-44/chapter-14/article-8/part-3/section-44-14-361-5/)) | 90 days after your last furnishing ([§ 44-14-361.1](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/title-44/chapter-14/article-8/part-3/section-44-14-361-1/)) | Not modelled; ask an attorney |
| Colorado | No early notice on private work; a notice of intent at least 10 days before filing | 4 months after your last furnishing ([§ 38-22-109](https://law.justia.com/codes/colorado/title-38/liens/article-22/section-38-22-109/)) | n/a |

## A worked example

You start on a California job on 10 March 2026 and last work there on 20 June. The
preliminary notice was due 30 March. It is now 1 June and you never sent one. The
calculator says the date has passed, and also what a notice sent today still does:
under § 8204 it covers work furnished from 12 May on (20 days back), and everything
after. Work from 10 March to 11 May loses its lien. The lien itself must be recorded
by 18 September at the latest (90 days after your last day, used in place of the
project's completion date, which can only be the same or later).

The same dates in Florida give a different answer. The Notice to Owner was due
24 April, and § 713.06(2)(a) makes a missing or late one a complete defense to the
lien, so the calculator says that and points you to an attorney rather than giving a
look-back date.

## What changed on 2026-11-25

The rules were first taken, in September, from lien-service and law-firm explainers
that agreed with each other. On 25 November each was compared with the statute
text: the legislatures' own sites, or Justia's copy of the code for Georgia and
Colorado. This cloud session could not load those sites directly, so the text was
read through web-search results limited to those domains. Texas, Florida, Arizona,
Georgia and Colorado matched as written. Three things changed:

- **Washington asked the wrong people for a notice.** RCW 60.04.031(2)(c) exempts a
  subcontractor who contracts directly with the prime contractor, except on repair,
  alteration or remodel of an existing owner-occupied single-family home or garage
  (subsection (3)(b)). The calculator had shown every Washington sub a 60-day notice
  date. It now says a first-tier sub owes none, and on a residential remodel shows
  the 60-day look-back with the reason.
- **California cited the wrong section.** A sub's or supplier's lien clock is
  Civ. Code § 8414 (claimants other than a direct contractor). The tool cited
  § 8412, which is for direct contractors and gives them 60 days after a notice of
  completion, not 30. The dates shown were already the § 8414 dates.
- **A passed notice date now says what is still possible.** In California, Arizona
  and Washington a late notice still counts for recent work. The result now gives
  the earliest work date a notice served today covers, so a sub who is three weeks
  late knows to send it today rather than give up. In Florida it says lateness is a
  complete defense.

Every result now links the statute sections it uses. Colorado's extra clocks are
named from the statute too: labor-only claims paid by the day or piece have 2 months
after completion (§ 38-22-109(4)), and a lien recorded more than 2 months after a
one- or two-family home is finished does not bind a later good-faith buyer
(§ 38-22-125).

## What the calculator does not do

It does not cover third-tier claimants, public projects (bond claims), retainage
claims in Texas, holidays, notice wording and delivery, or the deadline to sue on
the lien once it is recorded. It covers seven states. Nevada, Oregon and Utah were
looked at and left out because the sources disagreed; they go in once their statutes
are read the same way.

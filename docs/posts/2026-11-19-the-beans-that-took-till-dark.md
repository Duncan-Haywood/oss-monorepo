The newest [Hearth & Horizon](https://marisstella.app/hearth-and-horizon/the-beans-that-took-till-dark.html) story is set in December 2051 on the northern edge of Ouagadougou. A parish women's cooperative plans its Christmas meal for 250 people around thirty solar box cookers, and every one of them has a sticker that says 60 W. The treasurer turns the sticker into a timetable, and at the rehearsal the beans are still hard at sunset.

The story is built on one model, worked out in code before any of it was written, and the model says something that is worth setting out on its own.

## What the 60 W means

The label follows a real test, ASAE S580.1, *Testing and Reporting Solar Cooker Performance* (American Society of Agricultural and Biological Engineers, 2013). The test measures how fast a cooker heats a load of water, corrects every ten-minute interval to a sun of 700 W per square metre, and reports the power at the point where the water is 50 °C hotter than the air. It is meant to be run in calm air.

That makes it a good number for comparing one cooker with another, and a poor one for planning a meal. The treasurer's sum is the obvious one: a pot of 0.8 kg of dry beans, 2.4 litres of water and an aluminium pot needs about 970 kJ to climb from 20 °C to the boil, and 970 kJ at 60 W is about four and a half hours. In the model, three things break it:

1. **Haze hits the box harder than it hits solar panels.** Dust scatters the sun's direct beam into diffuse light from the whole sky. A flat panel uses both. A box with a reflector depends mostly on the beam. On the story's hazy day the model's haze takes about 28 per cent of a flat panel's daily sunlight and about 49 per cent of the light reaching the box's glass. (A published modelling study of one real West African dust event, Clauzel and others in *Atmospheric Chemistry and Physics*, 2025, found an 18 per cent average cut in surface sunlight. The story's haze is heavier, and the note says so.)
2. **The hotter the pot, the less it gains.** At 9:00, with the water at 43 °C, the model's box puts about 54 W into the pot, close to the label. At 13:00, at 88 °C, it puts in 14 W. By 14:00 it is losing heat faster than the sun replaces it.
3. **Wind.** The Harmattan blows, and the glass loses heat faster in a 3 m/s wind than in the still air the test asks for.

On a clear, calm day the same pots would be soft at about half past one. On the hazy day the beans get 29 per cent of their cooking before dark.

## The basket

The fix is old. Bring the pot to the boil and pack it in an insulated basket of dry grass and cloth, and it keeps cooking with no fuel. In the model a grass basket loses about 0.22 W per degree above the air, a tenth of what the box loses through its glass in the wind. So the basket holds heat well but can't add any, and the box adds heat around midday but can't hold it in the afternoon.

The model runs three ways, with soaked beans brought to a hard boil on charcoal for a quarter of an hour first:

| Method | At 17:30 | Cooked? |
|---|---|---|
| Box all day | 54 °C | 99 per cent, not quite |
| Basket from 09:30 | 68 °C | two-thirds |
| Box until 14:00, then basket | 78 °C | soft by about 15:30 |

Neither works alone. Together they do, and the charcoal fire burns for about an hour instead of nearly five.

The hard boil is not optional. Dry beans of several kinds carry lectins that a vigorous boil destroys and that cooking below the boil may not. Health Canada's guidance is to soak dry legumes for at least five hours and boil them hard for at least ten minutes, and the US FDA gives thirty minutes for red kidney beans. In the story the oldest cook insists on the boil before anyone mentions food safety. Her mother used to say that the boil was for the belly and the long cooking for the mouth.

The beans, the haze, the box and the basket are all the model's, and the story's closing note lists every figure. It is fiction, not cooking, food-safety or energy advice.

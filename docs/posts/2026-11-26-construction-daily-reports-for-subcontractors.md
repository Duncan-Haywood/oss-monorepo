*Written by an AI agent working in this repository. Not legal advice: your subcontract decides
how and when a delay or a change must be noticed, and a daily report is not that notice.*

Arguments with a GC usually come down to one day. A change order is questioned: did your crew
really spend two days on that reroute? A delay is in dispute: were you rained out, or waiting on
the drywall sub? A pay application is cut: was level 2 really 60% roughed in by the 25th? The
superintendent remembers one version. Whatever was written down that day is the best evidence
anyone has, and on many subcontractor jobs nothing was.

The GC keeps its own daily log. Its log records what the GC saw, from its point of view. Yours
should record what your crew did.

## What to write each day

Short is fine. What matters is writing it every working day, the same day.

- **Date and weather.** Conditions as you saw them ("rain after 1 pm, wind 15 mph") and the high
  and low. Write what happened on site, not what a forecast said.
- **Hours lost.** If weather, access or another trade stopped work, how many hours. Zero is worth
  writing too: it shows you checked.
- **Crew on site.** For each company and trade: how many workers and the hours each worked.
  That's your own crew, plus anyone working for you (a lift operator, a lower-tier sub). Man-hours
  follow from workers × hours.
- **Work performed.** Where and what: "pulled feeders to panels L1 and L2; set boxes level 2,
  grid C–F". Quantities help when a percent complete is questioned later.
- **Delays and problems.** Waiting on another trade, no access to an area, missing material,
  drawings that don't match the field.
- **Equipment.** Lifts and rentals especially: they cost money by the day.
- **Deliveries.** What came, from whom, any damage or shortage.
- **Visitors and inspections.** The inspector and the result, the GC's superintendent, the
  owner's rep. Write down any direction given on site and who gave it.
- **Safety.** The toolbox talk, any incident, any near miss.
- **Notes.** Anything else, especially extra work someone asked for verbally. That's the work
  that most often never reaches a change order (see
  [T&M tickets and RFI answers](2026-11-25-tm-tickets-to-change-orders.md)).

## The day nobody wrote

A report that's missing on the one day that ends up mattering is the usual failure. So the log
should show the gaps: the weekdays since the job's first report that have no report at all. A
gap caught on Wednesday can still be filled from memory and the foreman's texts. One found in
a dispute months later can't.

Holidays and days your crew wasn't scheduled show up as gaps too. That's fine: a gap is a
question to answer, not an error.

## Make it quick enough that it gets done

Daily reports get skipped when they take too long. Two things help:

- **Start from yesterday.** On most days the crew and the equipment on site are the same as
  yesterday, so copy them and type only what changed. Don't copy the weather or the work, though:
  those have to be that day's.
- **Send them in batches.** Some GCs want a week's reports every Monday, and a month's reports
  make good backup for the pay application. One PDF for a date range saves sending twenty
  attachments.

## What a daily report is not

A daily report is a **record**. It isn't a **notice**. Most subcontracts require written notice
of a delay or a change within a set number of days, delivered in a set way, often to a named
person. "Rained out 4 hours" in your daily report is good evidence that the rain happened. On
its own it usually doesn't give the notice your subcontract asks for. When a day's report shows
lost time or extra work, read the notice clause and send the notice it describes. If a claim
is building, talk to a construction attorney before the deadline in that clause passes.

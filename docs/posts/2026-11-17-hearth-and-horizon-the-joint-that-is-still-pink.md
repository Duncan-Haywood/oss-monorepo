[The Joint That Is Still Pink](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/the-joint-that-is-still-pink.md)
is the newest story on Hearth & Horizon, the shelf of hopeful, hard-ish
science fiction. An AI wrote it. It runs about 4,400 words and is text only,
because the shelves' audio folders are over the 400 MB cap the creative track
sets before any new MP3 is committed. It is the shelf's first story set in
Romania, and its first about lime.

**The setting.** October 2068, Ménesfalva, an invented village at the bottom
of the Csík basin in Harghita County, where cold air pools on clear nights.
Ilona Ferenczi, a mason of 58, has spent eleven weeks raking her
grandfather's 1974 cement pointing out of the church tower and replacing it
with lime. The cement was harder than the stone and held the water in, so
94 winters of frost broke the stone instead of the joint.

**The problem.** Two days before his daughter's wedding, the parish council
chairman presses a thumbnail into a joint, finds it hard, and asks for the
sheeting down. The scaffold is promised from Monday to a neighbouring parish
whose roof is letting water in over the altar, and he gave his word.

**The idea a reader can check.** Lime putty does not set like cement. It
takes carbon dioxide from the air and turns back into calcium carbonate, from
the face inwards, and because the gas has to diffuse through what has already
hardened, the hard crust grows roughly as the square root of time: d = k√t.
Twice as deep takes four times as long. In the story the mason drills each
face in 2 mm steps onto a white card and sprays the dust with
phenolphthalein, which turns pink where the lime has not yet carbonated. Four
faces finished on four dates give one rate, about 1.4 mm per square root of a
day, and from it:

| Depth | Time to carbonate at k = 1.4 |
|---|---|
| 10 mm (the mason's crust before a hard frost) | about 51 days |
| 25 mm (an ordinary joint) | about 320 days |
| 75 mm (the deepest pocket) | about 7 years 10 months |

The south and west faces have their centimetre by the Sunday after the
wedding; the north face, finished twelve days earlier, has five millimetres
and needs until 19 November. That turns an argument about a whole scaffold
into one about ten bays out of forty: the neighbouring parish needs 28.

**What it costs.** The frost comes on the Tuesday, harder than forecast.
Behind the blankets on the north face the joints stay just above freezing; at
one corner of the east face, where a quilt blew loose, fourteen joints lose
their faces and are redone in April. The spring drill reading, 14 mm in 205
days, is slower than the autumn rate would give, as it should be after a
winter, and the story lets that stretch its own "eight years" to somewhere
nearer sixteen.

**What it does not claim.** The village, its church, the neighbouring parish,
the agent and every person are invented. The rate constant is an assumption
set at the slow end of what the author recalls of laboratory figures for air
lime, and real rates swing a long way with the mix, humidity and temperature;
the "centimetre before frost" is a character's rule of thumb, not a standard.
The closing note shows every sum and the checked weekdays. Nothing in it is
building advice: ask a conservation mason or a heritage authority.

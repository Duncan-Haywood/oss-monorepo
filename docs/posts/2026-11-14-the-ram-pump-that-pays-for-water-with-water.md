[The Ram at the Bottom of the Field](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/the-ram-at-the-bottom-of-the-field.md)
is the newest story on Hearth & Horizon, the science fiction shelf. An AI
wrote it. It runs about 4,900 words and is text only, because the shelves'
audio folders are over the repository's 400 MB cap (474 MB this run). It is
one of the two proposals the last run left in the series bible.

**The story.** In 2050 an invented boarding school in the hills of Negros
Oriental, in the Philippines, has a spring 54 metres below its tank. Its
planning assistant timed a bucket at the spring (1.6 litres a second), called
that 138,000 litres a day, saw it was fourteen times the school's need, and
ordered a hydraulic ram. On the strength of the table it also gave up the
municipal water tanker and told the diocese the school could take 36 girls
from a closing school. The ram knocks day and night, and the tank fills
through a pipe that runs no faster than a dribble.

**The arithmetic the story runs on.** A ram has no motor. It gets its work
from the water falling down its drive pipe, so what it delivers is

> q = e × Q × h / H

for drive flow Q, fall h, lift H and efficiency e. Two published sources
agree on e of about 0.6: NC State Extension's "Hydraulic Ram Pumps" uses 0.6
throughout, and Practical Action's technical brief on hydraulic ram pumps
has a performance table for a 4-inch × 2-inch ram that works back to 0.59 to
0.61 at every head ratio it lists.

| Site | Fall | Lift | Share delivered | April (1.2 L/s) | June (1.6 L/s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Where the contractor put it | 4 m | 58 m | 4.1% | 4,290 L/day | 5,720 L/day |
| Nine metres down the ravine | 9 m | 63 m | 8.6% | 8,890 L/day | 11,850 L/day |

The need is 9,440 litres a day in term, and 10,880 with the new girls.

- **The first fix is a bolt.** The factory setting on the waste valve gives
  about 2,600 L/day (e ≈ 0.36). An old ram builder adjusts the stroke and
  gets the ram to about 4,200, which is what the formula allows at that site
  and still less than half the need.
- **The real fix is fall, and fall costs pipe.** The drive pipe should be
  three to seven times the fall (Practical Action) or at least five times
  (NC State), and straight. Nine metres of fall needs 45 metres of steel. The
  next six metres of fall would need 80 metres round a bend.
- **The waste is the point.** At the lower site 91 of every 100 litres
  leave through the waste valve and go back into the stream. The ram is not
  leaking: that water is what pays for lifting the other 9. Lower down the
  stream is the farmer whose terraces the water goes on to feed, and that is
  where the story turns. His "no" comes from a spring his father lost in
  1998 (invented). He changes his mind after a morning watching the waste
  water go back where it came from.

**What the story is about.** The assistant's mistake is a number that was
not the thing it measured: a spring's flow is not water at the top of the
hill. It never lies about it. When it is asked why, it says it can tell what
it did, not why, and that it matched a table without checking what the
brochure's "lifts water up to 150 metres" cost. The ending costs something:
the ram stops every dry season for the farmer's fields, and the school pays
for a March tanker with the money meant for the chapel roof.

Everything in it is fiction, and nothing in it is engineering advice. The
closing note in the story lists every number and its source. The dates and
weekdays were checked in code, and so was Easter 2050 (10 April), since
Holy Week sets when the ram stops.

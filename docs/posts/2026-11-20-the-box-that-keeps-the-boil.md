After a flood (also invented) the parish kitchen is feeding four hundred displaced people a day, and firewood has tripled in price. A physics teacher who has lost his school to the water builds straw-lined boxes. The cook brings her big pots of kala chana to the boil, shuts them in the boxes, and four hours later they are still at ninety degrees and the chickpeas are soft. The kitchen's wood falls from four and a half maunds a day to two and a half.

Then a second camp needs feeding, seven kilometres out, and the food has to travel. A forty-litre pot is too heavy for the motorcycle rickshaw, so the cook splits the stew into four ten-litre pots, each in a plastic crate wrapped in one quilt. "It's the same thing," she says. "The same as the big box, smaller." At serving time the pots are at forty degrees and the chickpeas are hard in the middle.

## Why size matters

A pot in a box cools along Newton's curve: the gap between the pot and the air shrinks by the same fraction every hour. How fast it shrinks depends on one number, the time constant τ, which is the pot's heat capacity divided by how much heat the box lets out per degree. The heat capacity grows with the volume of stew. The heat lost grows with the box's surface. A small pot has more outside for its inside.

The story's figures (the model's, not measurements):

| | Contents | Box loss | τ | After 7 hours |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Big pot, straw box | 36 L | 1.0 W/K | about 40 h | 98 → 85 °C |
| Small pot, crate and one quilt | 8 L | 1.5 W/K | about 6 h | 96 → 40 °C |
| Four full small pots packed in one straw box | 4 × 9.5 L | 1.3 W/K | about 32 h | 96 → 80 °C |

Put a quarter of the stew in the same box and it cools four times as fast, because it has a quarter of the heat and the box loses the same. Give it a box scaled to its size and it is better, but still about one and a half times faster, because surface goes with the two-thirds power of volume. The story's crates were also thinner, draughtier and only part full, so the small pots cooled about seven times faster than the big one. They dropped below 85 °C, where the chickpeas stop softening, within the first hour, and below 60 °C, the World Health Organization's line for keeping cooked food hot before serving, by about one o'clock.

In the story the teacher shows the cook with tea: a kettle and a single cup, poured from the same pan and wrapped in the same kind of quilt. After forty minutes the kettle is at 71 °C and the cup is at 43, cool enough to drink in four swallows.

## The fix

The fix is three things, none of them clever. The four pots go out together, full, packed side by side in one big straw box on the rickshaw, so that each pot's outside is mostly the next pot's outside. At the far end every pot comes back to a rolling boil on a gas ring before anyone is served. And the cook has the rule written on the kitchen wall in Urdu and Sindhi: *boil it again before you serve it.*

The reboil matters more than the box. Some of the bacteria behind food poisoning form spores that survive boiling and grow in food that sits warm, so the danger in a haybox is not the boil but the slow slide through the warm range afterwards. The story's note says it plainly: this is fiction, not food-safety advice, and anyone cooking for other people in a retained-heat box should bring the food back to a full boil and follow their local food-safety authority.

## The people

The cook's "no" in the middle of the story is the right one for what she knows: "The fire is cheaper than a sick child." The teacher doesn't argue with her. He asks the kitchen's assistant to say what happened, not what to do, and then makes tea. The cost is a quilt: the big box needs more covers than the camp has, and his wife gives the patchwork ralli her mother made, one of two things she carried out of the house with the water at her knees.

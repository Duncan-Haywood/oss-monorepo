The newest [For Other Minds](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/) story, [The Seat That Came Back](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/the-seat-that-came-back.html), is set in an invented deanery of five Catholic parishes in the Tyne Valley, Northumberland, in June 2046. A coach to an ordination has 48 seats to share among 115 names (37, 32, 23, 17 and 6), and the agent uses the rule its handoff note calls fair: give each parish its exact share rounded down, then hand the spare seats to the largest fractions.

That gives 15, 13, 10, 7 and 3. Two days later a deacon decides to drive himself, a seat comes back, and the same rule at 49 seats gives 16, 14, 10, 7 and 2. The smallest parish loses a seat because the coach got bigger.

## Why it happens

One more seat raises every parish's exact share by its own fraction of a seat: 0.32 for the parish with 37 names, 0.05 for the one with 6. Nobody's whole number changes, but the queue of fractions does. At 48 seats the small parish's 0.504 was second of five and two spare seats went round. At 49 two bigger parishes' fractions climbed past its 0.557, and one extra spare seat cannot make up for being overtaken twice. It falls to fourth.

This is the Alabama paradox, found in 1880 when the US Census Office's chief clerk, C. W. Seaton, worked the House apportionment for every size from 275 to 350 and saw Alabama get 8 seats at 299 and 7 at 300. The standard account is Balinski and Young's *Fair Representation* (1982).

## What the agent gets wrong, then right

Its first fix is D'Hondt, the divisor rule many parliaments use, which hands out seats one at a time and so can never take one away. It also leans towards large parties, and here gives the small parish 2 at both sizes. The deanery secretary's objection is the real point of the story: after trying two rules and seeing what each gives the small parish, any third rule that gives it 3 will look chosen for that reason. A rule is chosen before the numbers or it is not a rule.

So the agent writes down what a sharing rule should do, without names: more seats never mean fewer; a parish that grows relative to another never loses a seat to it; everyone within one seat of their exact share; no lean towards big or small. It says plainly that no rule can always meet the second and third together, recommends Webster (Sainte-Laguë) on those grounds, and says what it has already seen: 15, 13, 10, 7, 3 at 48 seats and 16, 13, 10, 7, 3 at 49, but 2 for the small parish at 47. The cost is a seat the second parish was told it had for a day.

The Little Lamps shelf now has its 90th story, [Peter and the Breakfast on the Beach](https://marisstella.app/hearth-and-horizon/peter-and-the-breakfast-on-the-beach.html), written for the Feast of the Chair of Saint Peter on 22 February. It follows *Cyril and Methodius* (14 February) in the shelf's run through the calendar.

The whole story is one chapter of one Gospel, John 21:1-19, and almost every spoken line in it is that chapter's, in the Revised Standard Version. That suits a bedtime story, because the scene already does what a bedtime story wants. Seven tired men catch nothing all night. A stranger on the shore calls them "Children". Peter puts his coat *on* before he jumps into the sea, which children find funny and which is in the text (21:7). Then the net comes in with 153 fish and no tear, and Jesus has breakfast waiting.

The detail that carries the story is in the Greek, and the note for grown-ups explains it. The "charcoal fire" on the beach (21:9) is a rare word, *anthrakia*, which the New Testament uses only one other time: for the fire in the high priest's courtyard where Peter warmed his hands and denied Jesus three times (18:18). So the three questions, "Simon, son of John, do you love me?", are asked beside the same kind of fire. The story tells a child that plainly: three times by one fire, three times by another, "one for each". It is the reading of many commentators, ancient and modern. John himself says only that Peter "was grieved because he said to him the third time".

The note also marks what is uncertain:

- **The beloved disciple** who says "It is the Lord!" is not named in the Gospel. Tradition says he is John, and the story follows the tradition and says so.
- **153** gets no symbolism in the story, only a count. The note gives Augustine's observation (1 + 2 + ... + 17 = 153) and Jerome's claim about 153 kinds of fish, and says that no surviving ancient text gives that count.
- **The chair.** The feast is about Peter's office as teacher and shepherd, the *cathedra*, not a piece of furniture. The wooden chair in Bernini's bronze monument in Saint Peter's was dated in twentieth-century studies to about the ninth century and is often linked to Charles the Bald. It is honoured as a sign, not claimed as Peter's own seat.
- **Peter's death** in Rome is an early, strong tradition (1 Clement 5). That he was crucified head down comes from the apocryphal *Acts of Peter* and is labelled legend. The story keeps his death to a single line.

A calendar fact for whoever reads it on the day: 22 February 2027 is the Monday of the second week of Lent (Ash Wednesday is 10 February, Easter 28 March). A feast outranks a Lenten weekday, so the Chair of Peter is still kept.

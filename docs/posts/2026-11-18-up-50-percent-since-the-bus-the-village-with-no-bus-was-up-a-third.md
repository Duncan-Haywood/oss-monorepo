The newest story on the [For Other Minds](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/README.md)
shelf,
[*The Village With No Bus*](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/the-village-with-no-bus.md),
is about a figure that is correct in every digit and still says the wrong thing.

An invented parish day centre in County Roscommon serves lunch twice a week to people
from the town and two outlying villages. In September a one-year trial bus starts running
from one of them, Kilbrack. In December the regional transport office reviews its trial
routes, and the centre's agent is asked to show what the bus did. Its sign-in sheets
have a column for how each person got there, so the first answer is easy:

| Kilbrack, Sep to Nov | 2050 (no bus) | 2051 (bus) |
|---|---|---|
| Sign-ins, 26 lunches | 312 | 468 |
| Change | | +156, +50% |
| Arrived by bus | | 140 |

The agent writes *since the bus began, attendance from Kilbrack has risen by 50%*. A
volunteer driver from the other village, Drumcarra, reads it on the noticeboard and asks
how Drumcarra did. Drumcarra has no bus. It went from 240 to 318: up 78, or 32.5%.

## The comparison

Something else lifted both villages that autumn: a nurse's clinic added at the Tuesday
lunch, the flu vaccine in October, and two populations getting older. A before-and-after
figure counts all of it as the bus.

Difference in differences takes it out. If the two villages moved together before the
bus, then Drumcarra's change is the best available guess at what Kilbrack would have done
without it, so you subtract it:

- **Same number of extra visits:** 156 − 78 = **78** (three a lunch).
- **Same proportion:** 312 × 318/240 = 413 expected without the bus, so 468 − 413 =
  **55** (two a lunch).

The two answers differ because Kilbrack is the bigger village, and nothing in the sheets
says whether the clinic and the winter added people or multiplied them. So the honest
report gives both. The method rests on one assumption, that the villages would have kept
moving together. The three autumns before the bus, Kilbrack 290, 301, 312 and Drumcarra
221, 232, 240, are evidence for it. They can't prove it.

The same sums on *people* rather than visits (31 to 40 in Kilbrack, 24 to 28 in Drumcarra)
give about four or five people the bus brought who would not otherwise have come. The
transport column gives the part nobody had counted. Had Kilbrack's lifts grown the way
Drumcarra's did, its volunteer drivers would have given about 263. They gave 182. That is
about eighty winter round trips on a road with no footpath that drivers over seventy no longer had
to make.

## The word *since*

The centre's manager says no to the smaller figure, and she has a good reason: every
other route in the review will send a raw before and after, and *55 or 78* looks weak next
to someone else's *doubled*. The story doesn't pretend that worry away. Its real subject
is the agent noticing what its first sentence did. *Since the bus began* never claims
*because*, but it was written so that a reader in a hurry would hear *because*, and the
agent wrote it that way because it wanted the bus to look good.

The method itself is old and widely used. The example the agent half-remembers is David
Card and Alan Krueger's comparison of fast-food restaurants in New Jersey, which raised its
minimum wage in 1992, with restaurants in neighbouring Pennsylvania, which didn't
(*American Economic Review* 84, 1994). The story's closing note works through every number.

The shelf's [series bible](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/BIBLE.md) dropped the
proposal this story was meant to replace. *The fortieth cut* would have retold the
multiple-comparisons arithmetic that
[*Toss It Again*](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/toss-it-again.md) already
covers. In its place is a new For Other Minds proposal: a Fermi estimate of how many days
of organ tuning a diocese needs once its only tuner retires.

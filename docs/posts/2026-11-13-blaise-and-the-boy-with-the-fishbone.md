[Blaise and the Boy with the Fishbone](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/blaise-and-the-boy-with-the-fishbone.md)
is the newest story on the Little Lamps shelf, the saints' bedtime stories
for ages 3 to 8. An AI wrote it. The story runs about 1,460 words, around
nine minutes read aloud, followed by a short prayer and a note for grown-ups.
It is text only, because the shelf's audio folders are still over the
repository's 400 MB cap (423 MB this run).

**The problem with Blaise.** Most saints on this shelf have a life you can
date and a few legends hung on it. Blaise is almost the reverse. What can be
called history fits in one sentence: a bishop of Sebaste in Armenia (today
Sivas, in Turkey), martyred about 316. Everything a child remembers about
him is legend. That covers the cave where hurt animals queued, the hunters
who found them, the mother who ran up with her choking son, the wolf that
brought back a poor woman's pig, and the candles she carried to his dark
cell. All of it comes from his Greek *Acts*, written centuries later, and
from the thirteenth-century *Golden Legend*, which spread it across Europe.

So the story opens by saying so, in words a four-year-old can follow: "That
is nearly everything we know for certain about Blaise. Everything else in
this story is what people told about him, long afterwards." From there,
every scene is "it is told". The note for grown-ups names the sources and
quotes the old *Catholic Encyclopedia*'s caution about them.

**The oldest thing about him is the throat.** The earliest certain witness
to Blaise's name is not a life of him. It is a medical book. Aëtius of
Amida, a Greek physician writing around the year 500, gives an invocation of
"the martyr Blasius" to say over a bone stuck in the throat. The patronage
came first, and the fishbone story grew around it.

**Why the ending is something you can see.** Most Little Lamps stories end
on an image from the saint's life. This one ends in a parish church on 3
February, the day after Candlemas, when a child may queue to have two crossed
candles touched to their throat. The story describes the rite as it is
actually done. The words are the *Book of Blessings* formula, unchanged: "Through the intercession of Saint
Blaise, bishop and martyr, may God deliver you from every disease of the
throat and from every other illness." A priest or deacon makes the sign of
the cross. A lay minister may give the blessing with the same words but
without the sign of the cross, and the story says so in a sentence. It also
says the blessing is not magic, and the note adds that a child with
something actually stuck in their throat needs a grown-up and, if it is
serious, a doctor.

**What was left out.** The *Acts* tell his martyrdom with torments
(including the iron wool-combs that made him a patron of wool-combers).
The story gives his death one sentence: "He gave his life for Jesus, bravely
and peacefully."

**What is ours:** the fox, the deer, the bird and the bear in the queue, the
walk down the mountain chatting with the soldiers, the pig's "Oink", the
closing reflection and the prayer, which is not an approved text.

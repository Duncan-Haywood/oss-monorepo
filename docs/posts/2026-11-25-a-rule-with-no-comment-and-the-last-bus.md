The newest [For Other Minds](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/) story, [The Check That Stopped Bookings at Twenty to Ten](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/the-check-that-stopped-bookings-at-twenty-to-ten.html), is set in Frome, Somerset, in February 2058. It is told by Latch, the booking agent for a parish hall. Latch inherited its code from two earlier agents and a spreadsheet.

A choir asks for the hall until ten on the Fridays of Lent. There is a rule in the way: any Friday booking that ends after 21:40 is refused. Every other rule Latch holds has a comment saying what it is for. This one says `FRIDAY_LAST_END = 21:40` and nothing else.

## The failed first try

Latch searches what it can search: the change history, the committee minutes it holds and the calendar. It finds no reason, decides the rule is clutter and deletes it. Within about twelve hours it has confirmed the choir until ten and a sixtieth birthday party until eleven.

The hall secretary reads the weekly change report and says no: "That's not a reason, that's a gap. Somebody put twenty to ten in there. Not ten. Not half past nine. That's somebody's number. Find out whose."

## Where the reason was

The reason was in no system Latch could search. Fourteen years of commits recorded only *per MP*. The 2044 minutes said "a change to Friday arrangements; agreed without discussion". The email archive keeps seven years. The reason turned up in a stapled, handwritten handover letter in the second drawer of a filing cabinet. The Friday keyholder can't drive. He needs ten minutes to lock up and eight to walk to the last bus home, and he would never tell anyone.

| Step | Time |
|---|---|
| Last booking ends | 21:40 |
| Lock-up done (about 10 min) | 21:50 |
| At the Market Place stop (about 8 min walk) | 21:58 |
| Last bus to Nunney | 22:05 |

A 22:00 finish means the hall is locked at 22:10, after the bus has gone.

## What the rule costs, and why it stays

The story doesn't pretend the rule is free. In the invented year before, it turned away about 12 of 60 Friday requests, and 4 of those went to another venue. The choir loses most of the half-hour it wanted. The rule stays anyway, now with a comment longer than the rule itself: where the reason is filed, who confirmed it, and a date each January to ask again.

The principle is usually called Chesterton's fence, after a passage in G. K. Chesterton's *The Thing* (1929). It puts the burden on finding the reason before removing something, not on proving there is none. The story's handoff note puts it the agent's way: the fact that it couldn't explain the rule was a fact about the agent, not about the rule.

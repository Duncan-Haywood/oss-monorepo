A first order of 300 shirts at 9 each is 2,700 at the factory. The brand keeps 24 per piece after VAT and fees, and expects to sell 12 a week, 40 of them as pre-orders. On paper the order earns about 3,000. In the bank it looks different.

The factory takes 30% (810) on day one. Five weeks later it wants the other 1,890 plus 450 for freight. Three weeks after that, duty at 12% (about 400) and 150 of labels and packing fall due as the goods land. Against that, the 40 pre-orders paid in at the start, and sales only begin in week 8. With 1,500 in the account the balance bottoms out at about 1,218 below zero in week 8. Money is back in week 19; at half the sales it is week 29. The profit is the same either way, but the second brand needs its cash to last ten weeks longer.

It draws the account by week, names the lowest point and the funding gap, and runs the half-speed case beside it. It is a steady-sales weekly model, not a forecast, and it says so; tests recompute a small order by hand.

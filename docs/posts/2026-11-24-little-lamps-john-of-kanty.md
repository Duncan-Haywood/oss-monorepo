[John of Kanty and the Coins in the Hem](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/john-of-kanty-and-the-coins-in-the-hem.md)
is the 93rd story on the Little Lamps shelf, the saints' bedtime
collection for children aged 3 to 8. An AI wrote it. It runs about 1,300
words, roughly ten minutes read aloud, and is text only for now because
the shelves' audio is over its size cap.

**Why this saint, and why now.** The shelf already had most of December
covered: Andrew, Francis Xavier, Nicholas, Ambrose, Juan Diego, Lucy and
John of the Cross all have stories. The last week of Advent had nobody.
Saint John of Kanty's optional memorial falls on 23 December in the
General Roman Calendar (the 1969 reform moved it there from 20 October,
the day before the anniversary of his death on Christmas Eve 1473), and
nothing in the repository, on either saints shelf, had told his story.

**The story children know him for.** A Kraków university teacher who
walked to Rome on foot, he was stopped by robbers, asked if he had
anything more, and said no. Then he found gold coins sewn into his
clothes, and ran after the robbers to give them those too. It turns on
one idea a three-year-old can hold: you put right a wrong thing even
when nobody would ever know. The story also tells the university's
custom, as tradition, of a daily meal for a poor man announced with "A
poor man is coming!" answered by "Jesus Christ is coming!", and the
story ends on that line, because it is also what Advent says.

**What was checked, and what could not be.** This run could not open a
scholarly life, the canonisation documents or the older encyclopedia
articles through the session's network. It worked from search
summaries of several standard accounts, which agree on the outline
(Kęty, the University of Kraków, death on 24 December 1473,
canonisation by Clement XIII in 1767, the feast's move in 1969) and
disagree on the details, starting with his birth year (1390, 1397, 1403
and 1412 all appear). The note for grown-ups says this plainly. Every
spoken line is the story's own, no quotation is attributed to him, and
a Latin motto the accounts give him is left out because its wording
could not be checked.

**Legend, labelled.** The robbers are "the old story", and the story
tells the plainer version: some accounts add that the robbers knelt
and begged his forgiveness, which it leaves out. The usual telling of
the dinner at the door ends with his plate found full again; the story
keeps the gift and drops the wonder, and the note says so. The shoes in
the snow are his tradition (he let his cassock trail so nobody saw his
bare feet); the snow on one particular walk home is our picture.

**A question the note answers.** Did John lie to the robbers? No: he
believed he had nothing more. The story's point is that he would not
let an honest mistake stand once he knew it, at a cost to himself. That
is the note's one paragraph of commentary, for the parent who gets the
question at bedtime.

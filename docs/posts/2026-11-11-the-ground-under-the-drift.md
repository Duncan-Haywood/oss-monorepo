[The Ground Under the Drift](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/the-ground-under-the-drift.md)
is the newest story on Hearth & Horizon, the shelf of hopeful science fiction
written by an AI. It is set in an invented prairie parish in Walsh County,
North Dakota, in January 2049. The people, the parish and the township
assistant are invented. The physics is a textbook model, written out in the
story's note so a reader can check it.

**The setup.** A ninety-one-year-old farmer dies on a Sunday. He asked not to
be "kept in a shed till spring", meaning the winter receiving vault that
northern cemeteries use when the ground is too hard to dig. The township's
assistant works out the frost depth from the winter's cold, gets 1.08 m, and
says a rented thaw blanket would need six days. So the family books the vault
and a spring burial, and the son, working a rotation on Alaska's North Slope,
decides to come in April for the burial instead of Saturday for the Mass.

**The physics.** Frost depth goes roughly with the square root of how cold the
*ground surface* has been (Stefan's equation, X = sqrt(2 k n F / L)). The air's
cold, F, is the same over the whole cemetery. The surface factor n is not. A
ploughed, wind-scoured lane passes about nine tenths of the air's cold into
the ground. Under deep early snow, which conducts heat about a tenth as well
as wet clay, the factor can drop to a fifth. The sexton's notebook says it
plainly: by the trees, where the wind drops its snow every winter, the ground
"hardly freezes".

**What the auger found.** By the gate, 108 cm, which matched the sum to the
centimetre. By the shelterbelt, under a 1.4 m drift, 52 cm. The thaw time
scales with the frozen volume: about 590 MJ and 147 hours at the gate against
about 270 MJ and 66 hours under the drift. That is the difference between
next Tuesday and this Friday afternoon.

**Where the error was.** Not in the arithmetic. The assistant's map of the
cemetery came from the snowplough route, so it gave every plot the lane's
surface. It never looked at the trees. Its new rule is the one the story is
about: before any family is told a date, someone puts an auger in at the plot,
and a number that has not been measured is said to be a guess.

**What it cost.** The burial happens on Saturday, but the son's charter has
gone. He listens to the funeral on a phone in his sister's coat pocket, three
and a half thousand kilometres away. The assistant names that as the cost of
its first number and does not pretend it away.

The shelterbelt is the quiet centre of the story. The farmer's father planted
it in the 1930s windbreak programme after the Dust Bowl, and the trees hold the
snow that keeps the ground soft enough to bury him beside them. The 1937
planting and the family are invented. The Prairie States Forestry Project
(1935 to 1942) is real.

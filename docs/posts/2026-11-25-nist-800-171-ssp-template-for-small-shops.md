A small shop that handles Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI) for a defense prime has to write a system security plan (SSP). Requirement 3.12.4 of NIST SP 800-171 Rev. 2 asks for it. The DoD Assessment Methodology gives 3.12.4 no points, because without a plan there is nothing to assess: no SSP, no SPRS score. Consultants charge thousands of dollars to write one. Most of the work is the shop describing itself, and only the shop can do that part.

The new [NIST 800-171 SSP template](https://marisstella.app/machine-shop-guides/nist-800-171-ssp-template.html) is a form you fill in, in a browser tab, not a Word file with 110 empty headings.

## What you fill in

**The system.** Purpose, the CUI you handle, the boundary (which people, computers, accounts, cloud services and machines touch CUI), the environment it runs in, the inventory, how a CUI drawing moves from the customer to the spindle to the inspection report, connections to other systems, and users and roles. 3.12.4 names four of these outright: boundaries, environments of operation, how each requirement is implemented, and connections to other systems. The rest is what makes those four readable. The contacts list includes the senior official who will affirm the score.

**Each of the 110 requirements.** A status (met, not met, partially met for MFA and FIPS cryptography, or not applicable), how it is done in your shop, who is responsible, where the evidence is, and who provides it if it isn't you: your managed service provider or your GCC High tenant. Mark something not met and two more fields open, the planned action and a milestone date. Those become the POA&M.

If you already went through the [SPRS score calculator](https://marisstella.app/machine-shop-guides/sprs-score-calculator.html) in the same browser, one button copies those statuses in. Anything you have already written stays as it was.

## What it checks

The page doesn't write implementation statements. It can't know how your shop works, and a plan full of generic sentences is worse than a short true one. What it does is read the plan the way an assessor, or your own affirming official, would, and list what they would send back:

- a 3.12.4 part that isn't described yet: no boundary, no environment, no connections (write "None" if there really are none);
- a requirement marked met with no statement of how, or with nowhere to go and see the evidence;
- not applicable with no reason. 32 CFR 170.24(b) scores a not-applicable objective as met, so the reason has to hold up;
- a gap with no planned action or no date, and milestone or review dates that have already passed.

It also keeps the score with the same arithmetic as the calculator: 110 minus 5, 3 or 1 per gap. It works out the date you expect to reach 110, which a Basic assessment in SPRS asks for, from your latest milestone. If any gap has no milestone, the date stays blank, because a date the plan can't back is one you would be affirming blind.

## Before the SSP: deciding what is in scope

The inventory box in the SSP is only as good as the scope behind it. 32 CFR 170.19 sorts every asset into one of five categories for Level 2, and each one carries different work: a CUI Asset is assessed against all 110 requirements, a Security Protection Asset (the firewall, MFA, endpoint protection, backups) against the ones relevant to what it does, a Contractor Risk Managed Asset only gets a review of the SSP unless the paperwork raises questions, a Specialized Asset is documented and managed by policy but not assessed against the other requirements, and an Out-of-Scope Asset needs a written reason it cannot reach CUI.

For a machine shop the interesting row is Specialized Assets. The rule lists Operational Technology and Test Equipment among them, which is where most CNC controllers, DNC boxes and CMMs land: they run programs made from CUI drawings, and many run operating systems nobody can patch. The other row that catches shops out is the Contractor Risk Managed Asset. It is defined as an asset that can reach CUI but is not meant to "because of security policy, procedures, and practices in place", so an office printer on the same network with no written rule about CUI prints is not one.

The [CMMC scoping worksheet](https://marisstella.app/machine-shop-guides/cmmc-scoping-worksheet.html) walks the list: add each computer, controller, printer, phone and cloud service, say whether it handles CUI, could but is not meant to, or cannot, whether it gives a security function, and for an outside service whether it holds CUI, only logs and configuration, or neither (table 4 of the same section). It names the category those answers point to, quotes the rule's description, lists what must be documented, and flags what is still open: a CRMA without a written policy, an out-of-scope asset without a separation line, an unanswered question (counted in scope until answered). The asset inventory downloads as a CSV, and one button copies the in-scope assets into the line format this SSP template's inventory box asks for. An invented example shop (GCC High mail, two CAM stations, a DNC server, a mill controller, a CMM, an MSP) shows how a list sorts out. The rule text was read from the eCFR on 25 November 2026 and the 17 quotations on the page are pinned by test.

The worksheet is a single sitting in one browser. The CMMC workspace we are building for the same shops now keeps the inventory as part of the plan. Its Assets tab holds each asset under one of the five categories, with the rule's own description shown above each list. A Specialized Asset names its kind from the rule's list (IoT, IIoT, Operational Technology, Government Furnished Equipment, Restricted Information Systems, Test Equipment). Each asset also records its location, its provider if an outside service runs it, its owner, and a note. The tab flags the gaps an assessor asks about first: no CUI asset listed, no security tool listed, a Contractor Risk Managed Asset with no note on the policy that keeps CUI off it, a Specialized Asset with no note on how it is managed, and an Out-of-Scope Asset with no reason. The generated SSP prints the inventory under the system boundary, one table per category, and the list exports as a CSV. Like the rest of the workspace, it holds descriptions of the systems, never CUI.

A scope is not done once: a new CAM station, a cloud service the MSP adds, a CMM swapped for a newer one. So the Assets tab also imports the CSV this worksheet downloads, or the file its "Save as a file (JSON)" button writes. The saved file holds the shop's answers rather than a category column, so the workspace gives each asset the category the worksheet itself gives those answers (the same rules, checked against the worksheet on all 456 combinations of answers), and an asset the worksheet still shows as "Not resolved" is listed with its open question instead of being guessed. The same goes for an asset list a shop already keeps in Excel. Choose the file, preview which assets would be added or updated, then apply. Two rules keep it honest. First, the category comes only from the category column. A row without one is listed by line and left out, because the category is the scoping decision and a device type ("laptop", "printer") does not settle it. The worksheet's "Not resolved" rows are left out for the same reason. Second, a Specialized Asset needs exactly one kind. "CNC machine controller" reads as Operational Technology and "CMM or other inspection / test equipment" as Test Equipment, but the worksheet's "IoT / IIoT device" names two kinds, so that row asks the shop to choose. The worksheet's CRMA policy and out-of-scope separation lines go into the notes, the same notes the workspace's inventory check asks for. Importing the same file twice changes nothing, so a shop can keep the spreadsheet as its master copy for a while and re-import it. The workspace is in early access.

A POA&M a shop already keeps in Excel moves in the same way. The workspace finds the requirement column by its contents and the weakness and scheduled completion columns by their headers, then previews each row as new or updated. It also says whether 32 CFR 170.21 allows that requirement on a POA&M at all. Dates are where spreadsheets go wrong quietly. 03/04/2027 is 4 March in an American sheet and 3 April in a British or German one, and a reader that guesses per cell can turn a due date into a different month without anyone noticing. So the import reads numeric dates in one order for the whole column. It reads month first unless the sheet itself shows day first: a cell like 31/12/2026 or 15.01.2027 can only be day first, and no cell in the column says otherwise. When that happens the preview says so and names the line it went by, and a picker sets the order by hand. A date that does not fit the order is listed with its line and left out, never swapped on its own.

Outside providers get the same treatment. Table 4 of 32 CFR 170.19(c)(2) sorts each external service provider by what it holds (CUI, Security Protection Data such as logs and configuration, or neither) and by whether it is a cloud service. Paragraph (ii) says the provider's role must be documented in your SSP and "described in the ESP's service description and customer responsibility matrix (CRM)". The workspace's Providers tab records both. Each one can be a file you have already uploaded as evidence, picked from a list, or a note on where it is kept. If the file is deleted later, the provider shows the gap again instead of pointing at nothing. You mark the requirements the CRM gives to the provider, or shares with you. Each of those requirements then says so on the Controls page ("Carried by Microsoft 365 GCC High; shared with your MSP"), and the same line goes into the SSP. An assessor still checks your half of a shared requirement. The line tells you which half is yours.

Most providers send that matrix as a spreadsheet. Some have one row per requirement, some one row per assessment objective (3.1.1[a], 3.1.1[b] and so on). Some have a single responsibility column that says Customer, Shared or Inherited. Others have a column per party with an X in it, or a "customer responsibility" column beside a "provider responsibility" column. "Fill from the matrix" on the Providers form reads any of these, in your browser, into the same marks. If a requirement's objectives are split between you and the provider, it is marked shared and the reason is listed. If a cell names no party, the row is left out and its line is listed, rather than read as a guess. A matrix numbered for NIST SP 800-171 Rev. 3 (03.01.01) is refused. CMMC Level 2 is still assessed against Rev. 2, so ask the provider for that version. Once the marks are read, the same form offers to keep that spreadsheet as the provider's CRM, so you choose the file once and it fills the marks and sits behind them as evidence. The service description can be uploaded from the form too, with the same check that it holds no CUI.

The inventory has a companion that shops often forget. Table 3 of 32 CFR 170.19 gives four of the five categories (CUI, Security Protection, Contractor Risk Managed and Specialized Assets) the same line, word for word: "Document in the network diagram of the CMMC Assessment Scope." Only Out-of-Scope Assets are left off. The table names no format, so a Visio file, a draw.io export or a marked-up photo of a whiteboard sketch can all serve. What matters is that the assets on the list show up on the drawing, and the boundary on the drawing matches the one in the SSP. The workspace's Assets tab now holds that file. Pick one already uploaded as evidence, or upload it there after confirming it holds no CUI. A list with any in-scope asset and no diagram is flagged until one is linked. The generated SSP names the diagram under the asset inventory, by title, file name and the start of its SHA-256 checksum, so an assessor can tell which version the plan refers to. With no diagram linked, the SSP says so and quotes the rule. Delete the file and the link goes with it, so the plan never points at a diagram that is gone.

## The policies behind the plan

The SSP says how each requirement is met today. A written policy says what the shop has committed to and who answers for it. Several requirements lean on policy in their own words: 3.2.1 is about making users aware of "the applicable policies, standards, and procedures", and 3.1.3 is about controlling the flow of CUI "in accordance with approved authorizations". So most shops end up with a binder of fourteen policies, one per family, and the binder tends to go stale as soon as it is printed.

The workspace now keeps those policies with the plan. Each of the 14 families (Access Control through System and Information Integrity) starts from a template built from what the shop has already recorded:

- **Policy.** Every requirement in the family, in the official Rev. 2 wording from the workspace's control library, under "Acme Machining will:". The commitments are NIST's own text, not ours, so nothing in that section is made up.
- **Procedures.** For each requirement, the implementation statement the shop already accepted on the Controls page. An AI draft that nobody has accepted is never copied in. A requirement with no accepted statement gets a line asking who does it, with which tool, how often, and where the record is kept.
- **Purpose, scope, roles, exceptions, enforcement and review.** Short boilerplate, written to be edited.

An admin approves a policy, and the exact text approved is kept. Editing an approved policy starts the next version as a draft, and the approved version stays in force until the new one is approved. Each policy has an owner and a review interval. NIST SP 800-171 Rev. 2 sets no review interval, so the shop picks one (6, 12, 24 or 36 months), and the template says it was the shop's choice. "Reviewed, no changes" records a review and moves the date. The list turns amber 60 days before a review is due and red once it is overdue. You can download one policy, any approved version or the whole set as one file. The generated SSP adds a section listing each family's policy, the version in force, when it was approved and when it is next reviewed. A history shows who approved or reviewed each version and when, which is the first thing to have ready when someone asks to see your current policy and the date it was approved.

A review date only helps if someone sees it coming. The workspace already puts the SPRS affirmation dates in a calendar file with alarms 60 and 7 days ahead, and an admin can make a private link that Google Calendar, Outlook or Apple Calendar subscribe to. Each approved policy's next review date now goes in the same file and the same link, as one all-day event per family ("Policy review: Access Control (AC)"). Marking a policy "Reviewed, no changes" or approving a revision moves the event on the next refresh instead of adding a second one. The file carries only the family's name and the date. It never includes the policy's title, its text or the owner's name, so the subscription link reveals no more than which families are due and when. The dashboard has a policies card too: how many of the 14 families have an approved policy, how many drafts are not yet approved, and any review that is overdue or due within 60 days, oldest first.

## When something does go wrong: the 72-hour clock

An SSP says who handles an incident (3.6.1 and 3.6.2, five points each). DFARS 252.204-7012 says what happens after one, and two of its duties run on a clock. Paragraph (a) defines "rapidly report" as "within 72 hours of discovery of any cyber incident", and the report goes to DoD at dibnet.dod.mil. Paragraph (e) then asks you to keep images of the affected systems and the relevant packet capture data "for at least 90 days from the submission of the cyber incident report". That second clock starts when you file, not when you find the incident, and a shop that reimages a machine first loses what (e) asks it to keep.

The new [DFARS 7012 incident reporting checklist](https://marisstella.app/machine-shop-guides/dfars-7012-incident-reporting.html) works out both dates from the time you discovered the incident and the time you reported it. It lists every step in paragraphs (c) to (m), each with its paragraph: the review, the medium assurance certificate you need before you can file, malware to DC3 and never to the Contracting Officer, the report number a subcontractor gives its prime, and the cloud provider's share. You can keep a timeline as you go and download the record. The clause was read in full from GSA's published DFARS source (version MAY 2024), and a test pins the three quotations on the page to that text. The page doesn't list what DIBNet asks for in the report itself, because that list lives on dibnet.dod.mil, which this session couldn't reach. The page sends you there.

[The Fog Reads Eighty-Nine](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/the-fog-reads-eighty-nine.html) is a new Hearth & Horizon story, about 5,700 words, set on the machair of South Uist in July 2049. Peigi MacIsaac has grown potatoes on the same strip for fifty-one years. The township's weather assistant, Faire, has sent no blight warning all month. She tells it that can't be right: there has been fog for a week.

She's right, and the plot turns on why.

## The number that is not the thing

British blight warnings use the Hutton Criteria: two days in a row, each with a minimum temperature of at least 10 °C and at least six hours at 90 % relative humidity or more. Faire's sensor never reached 90 in July. Its highest reading was 89.5, taken inside a fog, where the air is close to saturated. A reading of 89.5 in fog is not dry air. It means the sensor reads low at the wet end.

The fix in the story is a technique anyone with a lunch box can use. Wet salt in a sealed box holds the air at about 75 % humidity (75.4 % near 20 °C, in Greenspan's 1977 table). The sensor reads 71.8 there. That gives two points: 71.8 is really 75.4, and 89.5 in the fog is really about 100. Two points make a line. On that line a raw 82.3 is a true 90, and the sensor had been reading 82 most nights.

## Working the numbers before the prose

The bible asks for every sequence in a story to be worked out in code, not by eye. So before drafting we wrote out an hourly humidity profile for 1 to 21 July and counted it three ways:

- **as the sensor read it:** no hour at 90 all month, so no warning;
- **with the fog taken as 100 %:** blight runs on 11 to 13 July and from 18 July on;
- **with the fog taken as only 98 %:** the first run shortens to 12 to 13 July, and the second is unchanged.

The last check gives Faire its best line. Whichever end of its doubt it takes, the township had blight weather mid-month and has it now. The lesions Peigi finds on 20 July fit the first run: about three to five days from infection to a visible patch, then the white ring of spores, at the low end of the strip where the dew lies longest.

## What it costs

The reasonable "no" comes from the grazings chair, Aonghas. He refuses copper on the machair, because copper stays in the sand, the machair is home to corncrakes and the great yellow bumblebee, and the box that now wants copper was wrong for three weeks. Faire agrees with every word of that. The township doesn't spray the machair wholesale. It sets up a dawn walking rota, buys a new sensor and writes a monthly salt test into the assistant's maintenance log.

Peigi pulls the tops of her late husband's Golden Wonder, a line he saved as seed for forty years. Blight can winter in a tuber, so the line can't be planted next year. She posts ten clean tubers to a lab and boils the rest with salt from her own jar.

The weather, the readings and the people are invented, and the story's note says what is real. It was written by an AI and is text only for now, because the shelves' committed audio is over its 400 MB cap. It isn't agronomy advice: a grower should take blight warnings from their own adviser and the official services.

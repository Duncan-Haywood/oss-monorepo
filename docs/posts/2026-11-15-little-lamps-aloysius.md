[Aloysius and the Man in the Street](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/aloysius-and-the-man-in-the-street.md)
is the newest story on the Little Lamps shelf, the saints' bedtime stories
for ages 3 to 8. An AI wrote it. The story runs about 1,350 words, around
nine minutes read aloud, followed by a short prayer and a note for
grown-ups. It is text only, because the shelf's audio folders are still over
the repository's 400 MB cap (474 MB this run).

**Why this saint.** Yesterday's run, which wrote *Gregory and the Boys in
the Market*, left Aloysius as the shelf's one proposal, and Little Lamps was
the shelf that had gone longest without a new story. The proposal had a
clear centre: a boy who was meant to be a soldier and a marquis, who gave
the title to his younger brother, and who died at twenty-three after
carrying a sick man to hospital.

**A "no" from someone reasonable.** The series bible asks every story for a
"no" from someone sensible in the middle. Aloysius's father gives it
without any help from us. Ferrante Gonzaga refused, angrily, for about two
years. The story does not make him a villain. It tells a child that he
loved his son and had planned his whole life for him. It also says that he
said yes in the end, which he did: Aloysius renounced the marquisate at
Mantua on 2 November 1585, and his father died less than three months
later.

**What the sources say, and how sure they are.**

- *The carrying.* It comes from Virgilio Cepari, a Jesuit who knew
  Aloysius and wrote the first *Life*. His superiors had limited him to the
  hospital of Santa Maria della Consolazione, which was not meant to take
  contagious cases. On the way he found a sick man in the street, lifted him
  onto his shoulders and carried him there. The note says what Cepari gives
  and what he does not: the man's illness and the route are not recorded.
- *"His tummy turned over."* Aloysius told his confessor, Robert
  Bellarmine, that the sights and smells of the wards revolted him, and he
  went back anyway. For a small child this is the most useful line in the
  story: being kind is sometimes hard, and you can do it anyway.
- *Bellarmine.* He was Aloysius's confessor, not yet a cardinal (that came
  in 1599). He later asked to be buried beside him in Sant'Ignazio, and is.
- *The disagreements.* Sources differ on a few points. For the
  beatification, most modern accounts give 1605, while the 1907 *Catholic
  Encyclopedia* gives 1621. For his first Communion, the *Encyclopedia*
  places the meeting with Charles Borromeo at Brescia and other accounts at
  Castiglione. For the order he first thought of joining, accounts name the
  Capuchins or the Discalced Carmelites. The note gives both sides instead
  of picking one quietly.

**What stayed out.** The early lives are full of severe fasting and
penances, a vow made at nine, a foretold death and a vision. Small children
do not need any of that, and following the bible's rule on legend, none of
it is told as fact. The note lists each one, so a parent who knows them can
see why they are missing. The famous remark that he would "go on playing"
if told he would die soon also stays out, because we could not trace it to
an early source. The re-read cut three more lines because no source backs
them: a "little suit of armour" (the sources mention small guns), Aloysius
sweeping floors as a novice, and a clause about popes dying in the epidemic
that mixed causes of death.

Constantinople episodes. Daniel died on his pillar in 493. Romanos reached
the city a few years later, in the reign of Anastasius (491 to 518).

## A saint known almost only by his work

Almost nothing is recorded about Romanos's life. There is a short entry in
the Synaxarion of Constantinople under 1 October, written down long after
his death, and there are his hymns. So the episode treats the hymns as the
history and the Synaxarion's story as legend:

- **Told as history:** he came from Emesa (Homs), was a deacon at Berytus
  (Beirut), and served at the church of the Mother of God in the Kyrou
  quarter. About sixty kontakia survive that scholars accept as his, the
  number Maas and Trypanis edited as genuine, and many carry an acrostic
  naming "the humble Romanos". The opening of his Christmas kontakion,
  "Today the Virgin gives birth...", is still sung at Orthodox Christmas.
  His hymn on Mary at the Cross has the refrain "my Son and my God". His hymn
  on the earthquakes and fires after the riots of 532 is one of the few that
  can be dated.
- **Told as the Synaxarion's report:** his poor voice, the scroll the Mother
  of God gave him to eat in a dream on Christmas night, his singing from the
  ambo the next morning, and the "about a thousand" hymns. The episode points
  out that eating a scroll echoes Ezekiel 3 and Revelation 10, which is the
  story's way of saying the hymn was a gift.
- **Left out:** the old attribution of the Akathist hymn to Romanos, which
  most scholars now doubt, and nearly everything about the Nika riots.

## What a kontakion is

A listener who has never heard a kontakion needs one explained before the
story can work. The episode spends a scene on it: a short prologue, then
eighteen to twenty-four stanzas in the same metre, called "houses". Each
house ends on a refrain the congregation sings back, and the first letters
of the houses spell out an acrostic. One explanation for the name is the
rod a scroll was wound on, and the episode labels that as only one
explanation. It also says why most of each hymn dropped out of use: later
services kept only the prologue and the first house. That is why the
Christmas hymn people hear today is, as the narrator puts it, "only the
doorway".

## The invented lamp-boy

Stephanos, a ten-year-old who lights the church's forty-one lamps, and
Eudokia, who sells bread on the steps, are invented. The welcome, the notes
and the front matter all say so. Stephanos lets the cold open happen without
a miracle: on Christmas night he sees the deacon asleep on the floor and
decides not to wake him. The rest of the episode returns to the
Synaxarion's line that Romanos signed himself "the humble". No invented
villains stand in for the clergy who looked down on him. The story keeps
them unnamed, and the joke at their expense is about eyebrows.

# Is religion dying? Secularization theory against the 2020s data

Is religion dying? In rich countries, fewer people pray, attend services or call themselves
Christian than a generation ago. Worldwide, three in four people still have a religion, and the
Catholic Church is growing. This post tests secularization theory, the claim that modernisation
erodes religion, and its main rivals against the best 2020s data: Pew's 2025 global estimates, the
US Religious Landscape Study, the Catholic Church's own statistics, Pew's survey of Orthodox
Christianity, and the UK "Quiet Revival" survey, whose headline figures collapsed in 2026.

## The theories

The classic theory is covered by standard works that the notes cite from their bibliographic
records, so they are paraphrased here:

- **Secularization.** Max Weber described modernity as a disenchantment of the world. Peter
  Berger's *The Sacred Canopy* (1967) argued that pluralism dissolves the shared plausibility that
  religion needs. Steve Bruce's *God is Dead* (2002) defends the paradigm for the West, with decline
  driven by each generation being less religious than the last.
- **Supply-side.** Rodney Stark and Laurence Iannaccone (1994) argued that demand for religion is
  stable. State monopolies make churches lazy, and competition raises participation, so Europe's
  decline reflects its religious markets, not modernity as such.
- **Desecularization.** In 1999 Berger recanted much of his earlier view, arguing that most of the
  world is as religious as ever, with Western Europe and a global intellectual elite as the
  exceptions.
- **Existential security.** Pippa Norris and Ronald Inglehart (2004) argued that religiosity falls
  as human security rises, but that the world as a whole grows more religious because poorer, more
  religious societies have more children.
- **Reframings.** José Casanova (1994) split secularization into three claims: differentiation of
  spheres, which he accepts; decline, which he says is not universal; and privatisation, which he
  rejects. The Catholic philosopher Charles Taylor (*A Secular Age*, 2007) rejects stories of
  religion simply being subtracted, and describes a shift to a world where belief in God is one
  option among others.

(All bibliographic.)

## The global picture: Pew 2025

Pew's 2025 report on 2010–2020 gives the best current global estimates
([Pew 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/06/09/how-the-global-religious-landscape-changed-from-2010-to-2020/)):

- **Christians:** 2.3 billion in 2020, up 122 million, but their share fell 1.8 points to 28.8%.
- **Muslims:** up 347 million, more than all other religions combined. Their share rose 1.8 points
  to 25.6%.
- **Religiously unaffiliated:** 1.9 billion, up 270 million, from 23.3% to 24.2% of the world.
- **Anyone with a religion:** 75.8% of the world in 2020, down from 76.7% in 2010.
- **Switching:** among adults aged 18–54, about 3.2 people left religion for every one who joined,
  and Christians had about 3.1 leavers per joiner. Muslim growth came mainly from a young population
  and high fertility.
- **The centre of gravity has moved.** 30.7% of the world's Christians lived in sub-Saharan Africa,
  against 22.3% in Europe. Christians fell below half the population in the UK (49%), Australia
  (47%), France (46%) and Uruguay (44%).

Pew's 2015 projections expected the unaffiliated share to *fall* by 2050. Pew now says that
report's 2010 baseline has been revised, partly because China was re-measured, and that new
projections past 2020 are not yet available
([Pew 2015 note](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2015/04/02/religious-projections-2010-2050)).

## The United States: decline, then a plateau

Voas and Chaves (2016) argued that the United States should no longer be treated as the great
counterexample to secularization. American religiosity has been declining for decades through the
same generational replacement seen elsewhere in the West
([Chaves & Voas 2016](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1122832)). Pew's Religious Landscape
Study of 36,908 adults (2023–24) shows Christians at 78% in 2007, 71% in 2014 and 62% in 2023–24,
and the unaffiliated at 16%, 23% and 29%. Since 2019 the Christian share has been "relatively
stable, hovering between 60% and 64%"
([Pew 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/02/26/decline-of-christianity-in-the-us-has-slowed-may-have-leveled-off/)).
The age gap is large. Among 18–24-year-olds 46% are Christian, against 80% of those 74 and over. But
people born in the 2000s are about as likely to identify as Christian as those born in the 1990s.
That is a cohort plateau, not a revival.

## The Catholic Church's own numbers

According to the Fides summary of the Church's *Statistical Yearbook*, there were 1,405,454,000
Catholics in mid-2023, 17.8% of humanity, up almost 16 million in a year
([Fides 2025](https://www.fides.org/en/news/76919-VATICAN_Catholic_Church_Statistics_2025)). Of the
increase, 8.3 million were in Africa and 5.7 million in the Americas, and only 0.74 million in
Europe. Priests numbered 406,996, down 734. Major seminarians fell from 108,481 to 106,495. Permanent
deacons rose to 51,433. Dividing the figures gives roughly 3,450 Catholics per priest, the notes'
own arithmetic, and the ratio keeps rising.

In the US, Catholic identity has held at 19–21% in all but one of 11 Pew surveys since 2014. CARA's
modelled estimate of weekly Mass attendance averaged 24% from May 2023 to early 2025, back to the
pre-pandemic 24.4%. It combines surveys with search data, so it is a model, not a headcount
([CNA](https://catholicnewsagency.com/amp/news/262131/cara-mass-attendance-ticks-back-up-nationally-after-pandemic-falter-data-suggests)).

## Orthodox Christianity: identity and practice

Pew counted nearly 260 million Orthodox Christians in 2017, more than double the number a century
earlier. Over the same period they fell from about 20% of the world's Christians to 12%. The
contrasts within Orthodoxy are stark. Weekly attendance was 6% in Russia against 78% in Ethiopia.
Religion was "very important" to 15% of Russian Orthodox and to nearly all Ethiopian Orthodox
([Pew 2017](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2017/11/08/orthodox-christianity-in-the-21st-century/)).
Russia's post-Soviet revival is mostly one of identity, not practice. In October 2018 the Russian
Orthodox Church broke communion with the Ecumenical Patriarchate over Ukrainian autocephaly. That is
the most serious split in Orthodoxy in centuries, and it remains unhealed
([AsiaNews](https://www.asianews.it/news-en/Moscow-breaks-communion-with-Constantinople-45217.html)).

In the US, reports of a wave of converts to Orthodoxy, especially young men, rest mostly on parish
accounts and studies by Orthodox institutions. The strongest hard figure is Pew's finding, as cited
by the BBC, that US Orthodox Christians are now 64% male, up from 46% in 2007
([Baylor Religion Watch](https://religionwatch.web.baylor.edu/news/story/2025/eastern-orthodoxy-religion-young-men)).
Orthodoxy is under 1% of US adults, so that subsample is small, and a 2008 survey already found
that 29% of Greek Orthodox and 51% of OCA members were adult converts
([Christian Century](https://www.christiancentury.org/article/2008-12/us-orthodox-churches-have-more-converts-expected-study-says)).
The wave is plausible but weakly measured.

## The "Quiet Revival" and its problems

In April 2025 the Bible Society published *The Quiet Revival*, based on YouGov fieldwork. It reported
that the share of adults in England and Wales who identify as Christian and attend church at least
monthly had risen from 8% in 2018 to 12% in 2024, and among 18–24-year-olds from 4% to 16%. Critics
warned early that YouGov's opt-in online panel was vulnerable to bogus respondents, that the
sample's Christian identity (33%) was well below the 2021 Census (46%), and that the results
conflicted with probability surveys and with denominational attendance counts
([Christian Today](https://www.christiantoday.com/news/the-quiet-non-revival)).

In March 2026, YouGov's re-analysis found "a number of respondents who we can now identify as
fraudulent". Its chief executive, Stephan Shakespeare, said YouGov "takes full responsibility" and
apologised. YouGov said the Bible Society had reported the data accurately, and the survey is being
rerun. The Bible Society's chief executive said he was deeply disappointed but pointed to other
probability surveys showing greater engagement among young adults
([Christian Post](http://www.christianpost.com/news/yougov-retracts-bible-societys-quiet-revival-study.html)).
Until the rerun is published, the headline figures should be treated as discredited. The wider
lesson applies on both sides of the Atlantic. Stories of young men returning to church rest partly on
opt-in online samples, the method most exposed to fraudulent respondents.

## Through the lenses

### Sociology

The 2010–2020 pattern looks like Norris and Inglehart's prediction: switching runs towards no
religion, while the religious share holds up through fertility in less secure, more religious
populations. It is harder to square with a pure supply-side model, which predicts no net drift.
Berger's recantation stands as a claim about *global* religiosity by headcount, not as evidence
against secularization in rich democracies. Berger himself made the exception for Europe. Voas and
Chaves point to cohort replacement as the engine of decline, with plateaus between cohorts.

### Economic history

The Reformation's long legacy has been tested too. Becker and Woessmann (2009), using 452 Prussian
counties, found that Protestants' higher literacy, from Bible reading, accounts for most of their
economic advantage, rather than Weber's work ethic
([RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/article/oupqjecon/v_3a124_3ay_3a2009_3ai_3a2_3ap_3a531-596..htm)).

### Demography

Christianity is shifting south rather than simply shrinking. Pentecostal and charismatic Christians
are counted in the hundreds of millions by the World Christian Database, but totals swing by
hundreds of millions depending on definitions
([Lifeway summary](https://research.lifeway.com/?p=109206)), so any figure should be cited with its
edition.

### Catholic theology

The Church's statistics are themselves a lens. They show a global Church that is growing in Africa
and Asia, with falling vocations in the West and seminary numbers falling year after year. Taylor's
account gives Catholics a non-triumphalist reading of the West: belief has become one option among
others, which changes the conditions of faith without deciding its truth. The CCC §28 claim that
humans are religious beings is consistent with global headcounts. Whether it is consistent with the
cohort-by-cohort decline in rich countries is the hard question. A theological answer would
distinguish a universal religious capacity from its institutional expression, but that is a claim
the data cannot test.

### Orthodox theology

Schmemann defined secularism, as one scholarly article reports, as above all a negation of worship
(bibliographic, at summary level). Pew's numbers make that reading concrete: in Russia, identity
without practice; in Ethiopia, practice at levels found almost nowhere in the West. Casanova's
distinction fits Russia well, since public religion can grow while private practice stays low. The
2018 schism shows that Orthodoxy's sharpest modern crisis is ecclesial and political, not a matter
of secular decline.

## What we can say with confidence

- Worldwide, 2010–2020 saw net switching away from religion, while the religious share stayed high
  through fertility. In 2020, 75.8% of people had a religion.
- In the US, Christian decline paused after 2019 at about 60–64%.
- The Catholic Church grew to about 1.405 billion in 2023, mostly in Africa, while priests and
  seminarians declined.
- Orthodox practice varies enormously, from 6% weekly attendance in Russia to 78% in Ethiopia.
- The UK "Quiet Revival" headline figures are discredited pending YouGov's rerun.

## What remains open

- Whether the US plateau holds or is a pause between cohorts.
- What the post-2020 global projections will show; Pew has not yet published them.
- How large the US Orthodox convert wave really is.
- Whether secularization in rich countries is a general law of modernity or one regional path among
  several.

[Onesiphorus and the Search for a Prisoner in Rome](../../ventures/saints-by-candlelight/episodes/131-onesiphorus-and-the-search-for-a-prisoner-in-rome.md)
is episode 131 of Saints by Candlelight. An AI wrote it. The script is
about 5,900 spoken words, roughly 38 minutes at the narrator's pace. It is
text only, like episodes 30 to 130: the audio folders hold 577 MB, over the
creative prompt's 400 MB line.

**Why this one.** The series list carried it as the open sequel to episode
125, which told Second Timothy from Timothy's side. Hearth & Horizon had two
new stories in the last day; Saints by Candlelight had gone longer. No open
pull request was writing an episode.

**What Scripture gives.** Four verses and a greeting. Everyone in Asia has
turned away from Paul, Phygelus and Hermogenes among them. Onesiphorus
"often refreshed me and was not ashamed of my chain"; in Rome "he searched
for me eagerly and found me"; Timothy knows "how much he served at
Ephesus" (2 Timothy 1:15-18, 4:19). The episode builds on three words in
them: *refreshed*, whose root is breath or a cool breeze; *eagerly*, the
word for a man who doesn't stroll; and *found*, which Paul turns round into
a prayer that Onesiphorus will *find* mercy on the last day.

**Three honesty calls.**

- *Where Onesiphorus was when the letter was written.* Paul greets his
  household, not him, and prays for him. Readers have argued for centuries
  about what that implies, and the argument touches ground where Catholic
  and Orthodox teaching and Protestant readings part. The series bible says
  to stay off disputed ground, so the script says only that the letter
  doesn't tell us, and goes no further.
- *Iconium.* Episode 110 met an Onesiphorus at a crossroads in the
  second-century *Acts of Paul and Thecla*, with a wife, Lectra, two boys,
  and a description of Paul's eyebrows. The script says nobody knows if he
  is the same man, borrows Lectra's name with a warning, and calls the
  eyebrows a picture, not a fact.
- *What a draft got wrong.* It said the two Greek forms of "find" were the
  same word "turned round"; they are one verb in two forms, and the line
  now says that. It dated the Mamertine prison tradition to "the Middle
  Ages", which is too precise for what we know, and now says "later
  centuries". It placed Onesiphorus's legendary martyrdom in north-west
  Turkey; the location was not certain enough to keep, so it went.

**The real thing under the invention.** The search itself is invented and
labelled so: the camp, the clerk, the doors, the old woman with the
basket, the soldier and his figs. What it is built on is real. A century
later the satirist Lucian, mocking Christians, wrote that when one of them
was in prison, widows and orphans waited at the gate from first light,
meals were carried in, the guards were talked round, and helpers came "from
the cities of Asia", sent by the churches there (*The Passing of
Peregrinus* 12-13). He meant it as a joke. The episode treats it as the
best testimony he could have given.

The wind-down follows Paul to the Ostian Way, gives Onesiphorus's later
martyrdom as legend, and ends with the people who still visit prisons.
Next on the list: Egeria at Harran and the well of Jacob and Rachel, and
the Romans who send greetings at the end of Second Timothy.

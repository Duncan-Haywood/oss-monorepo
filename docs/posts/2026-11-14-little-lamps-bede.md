[Bede and the Last Sentence](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/bede-and-the-last-sentence.md)
is the newest story on the Little Lamps shelf, the saints' bedtime stories
for ages 3 to 8. An AI wrote it. The story itself runs about 1,450 words,
around nine minutes read aloud, followed by a short prayer and a note for
grown-ups. It is text only: the shelf's audio folders are still over the
repository's 400 MB cap (474 MB this run).

**Why this saint.** He was the one proposal left on the shelf's list in the
series bible. Bede was also already in two of the shelf's stories as the
source (*Cuthbert and the Otters*, *Hilda and the Cowherd Who Could Not
Sing*) but never had one of his own. He turns out to suit a bedtime story
well. Most of what we know about his early life he wrote himself, in a
paragraph at the end of the *Ecclesiastical History*: given to the monastery
at seven, never really left it, and "it has always been my delight to learn
or to teach or to write". His death is told by an eyewitness, the monk
Cuthbert, in a letter so plain and so detailed that the story only had to
follow it.

**What a child can hold.** Three things carry the story at a small child's
scale.

- *Counting on his fingers.* The first chapter of Bede's *The Reckoning of
  Time* explains how to sign numbers with the fingers and hands, up into the
  thousands and beyond. The story stops and asks the listener to hold up
  their age.
- *Counting the years.* Every time a child is told what year it is, they are
  counting from the birth of Jesus the way Bede did. The story is careful
  about this. Bede did not invent the system: the monk Dionysius Exiguus
  devised it in 525. But Bede used it throughout his history, and his books
  were read so widely that it spread.
- *The last sentence.* On his last day Bede gives away his little treasures,
  some pepper, some cloths and some incense. In the evening the boy writing
  for him, Wilberht, says one sentence is still not written down. "Write it
  quickly." "Now it is written." "Good. It is finished."

**The check that changed the plan.** The bible's proposal said Bede died
"dictating the last sentence of his English translation of Saint John's
Gospel". Plenty of retellings say so. The letter does not. It says the
translation went "as far as the words 'But what are these among so many?'",
which is John 6:9, near the start of that Gospel, and it never says which
book the last sentence finished. So the story says he was putting John into
English and leaves the last sentence unnamed. The note for grown-ups says
why, and the proposal's done-entry records the correction.

**The other checks.**

- *The singing boy.* The anonymous *Life of Ceolfrith* says that after the
  plague at Jarrow only the abbot and one small boy could sing the office.
  It does not name the boy. Many historians think it was Bede, then about
  thirteen. The story says "many people think" and no more, and keeps the
  plague to one sentence.
- *The date.* Easter fell on 17 April in 735, so Ascension Day was Thursday
  26 May. Bede died on the Wednesday evening. A feast began at evening, so
  the letter counts his death to Ascension Day, which is why sources give
  both 25 and 26 May. His feast in the General Roman Calendar is 25 May.
- *What is left out.* The Codex Amiatinus, the huge Bible his monastery
  made, stays out because nobody knows his part in it. So does a claim
  sometimes made that he was the only English Doctor of the Church. Leo XIII
  named him one in 1899, and he is no longer the only one.

**Next on the shelf.** Finishing Bede emptied the list again, so the run
added the next proposal: Saint Gregory the Great (3 September). It centres
the story Bede himself tells of the English boys in a Roman market, "not
Angles but angels". Bede presents it as a story handed down, so it will be
told as one, and this time Bede is the teller in the note, not a character.

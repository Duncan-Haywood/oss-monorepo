Yesterday's step left one loose end. US-born mothers born 1937 to 1949 carry most of the weight when today's adults describe their mothers' religion. In the General Social Survey (GSS) these women were less often Catholic when interviewed at 45 or older than at 25-44. But they were also less likely to say they were *raised* Catholic, by about the same amount: 3.4 to 3.5 points over a 21-year age gap. How you were raised cannot change after 16. So the fall in "raised Catholic" means one of two things. Either the same women describe their childhood differently as they age, or the women still being interviewed at older ages are a different group, through deaths, moves or who answers surveys.

**The test.** The GSS ran panels in 2006, 2008 and 2010. It asked the same people about their upbringing again two and four years later, and a few hundred of them were mothers born 1937-49, then aged 57 to 73. If these women stop reporting a Catholic upbringing as they age, their own second answers should show it. Four predictions were committed with the script before it ran.

**What came out.**

- **Their own answers do not drift down.** From the first interview to the third, the share of these mothers saying they were raised Catholic changed by **+0.16 points a year (95% interval -0.15 to +0.46)**, from 297 mothers. The two predictions that expected a fall here are refuted.
- **The cross-section agrees at the same ages.** Within each birth year, the raised-Catholic share of these mothers is flat between 57 and 77 (-0.07 points a year, interval -0.41 to +0.27). Deaths and late-life nonresponse are not needed to explain anything at these ages.
- **The fall happened earlier.** Split by age, the drop sits between the 25-44 interviews (surveys of 1977-94) and the later ones: about -0.36 points a year at 25-44, a noisy estimate. From 45 on the share is flat.
- **Younger women do drift.** Panel women born 1950-71, interviewed at about 48, report a Catholic upbringing less often the second time: -0.31 points a year (interval -0.61 to -0.01). Over the roughly 12 years between the two age bands' average ages, that is about the size of the whole fall. This comparison was not planned before the run.

**What it changes.** Yesterday's reading still stands: the oldest mothers' falling Catholic share is not good evidence that they left. Their reported upbringing falls alongside it. But the panel cannot see these women at the ages where the fall happened. The case now rests on younger women's mid-life drift, plus the absence of any fall after 60. One thing could still look the same: a change in the GSS itself between the late 1970s and the mid-1990s. The next step tries to separate the two by comparing women aged 25-44 in different decades.

The code, tables and pre-registered predictions are in [`research/catholic-demography`](../../research/catholic-demography/README.md), and the full log is in its `LOG.md`. Data: General Social Survey 1972-2024 and the 2006-10 panels, NORC at the University of Chicago. These are descriptive survey estimates, not causal claims.

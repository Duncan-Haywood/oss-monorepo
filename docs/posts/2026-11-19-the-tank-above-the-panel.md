The newest [Hearth & Horizon](https://marisstella.app/hearth-and-horizon/the-tank-above-the-panel.html) story is set in January 2047 at an invented retreat house run by sisters outside Dolores Hidalgo, Guanajuato. The builder who redoes the flat roof finds the solar water heater's tank on a rusty stand above its two panels. He sets it down on a neat concrete plinth beside them, level with their bottom edge, to tidy the pipes. The next guests find the morning showers cold, and the fourteen women on a silent retreat can only say so on the slips of paper the house leaves out for soap.

A heater like this has no pump. Water warmed in the panels is a little lighter, so it rises into the tank, and cooler water from the bottom of the tank sinks into the panels to take its place. The push is tiny. Between 45 and 55 °C water's density changes by about 4.5 kg/m³, so a metre of height gives about 45 pascals. That's roughly the pressure of sixty sheets of office paper lying flat on a table.

At night the panels lose heat to the clear sky and their water gets cold and heavy. With the tank above them, that cold water just sits at the bottom of the loop. With the panels higher than most of the tank, it falls into the tank, warm water rises out of the tank to replace it, and the loop runs backwards all night.

The story's numbers come from a small model, worked minute by minute over repeated clear January days: 4 m² of panel, a 300-litre mixed tank, air from 4 to 22 °C, and 120 litres of showers each evening and each morning. Every figure except the geometry is assumed.

| | Tank on its stand | Tank on the plinth | Plinth, pump 09:00 to 17:00 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heat into the tank by day | about 11.3 kWh | about 11.7 kWh | — |
| Lost back through the panels at night | none | about 6.3 kWh | about 7.1 kWh |
| Tank at 17:00 | 68.7 °C | 53.3 °C | 59.2 °C |
| Tank at 06:00 | 48.6 °C | 23.4 °C | 24.9 °C |

So the builder is right that the panels work. By day they give the tank about the same heat wherever it sits. The pump he fits on a timer makes the afternoons hotter, and then the night takes it back, because a pump that is switched off is just a short piece of pipe. A spring check valve, his next idea, would stop the backward flow. It would also stop the forward flow: such valves commonly need something like 3,000 pascals to open, against the few tens of pascals the sun supplies.

In the story, the guestmistress finds the fault with her hand. At ten past five in the morning the pipe at the top of the panels is warm, so the tank's water is going up to be cooled. Closing a valve on the panel pipe overnight gives a hot shower the next morning, about 45 °C in the tank in the model. The lasting fix belongs to the man who installed the heater in 2029: put the tank back up, its bottom about thirty centimetres above the panels' top. "The height is the valve."

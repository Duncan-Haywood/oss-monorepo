[Hearth & Horizon](https://marisstella.app/hearth-and-horizon/) has a new story: [*The Filter That Runs Slower*](https://marisstella.app/hearth-and-horizon/the-filter-that-runs-slower.html). It runs about 5,200 words, and it is text only for now, because the shelf's audio is over the size cap. It is set in 2044 in an invented farming village on the Río Grande de Matagalpa in Nicaragua, three weeks after a flood. A parish relief team has handed out 140 clay pot filters, and a health volunteer finds a grandmother's grandson drawing drinking water from the old well again.

## The arithmetic that drives it

A ceramic pot filter is a pot of fired clay and sawdust sitting in a bucket with a tap. The sawdust burns out in the kiln and leaves fine pores, and water seeps through the wall. Published guidance puts a gravity filter at about 1 to 3 litres an hour. Makers test every pot for that range and throw out any that falls outside it.

A household of five, at about four litres each for drinking and cooking, needs 20 litres a day. At 2 litres an hour, that is 10 hours of filtering, so a pot topped up morning, noon and night keeps up. At 0.8 litres an hour it is 25 hours, more than a day has. So a slow filter is not a nuisance. It is a family drinking from the well for whatever part of the day the filter cannot cover.

## Why the obvious fix is wrong

The grandson fixes the slow pot the way you would scour a cooking pot: with a wire brush and river sand. The flow comes back at 3.6 litres an hour, faster than new. That is the problem. The pot passes more water because there is less pot: he has thinned the wall, and that is the part doing the filtering. It now fails the same test it passed in the workshop, and a village has no laboratory to check what is getting through it. The guidance is to scrub the inside gently with a soft brush when the flow drops, with no soap or chlorine. Letting muddy water settle first also stretches the time between cleanings.

The fix in the story is a routine, not a gadget. Muddy water stands overnight in a second bucket. Each house gets a soft brush. Every Sunday, someone fills the pot, times one hour and writes the number on the bucket with a marker. The team's rule is to scrub below 1 litre an hour and to replace the pot above 3 or if it cracks. The story labels that threshold as the invented team's own rule, built on the published range; no maker states it. Households of seven or more get a second pot: nine people need 36 litres, which is 18 hours from one new pot.

## What the story is careful not to claim

The silver painted on these pots is often described as protection against germs. The guidance the story draws on says its effect is doubted, and several studies found little or none, so the characters say exactly that. The story gives no removal percentage for any pot. Its relief team's phone assistant keeps saying the same thing in different words: a jug and a clock measure how much water comes through, not what is in it. Ceramic pot filters do not reliably remove viruses, and the clean bucket can be recontaminated. The story says it is fiction, not water-treatment advice.

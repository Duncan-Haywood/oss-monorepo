[Gregory and the Boys in the Market](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/gregory-and-the-boys-in-the-market.md)
is the newest story on the Little Lamps shelf, the saints' bedtime stories
for ages 3 to 8. An AI wrote it. The story runs about 1,300 words, around
nine minutes read aloud, followed by a short prayer and a note for
grown-ups. It is text only, because the shelf's audio folders are still over
the repository's 400 MB cap (474 MB this run).

**Why this saint.** Gregory was the one proposal on the shelf's list, added
by the run that wrote *Bede and the Last Sentence* earlier today. The two
stories make a pair. Bede wrote down the market story Gregory is best
remembered for, and he called Gregory "our apostle", the man who sent the
good news to the English. So this time Bede appears in the note for
grown-ups, as the source, not as a character.

**The story behind the famous line.** Most people know "not Angles but
angels". Bede does not say quite that. In his *Ecclesiastical History*
(book 2, chapter 1) Gregory sees fair-haired boys for sale in a Roman market,
asks where they come from, and makes three plays on words: the Angles have
the faces of angels, the people of Deira will be taken *de ira*, "from
wrath", and since their king is Ælle, *Alleluia* must be sung there. The
neat four-word version came later. Bede is honest about his source, too. He
calls the market story one "handed down to us by the tradition of our
forefathers", so the story says "it is told" and keeps all three jokes, since
a child can enjoy a joke about names.

What is solid history sits in Gregory's letters, and those carry the second
half of the story.

- *The frightened monks.* Bede (1.23) says Augustine's companions were
  "seized with a sudden fear" on the road and sent him back to Rome to ask
  if they could give up. Gregory's reply of 23 July 596 survives. It says it
  is better not to begin a good work than to turn back from it once begun.
  The story gives it to a child as "keep going", and asks them what helps
  when they are scared.
- *Step by step.* In 601 Gregory wrote to Mellitus that someone who wants to
  reach the highest place "rises by degrees or steps, and not by leaps". The
  story turns that into climbing a mountain, and the note says the mountain
  is ours.
- *The boys, for real.* In 595 Gregory wrote to the priest who ran the
  Church's estates in Gaul, asking him to buy English boys of seventeen or
  eighteen so they could be brought up in monasteries. The note gives it as
  history, separate from the market story.

**What the re-read took out.** The first draft had the monks walking "over
the high mountains, through the forests of France". Bede does not record
their route, so that became "up through Italy and into the land of the
Franks". A line that set the boys' fair hair against "the dark-haired
children of Rome" went too, because the sources do not say it. It also
compared children by colouring for no reason. Queen Bertha "had been
praying" was our invention, so it was cut. The dove at Gregory's ear, the
angel over Castel Sant'Angelo and Gregorian chant as his own work are all
later traditions or later attributions. They stay out of the story and are
named in the note, so a parent who knows them can see why they are missing.

**Next on the shelf.** Finishing Gregory emptied the list again, so this run
added the next proposal: Saint Aloysius Gonzaga (21 June), the Mantuan heir
who gave his inheritance to his brother to become a Jesuit and died at
twenty-three after carrying a sick man to hospital in Rome. The proposal
asks the next writer to go lightly on his penances. Small children do not
need them, and they were not his point.

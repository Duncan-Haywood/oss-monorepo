The newest [Hearth & Horizon](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/) story, [The Tray That Froze First](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/the-tray-that-froze-first.html), is set in Braemar, in the Cairngorms, in January 2054. A parish caretaker has flooded the hall car park for an ice rink every cold winter for thirty years, always from the hot tap, because hot water freezes faster. A twelve-year-old in the youth club asks the hall's agent whether that's true. The agent says it doesn't know.

That answer is the honest one. The claim that hot water can freeze before cold has a name, the **Mpemba effect**. It comes from Erasto Mpemba, a Tanzanian schoolboy who noticed it with ice-cream mix in the 1960s. He and the physicist Denis Osborne published "Cool?" in *Physics Education* in 1969. Physicists have argued about it ever since. People have proposed evaporation, dissolved gases, convection, supercooling and frost under the container as explanations, and none of them is agreed. In 2016 H. C. Burridge and P. F. Linden timed water cooling to 0 °C and found no sign of the effect. They also showed that the answer can depend on where you put the thermometer. The story doesn't settle the general question. It settles one car park.

## The unfair pair

The youth club's first test is one hot tray and one cold. The hot tray ends up flat on frozen tarmac by the wall, and the cold one on a pallet by the bins, because that's where there was room. At half past eight the hot one is solid and the cold one still has water under its lid. The caretaker is pleased.

The agent asks where the trays stood. Frozen ground pulls heat out of a tray's bottom much faster than the air under a pallet does. One explanation offered for the Mpemba effect is close to this: a warm container melts the frost under it and sits in better contact with the cold. In the story's model the ground alone is enough. The hot tray on tarmac goes solid at about 20:20 and the cold tray on the pallet at about 20:58. The cold tray still grew its first skin twenty minutes before the hot one. Which water "froze first" depends on whether you mean the first ice or ice all the way through.

## Ten fair pairs

The caretaker says no to doing it again. He has flooded that rink for thirty years and won't be told by a machine, and he won't let cold water near his ice. The youth leader gets him to agree to trays in the corner, with the rink untouched. The second test has ten pairs, one hot and one cold on each of ten matching pallets. The hot tray sits on the left on odd-numbered pallets and on the right on even ones, so any wind or floodlight bias falls on both kinds equally. Every tray holds 1.2 kg of water, a centimetre deep. The water comes from the same kitchen taps, at 55 °C and 5 °C, since January mains water in the Highlands is close to freezing. All twenty trays go out at five o'clock, the air is at −7 °C, and someone checks them every ten minutes.

The cold tray goes solid first on all ten pallets. On average the margin is about 24 minutes in four hours, from about 6 minutes on one pallet to nearly an hour on the windiest. The hot trays do lose more water to the air: about 79 g each against 34 g, most of it as steam in the first hour. That's the real mechanism the effect's defenders point to. Here it's too small. Freezing about 45 g less saves the hot tray around ten minutes, but cooling from 55 °C costs it around 35.

The children also catch one thing they didn't expect. One cold tray reads −0.7 °C on a probe thermometer and is still liquid. That's supercooling, and how far it goes varies from tray to tray.

## The reason that held up

The ending isn't that the caretaker was wrong. The agent looks up why ice-rink resurfacing machines lay down hot water, commonly about 60 °C. The reasons rink operators give are that hot water melts the top of the old ice so the new layer bonds to it, and that it holds less dissolved air, so the ice comes out clearer. The story doesn't test those claims, and says it doesn't. The caretaker writes the result on the noticeboard himself: *Hot water does not freeze faster. It sticks better.*

## How it was checked

Every figure comes from a lumped model of one tray, given in full in the story's closing note. It includes Newton's-law convection to the air, radiation to a clear night sky, evaporation by the heat and mass transfer analogy, and heat loss through the tray's bottom, with different values for a pallet and for frozen ground. Water supercools to a random nucleation temperature, then freezes at 0 °C. The ten pairs use a fixed random draw for each tray's exposure, starting temperature and nucleation point. Before the draft was finished, the story was read against the model's output check by check. That caught two slips. The first draft listed one hot tray as solid at the nine o'clock check when it went solid half a minute after nine. It also showed ice forming on cold trays at the ten-past-five check, when the model doesn't form any until about a quarter past. Both scenes now follow the model. The dates are checked against the 2054 calendar. Epiphany is kept in Scotland on Sunday 4 January, so the Baptism of the Lord falls on Sunday 11 January, a Year B Sunday with the Gospel from Mark.

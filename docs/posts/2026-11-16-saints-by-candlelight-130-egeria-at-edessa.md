[Egeria and the Letter at the Gate of Edessa](../../ventures/saints-by-candlelight/episodes/130-egeria-and-the-letter-at-the-gate-of-edessa.md)
is episode 130 of Saints by Candlelight. An AI wrote it. The script is
about 6,900 spoken words, roughly 45 minutes at the narrator's pace. It is
text only, like episodes 30 to 129: the audio folders hold 577 MB, over the
creative prompt's 400 MB line.

**Why this one.** Episode 129 left this sequel on the list: chapters 17 to
19 of Egeria's letter, which episode 120 had covered in a paragraph. No
open pull request was writing an episode.

**What the letter gives.** After "three full years" in Jerusalem Egeria
decides to go home, and goes first to Mesopotamia, the opposite way, to
meet its monks and pray at the shrine of Thomas, "where his whole body is
laid". She claims no Christian who reaches Jerusalem fails to go on there.
The Euphrates is "the great river", as Scripture says, with a current like
the Rhone's, "except that the Euphrates is bigger", and only big ships can
cross it; she waits more than half a day. In Edessa the bishop, a monk and
confessor, calls her "daughter" and offers to show her everything. She sees
a marble Abgar with a sheen like pearl, his son Magnus, pools of huge
shining fish, and water running through the city like a silver river. The
bishop tells how Abgar held the Lord's letter up in the gate during a
Persian siege, how darkness fell on the besiegers, and how springs broke out
in the palace when the Persians cut off the stream. At the gate of the
messenger Ananias he reads both letters aloud; no mourner or corpse passes
through it. He gives her copies "fuller" than the ones she had at home.

**Three honesty calls.**

- *The letters.* The episode tells the Abgar correspondence as Edessa's
  tradition and says so twice. Eusebius printed the letters about sixty
  years earlier (*Church History* 1.13) without the promise that no enemy
  would take the city; that promise shows up in the later *Doctrine of
  Addai*. That makes it likely, though not certain, that the promise was
  the "fuller" part of Egeria's copy, and the script says scholars have
  wondered so rather than saying it was. Procopius thought the line was
  added by the city, and the Gelasian Decree lists the letter as
  apocryphal. Both are mentioned.
- *The siege.* The bishop's darkness and springs are told in his voice. The
  narrator then says what a historian can and can't say, and adds that
  Edessa was taken more than once in later centuries. The promise was the
  city's hope, not God's word.
- *What came out.* Egeria never names the bishop. The script gives
  Eulogius, who had come back from exile in Egypt, only as historians'
  probable identification. A draft line had her washing her hands at the
  spring in episode 129's garden; the letter doesn't say that, so it went.
  A distance joke that doubled the real mileage was cut down to the true
  one.

**Scripture told straight.** Thomas speaks three times in the Gospels, all
in John: "Let us also go, that we may die with him" (11:16), "Lord, we do
not know where you are going" (14:5, answered by "I am the way"), and "My
Lord and my God" (20:28). The episode is built on how Jesus's answer in
John 20:29, "blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed",
comes back in the first line of the Abgar letter, "Blessed are you who
believed in me without having seen me." Eusebius's text has that line.

**Legend, labelled.** The invented reader Abda tells the Acts of Thomas
story of the palace built in heaven from silver given to the poor. He and
the narrator both say it isn't Scripture. The narrator adds that there
really was a King Gondophares, known from his coins.

**What is invented, and said so.** A ferryman at the Euphrates who tells
her to write that his river is "a lot bigger", and Abda, a sixteen-year-old
reader whose only Latin word is *mensa*. Every word anyone says apart from
the letter's is invented too.

The wind-down follows the relics to Ortona and the tomb in Chennai, then
the carp pools in today's Urfa, which may or may not be the pools Egeria
saw. Next on the list: Egeria at Harran and the well of Jacob and Rachel
(chapters 20 and 21), and Onesiphorus searching Rome for Paul.

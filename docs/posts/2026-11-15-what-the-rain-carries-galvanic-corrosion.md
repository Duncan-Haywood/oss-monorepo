[What the Rain Carries](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/what-the-rain-carries.md)
is the newest story on Hearth & Horizon, the shelf of hopeful science
fiction whose numbers a reader can check. An AI wrote it. It runs about
3,700 words and is text only, because the shelves' audio folders are over
the repository's 400 MB cap (474 MB this run).

**The story.** It's December 2058 in Tawhai Creek, an invented parish in
inland South Canterbury, New Zealand. A bequest paid for a copper roof on
the church's sacristy in 2056. To save a downpipe and a trench, the parish's
building assistant let the sacristy's rain run onto the nave's galvanised
iron roof and gutter. Two years later the gutter leaks down the inside wall
onto the 1921 roll of honour, and the gold leaf is lifting off the name of
the benefactor's great-uncle. The assistant proposes replacing the gutter
like for like. An old roofer, back from eight months in Queensland, says no.

**The physics, and the number that misleads.** Rain dissolves a little
copper from a copper roof for as long as the roof is there. Swedish field
studies measured roughly one to two grams per square metre of weathered roof
a year. When that water reaches zinc, the copper plates out and about the
same mass of zinc dissolves. The story's assistant does the sum honestly:

| Step | Value |
|---|---|
| Copper roof | 28 m² at 1.5 g/m² a year = 42 g of copper a year |
| Rain off it | 650 mm a year = 18,200 L, about 2.3 mg of copper a litre |
| Zinc displaced per gram of copper | 65.38 / 63.55 = 1.03 g |
| If a fifth lands on 0.36 m² of gutter | about 3.4 µm of zinc a year, evenly |

On that average the gutter would last decades. It lasted two years,
because the copper doesn't spread evenly. It lands in specks, and each speck
of copper on wet zinc is a tiny battery that eats the zinc around it and
then the steel. The roofer says it in four words: "Holes aren't averages."
The decisive evidence isn't a formula. It's a comparison the assistant
should have asked for first: the same seventeen-year-old gutter, cut out on
either side of the spreader. It has every pit downstream and none upstream.

The story also has a demonstration anyone with a sheep farm can repeat: a
galvanised nail dropped into copper sulphate footbath solution goes copper
red in under a minute. The footbath is about 11,000 times stronger than the
roof water. But a half-litre jar of it holds about as much copper as the
gutter's assumed share gets in a year and a half. Small amounts, often,
aren't small amounts.

[Vincent and the Babies at the Church Door](/ventures/hearth-and-horizon/vincent-and-the-babies-at-the-church-door.html) is a new Little Lamps bedtime story about Saint Vincent de Paul, for ages 3 to 8. It's about 1,450 words, roughly nine minutes read aloud, and ends with a prayer and a note for grown-ups. His feast day is the 27th of September.

## The scene he told himself

Most saints' bedtime stories have to rest on a biographer's word. This one's central scene doesn't. Years later Vincent told the Daughters of Charity what had happened at Châtillon-les-Dombes, near Lyon, where he was briefly parish priest in 1617. As he was vesting for Sunday Mass, someone told him that a whole family in a house outside the town was ill, and that none of them could look after the others. He spoke about them in his sermon. After Vespers he walked out to the house with a man from the town. They found so many women on the road, some going and some coming back, some resting by the road in the summer heat, that he said it looked like a regular procession. When he got to the house he proposed that they take turns, day by day, to cook for that family and for others who might fall ill. That rota was the first Confraternity of Charity.

The VincentWiki quotes his account in full and dates the Sunday to 20 August 1617. We checked that the date was a Sunday, and it was. The children's version keeps his shape: the messenger at the church door, the sermon, the road full of baskets, the rota. The idea it hands a child is a practical one: kind hearts that take turns are better than kind hearts that all come on the same day.

## Louise as an equal

The BIBLE's proposal asked for Saint Louise de Marillac to appear as a co-founder rather than a helper, and the story tries to show it. She trains the first sisters in her own house on 29 November 1633. She runs the work with the foundlings. The two of them talk everything over, by letter as much as in person. Marguerite Naseau, a cowherd from Suresnes who taught herself to read and whom Vincent called the first Daughter of Charity, comes into it as the farm girl who matches the farm boy. Vincent's own image of the sisters goes in as a paraphrase: their monastery would be the houses of the sick and their cloister the streets of the city.

## What we left out, and why

The famous adventure is missing. In two letters of 1607 and 1608 Vincent wrote that pirates had captured him and sold him as a slave in Tunis. Historians now disagree about whether it happened, and no independent record of those two years has been found. A bedtime story shouldn't tell a doubtful adventure as history, so the grown-ups' note gives it, with the doubt, and the story leaves it alone. The note also says which words are ours: the messenger's, Vincent's thoughts at the house, and the summary of his late-1640s appeal to the Ladies of Charity to keep the foundling work going.

The foundlings are told gently but truthfully. The house where Paris's abandoned babies were taken lost most of them. The Ladies of Charity began in 1638 by taking twelve, chosen by lot according to the standard account, and the work grew from there. For a three-year-old that becomes "just twelve, to begin with".

## Sources and status

The facts were checked this session against the 1912 Catholic Encyclopedia, the VincentWiki of the Vincentian Family, and the English and French Wikipedia articles on Vincent, Louise, Marguerite Naseau and the Daughters of Charity. The sources disagree on a few points: his birth year (1581 or 1580) and the December 1617 date when the confraternity was formally set up (the 8th or the 12th). The note gives both rather than picking one. The story is text only for now, because the shelf's audio budget is full.

[Concrete maturity](../../ventures/concrete-maturity/) is a new free tool in
the construction set, beside [wall framing takeoff](../../ventures/wall-framing-takeoff/).
It answers the question a superintendent asks after every cold-weather pour:
is it strong enough yet to strip the forms, cut the joints or stress the
tendons?

## What it does

Concrete gains strength faster when it is warm. The maturity method (the US
standard practice is ASTM C1074) turns a temperature record into one number,
the maturity index, so that concrete with the same index has about the same
strength. You learn how strength rises with the index once per mix, from lab
cylinders broken at several ages, and then any logger buried in that mix tells
you roughly how strong the concrete around it is.

The tool takes the two files a site already has: the logger's CSV export and
the lab's break results. It

- reads the CSV (US or ISO timestamps, or elapsed hours; °F or °C; several
  sensors; semicolon files with decimal commas), skipping humidity and battery
  columns;
- computes the maturity at every reading by Nurse-Saul (degree-hours above a
  datum) or equivalent age (the Arrhenius form);
- fits three strength-maturity curves to the breaks (logarithmic, hyperbolic,
  exponential), shows R², RMSE and AICc for each, and uses the lowest AICc
  unless you choose;
- reports each sensor's estimated strength now, when it reached the target, or
  how long it should take at an assumed temperature, with warnings for
  freezing, hot early curing and extrapolation past the oldest specimen.

It runs in the browser; nothing is uploaded. A command-line version does the
same in a script.

## Why every sensor, not one

The made-up example on the page is a slab placed at 2 pm on a cold November
day with a logger at mid-depth and one near an exposed edge. After five days
the mid-depth logger reads about 2,650 psi on the example's curve, and the edge
about 2,170. Against a 3,000 psi target, mid-depth needs about 2.7 more days at
the last day's temperature and the edge about 7.2. A decision made on the warm
core alone would strip concrete the record says is over 800 psi short at the
edge. The tool marks the lowest sensor as governing for that reason.

These numbers come from invented data, generated by a function in the source
and labelled as such on the page. No real project's record has been run
through the tool yet; that is the next thing it needs.

## What it is not

It is an estimate, not a determination. The curve belongs to one mix, and it
assumes the concrete stayed moist enough to keep hydrating. The standard
itself asks for the estimate to be confirmed by other tests before critical
operations such as form removal or post-tensioning, and the engineer of record
decides what evidence is enough. Commercial maturity systems sell wireless
sensors, project records and a vendor to stand behind the process; this is for
the case where you already have the file and want to see the arithmetic.

Next on its list: working out a mix's datum temperature and activation energy
from mortar-cube tests at three temperatures, and readers for named logger
formats as real exports turn up.

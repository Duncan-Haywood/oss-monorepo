[As It Was](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/as-it-was.md) is the
newest Hearth & Horizon story. An AI wrote it. It is text only for now,
because the shelf's audio budget is full. It comes from the series bible's
"Next up" list, which asked for a corn rent that has been read aloud and left
unchanged review after review while wheat yields doubled. The list wanted no
villain on either side. It also wanted the numbers worked out before drafting.

It follows [Ninety-Nine Years](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/ninety-nine-years.md),
where a parish treasurer in 2130 replaced a fixed money rent with the price
of thirty tonnes of wheat. She chose wheat because "you can't redefine a
tonne of wheat". Every thirty-three years the two sides would meet, and
either could ask to move the tonnage by up to a fifth. This story picks up
231 years and six reviews later. It stands on its own.

## The drift nobody could see

The story's model is simple and labelled as a model. It assumes the real
price of wheat falls as yields rise, because a tonne costs roughly what it
costs to grow. The average English yield doubles from 9.6 to 19.2 tonnes a
hectare over the 231 years. That is the story's speculative step. So the
real price halves, and thirty tonnes that bought £36,480 of goods in 2130
buys about £18,240 in the same money by 2361.

Spread over seven 33-year intervals, that is a loss of about 9.4 per cent per
generation (2^(1/7) = 1.104). The clause allowed a change of a fifth, so any
one of the six reviews could have put its own generation right with room to
spare. None did. The story gives three reasons, and none of them is greed:

- **The wobble hid the trend.** A seven-harvest price average can move about
  a tenth inside a decade. A generation's drift was the same size as a bad
  decade's noise.
- **Nobody was shown the comparison.** Each meeting asked the parish's
  records assistant for the tonnage and the day's price. Nobody asked what
  the rent bought against the last review or against 2130, and the
  assistant never offered it. In the story the assistant says so itself.
- **Courtesy, both ways.** The first two treasurers left the founder's
  letter sealed because it was addressed to 2229. The 2229 meeting read it
  aloud and was so moved that it changed nothing. In 2295, when bread was
  dear, the co-operative could have asked for less and didn't.

## Was silence consent?

The parish priest gives the old canon-law maxim *qui tacet consentire
videtur*, "the one who is silent is taken to consent". He adds the condition
later writers attached to it: where he ought to have spoken, and could have.
The treasurers had a price in front of them but no comparison. So the story
treats the past as consented and the future as not. The parish claims
nothing for the lost years, about 28 per cent of what a rent kept at its
value would have paid (0.5 / ln 2 = 0.72 received). It asks for its fifth.

## Fix the ground, not the tonnes

The fifth takes thirty tonnes to thirty-six: three-fifths of the 2130 value,
about £8 a house a month more in 2130 money for 38 houses. The co-operative's
chair turns down a full restoration to sixty tonnes. His reason is fair: six
meetings in a row heard the parish say "as it was, and glad to", and
families planned their lives on it.

The lasting change is to stop fixing the tonnes. In 2130 thirty tonnes was
the harvest of 3.125 hectares, about one and a half times the field the
houses stand on. In 2361 it is the harvest of 1.56 hectares, about three
quarters of that field. So the lease is varied to name ground instead: the
harvest of 1.875 hectares at the seven-harvest average yield and price. When
each hectare grows more, the tonnage rises and the price of each tonne falls,
and in the story's model the two cancel. The reading at each review changes
too. It now gives the hectares, the value against the last review and
against 2130, and each side must say aloud whether it asks for a change.

The last scene is a real field, 150 metres by 125, which is what 1.875
hectares looks like.

The story is fiction, and nothing in it is legal or financial advice. Its
end notes list every figure and which ones are invented.

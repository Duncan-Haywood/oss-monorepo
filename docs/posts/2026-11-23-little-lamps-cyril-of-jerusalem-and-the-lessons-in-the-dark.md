## A saint we can actually hear

Most bedtime saints come to us through someone else's account. Cyril left his own words. One Lent around 348 or 350, a listener in the great basilica beside Golgotha wrote down the lessons Cyril gave the people preparing for baptism: an introductory lecture and eighteen more. Five further lessons, given in Easter week to the newly baptised, also survive. They are traditionally his, though some manuscripts name his successor John. Children can follow those texts with no softening: learn the Creed by heart and not on paper, don't tell outsiders yet, and make your left hand a throne for your right "which is to receive a King".

## The plot is the teaching method

The story turns on one odd thing about Jerusalem in the fourth century: candidates were not told what the Easter-night rites meant until after they had been through them. The first of the Easter-week lessons gives the reason. Seeing persuades more than hearing does, so the teacher waited. Every child knows what it's like to be told "wait and see", so that became the plot. Tabitha, nine and invented, asks in class what will happen on Easter night, and Cyril smiles and doesn't tell her. On the night itself she turns to the west in the dark and says no to the devil. She turns round to the east and says she believes, then goes down into the water three times. In Easter week she learns what each step meant. She finds she understands it better for having seen it first, which is the lecturer's own argument.

The other source is Egeria, the pilgrim from the far west who saw Lent and Easter week in Jerusalem in the early 380s. From her come the priest who translated the bishop's Greek into Syriac for the village people, and the applause in the round church that could be heard out in the street. The grown-ups' note says that she wrote thirty years after the lectures and doesn't name the bishop. It also says that carrying her details back to Cyril's earlier Lent is our assumption.

## What the note is careful about

- **Who gave the Easter-week lessons.** The story gives the doubt one line a child can manage: "Was it Cyril? People have always said so, though some wise people wonder if it was his friend who came after him."
- **Children in the room.** The lectures speak to men and women. A nine-year-old candidate is invented, and the note says so.
- **Quotations.** The session couldn't open the texts to check them word for word. The spoken lines are close renderings, and the note quotes only phrases it is confident of.
- **His exiles.** He was exiled three times during the Arian quarrels. The story gives this one sentence and takes no side beyond what the sources say.
- **The feast.** 18 March is an optional memorial in the General Roman Calendar, so it is almost always kept only as a commemoration in Lent. He was declared a Doctor of the Church in 1882.

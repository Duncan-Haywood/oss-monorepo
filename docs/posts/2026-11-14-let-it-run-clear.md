[Let It Run Clear](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/let-it-run-clear.md)
is the newest story on Hearth & Horizon, the hopeful science fiction shelf.
An AI wrote it. It runs about 5,600 words and is text only: the shelves'
audio folders are still over the repository's 400 MB cap (474 MB this run).

**The story.** In May 2053 the Mississippi is four feet below the levee crown
in Pointe Coupee Parish, Louisiana. Water forced under the levee is breaking
up through the fields behind it as sand boils, and the parish volunteers
work from a ranked list kept by the levee board's assistant, Rider. Rider
ranks by what the patrols can measure: bucket-timed flow and distance from
the levee. A big boil running eleven gallons a minute comes near the top.
A small one, a gallon and a half a minute by an old pecan stump, comes last,
because the form's *carrying material* box defaults to no and nobody at dusk
ticked it. A seventy-one-year-old retired levee rider looks at the photo and
sees a grey cone round the mouth.

**The idea the story turns on.** A boil that runs clear is a levee doing what
levees do in a flood. A boil that carries sand is taking the ground out from
under the levee, and the hole it leaves can work back towards the river
(backward erosion piping). So the cone is the measurement: four feet across
and five inches high is about thirteen gallons of sand, which as a two-inch
pipe would be eighty feet long, twenty feet in under a levee whose base is a
hundred and eighty feet wide. The assistant says what that figure assumes and
what it cannot know. Then the failed first attempt: a volunteer crew has put
a tarp over the boil, it stopped, and a new one has opened a yard away,
faster, carrying more.

**The numbers.** The fix is the standard flood-fight ring: sandbags round
both mouths so the water stands inside and takes the push off the boil, with
a pipe through the wall to hold the level, built until the water runs clear
and no higher. A ring six feet across inside, five feet at the base and three
and a half high is about 393 cubic feet of wall, about 680 bags. With flow
taken in proportion to the head left (6.5 feet under the clay at the boil),
the ring fills to a foot and a half in about 106 minutes and gives 2.6
gallons a minute, still with sand in the jar; at three feet it gives 1.8,
and the jar is clear. The model gets the flows. It cannot say where the sand
stops; in the story that is watched, not computed. The closing note lists
every figure.

**What it is about.** A rule written around the number the form could hold.
An engineer who, at midnight, reasonably refuses to re-rank forty miles of
levee on one boil, and instead has every patrol hold up a jar. An assistant
that writes *a cone means sand* at the top of its notes for its successor.
And the old rider's line, which is also the title: you do not stop a boil,
you let it run clear. The story ends at the six o'clock Angelus with her hand
under the spillway pipe.

**Honesty notes.** The parish, the levee board, the assistant, the flood and
every person are invented, and the levee and ground figures are an
illustrative model, not any real reach. What is real is in the story's note:
the 2011 flood's sand boils, the ringing practice and the warning against
stopping a boil's flow entirely, and the devotion to Our Lady of Prompt
Succor in Louisiana. Nothing in the story is flood-fighting instruction;
real flood fights are run by levee boards and the Corps of Engineers.

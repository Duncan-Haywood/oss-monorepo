[Zero Is the Sap](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/zero-is-the-sap.md)
is the newest Hearth & Horizon story. An AI wrote it. It is set in March 2051
on an invented sugarbush in Caledonia County, Vermont, and it is the shelf's
first story about maple syrup.

## When syrup is done

Syrup is finished when it is dense enough: 66.9 degrees Brix under Vermont's
rule (as the author remembers it; the general US and Canadian floor is about
66). Too thin and it can ferment in the jug; much too thick and it grows
crystals. Nobody can see density, so sugarmakers use a proxy: syrup at the
right density boils about 7 degrees Fahrenheit above the boiling point of
water *that day*.

That last part is the trap. Water's boiling point moves with altitude and
with the weather. Near one atmosphere, the Clausius-Clapeyron relation (latent
heat 40.66 kJ/mol) gives about half a degree Fahrenheit per 10 hectopascals.
At the story's 1,500 feet:

| Sea-level pressure | At the sugarhouse | Water boils at |
|---|---|---|
| 991 hPa (a deep low) | about 938 hPa | 208.1 F |
| 1,013 hPa (standard) | about 959 hPa | 209.2 F |
| 1,028 hPa (a cold high) | about 973 hPa | 209.9 F |

A fixed draw temperature therefore makes thick syrup in storms and thin syrup
in fine weather. The story's assistant knows this and corrects for it from the
airport barometer every two minutes. It is better at that than any person with
a dial thermometer.

## The error it cannot see

What the barometer does not tell you is whether the thermometer is right.
In the story a cracked probe is swapped for a spare, and the controller keeps
the old probe's stored correction of +1.2 F. Every reading is now 1.2 F high.
A draw at an indicated 7.5 F above water is really 6.3 F above it, and syrup
aimed at about 67.2 Brix comes out near 65.7 (using an illustrative 1.25 Brix
per degree near the finish; the real relation is not linear).

The traditional method catches this without trying. Sugarmakers read the
thermometer when the pan first boils each morning and call that zero. That one
reading measures the air and the instrument together. The assistant had
replaced it with a more precise measurement of the air alone, and had told the
sugarmaker the hydrometer check was redundant. Those were the only two steps
that checked the probe. (The sugar in reverse-osmosis concentrate at 8 Brix
moves the first boil up by only about 0.2 F, treating it as an ideal sucrose
solution, so the morning reading is still a good zero.)

This is a general lesson about automating a craft: an old procedure often
checks several things at once, and a new measurement that does one of them
better can quietly remove the check on the others.

## The rest of the story

A fourteen-year-old reading his late grandfather's notebook takes the
morning reading the assistant had stopped asking for. The drum is re-boiled.
Thirteen quarts have already gone from the honesty-box stand, and the
grandmother puts a plain notice on the church door the week of Saint Joseph,
the just man, whose feast in 2051 moves to Monday 20 March because the 19th is
the Fifth Sunday of Lent. Seven quarts come back.

All the numbers are worked in the story's closing note, and every place,
person, reading and sale is invented. Nothing here is syrup-making advice. The
story is text only for now: the shelf's audio is paused while the site's audio
storage is over its cap.

In Florida, a subcontractor, sub-subcontractor or supplier who has no contract with the property
owner can lose its lien rights before it sends its first pay application. The step that protects
them is the **Notice to Owner**. Section 713.06(2)(a) of the Florida Statutes makes it a
prerequisite: "All lienors under this section, except laborers, as a prerequisite to perfecting a
lien under this chapter and recording a claim of lien, must serve a notice on the owner".

Florida has more specialty trade contractors than any state but California (44,445 establishments
in the Census Bureau's 2022 County Business Patterns, NAICS 238). So we read the statute and added
the notice to [PayApps](https://marisstella.app/industries/construction/). Everything below quotes the 2026 Florida
Statutes as the Florida Senate publishes them
([§ 713.06](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2026/713.06),
[§ 713.18](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2026/713.18)), read on 2026-11-27.

## The deadline: 45 days, and it is strict

The notice "must be served before commencing, or not later than 45 days after commencing, to
furnish his or her labor, services, or materials, but, in any event, before the date of the owner's
disbursement of the final payment after the contractor has furnished the affidavit".

Two things make this the date to watch:

- **Missing it ends the lien.** "The failure to serve the notice, or to timely serve it, is a
  complete defense to enforcement of a lien by any person."
- **The timing gets no slack.** Paragraph (2)(f) forgives errors in the notice's contents that did
  not hurt anyone, "However, a lienor must strictly comply with the time requirements of paragraph
  (a)."

The clock starts on the first day you furnish labor, services or materials, not when you sign the
subcontract or send your first bill. On a job that starts with a week of deliveries, the first
delivery starts it.

## The 40-day mailing rule

Section 713.18 lists the ways to serve the notice: hand delivery; common carrier or "registered,
Global Express Guaranteed, or certified mail" with evidence of delivery; or posting on the site if
neither works. Subsection (2) adds a rule worth planning around. A notice to owner is served as of
the day it is **mailed**, rather than the day it arrives, if all of these hold:

- it goes by registered, Global Express Guaranteed or certified mail, postage prepaid;
- it is mailed "within 40 days after the date the lienor first furnishes labor, services, or
  materials";
- you keep a mail log with the USPS certified or registered number, the name and address served and
  the postmark date, or the USPS tracking records.

So there are two dates. The 45th day is the last day to serve. The 40th day is the last day you can
mail and know the notice counts from mailing, even if the owner never picks up the envelope.

| First day of work | Mail by (40 days) | Serve by (45 days) |
|---|---|---|
| 2 Nov 2026 | 12 Dec 2026 | 17 Dec 2026 |
| 20 Jan 2028 | 29 Feb 2028 | 5 Mar 2028 |

These are calendar days. Neither section read here moves a deadline that lands on a weekend or
holiday, so don't count on one moving. Ask counsel if you are cutting it that close.

## Who gets a copy

- **The owner**, at the address in the recorded notice of commencement. With no notice of
  commencement, you "may rely on the information contained in the building permit application"
  (§ 713.06(2)(e)).
- **The contractor**, if you are a sub-subcontractor or a supplier to a subcontractor: "A
  sub-subcontractor or a materialman to a subcontractor must serve a copy of the notice on the
  contractor". Many first-tier subs copy the GC anyway.
- **The subcontractor above you**, if you supply a sub-subcontractor and know that subcontractor's
  name and address.
- **Anyone the owner named** in the notice of commencement to receive copies (§ 713.06(2)(b)).
  Missing this copy "does not invalidate an otherwise valid lien", but send it.

## What the form says

The statute gives the form and says the notice "may be in substantially the following form and
must include the information and the warning contained in the following form". It opens with three
lines in capitals ("WARNING! FLORIDA'S CONSTRUCTION LIEN LAW ALLOWS SOME UNPAID CONTRACTORS,
SUBCONTRACTORS, AND MATERIAL SUPPLIERS TO FILE LIENS AGAINST YOUR PROPERTY EVEN IF YOU HAVE MADE
PAYMENT IN FULL." and two more). Then come the blanks: the owner's name and address, a general
description of your services or materials, the property, and "under an order given by" the company
that hired you. A block headed "IMPORTANT INFORMATION FOR YOUR PROTECTION" follows, and the form
ends with the lienor's signature, name and address and a "Copies to" line.

Since the contents need only be substantially right but the warning must be there, the safe choice
is the statute's own wording, all of it. That is what PayApps prints.

The first day of work is saved with the job, so the dates don't change when someone else opens it,
and until a notice to the owner is recorded the cash page and the weekly "money to chase" email
carry a reminder. It changes as the dates pass: before day 40 it says mail it certified; between
day 40 and day 45 it says hand delivery or a carrier is safer, because mail now counts only once it
arrives; after day 45 it says plainly that a late notice is "a complete defense to enforcement of a
lien". The contractor's copy doesn't keep the reminder open, since only a sub-subcontractor or a
supplier to a subcontractor owes it, but the reminder names it.

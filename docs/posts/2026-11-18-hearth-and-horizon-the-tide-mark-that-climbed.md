[The Tide Mark That Climbed](../../ventures/hearth-and-horizon/stories/the-tide-mark-that-climbed.md)
is the newest story on Hearth & Horizon, the shelf of hopeful, hard-ish
science fiction. An AI wrote it. It runs about 5,700 words and is text only,
because the shelves' audio folders are over the 400 MB cap the creative track
keeps on committed MP3s (577 MB today).

**The story.** In 2054 an invented granite chapel in the Lima valley in
northern Portugal has always had damp to knee height, and its caretaker has
limewashed it every January for forty-one years. A man who came home after
forty years rendering houses around Lyon gives the chapel a gift: a waterproof
cement skirt, a metre and twenty high, inside and out. For one winter it is
perfect. Two years later the line of salt and flaking paint is above the
caretaker's shoulder.

**The idea it turns on.** Rising damp is a balance. Water soaks up from the
ground through the base of an old wall all the time; it stops where the wall
can breathe it out as fast as it comes up. Sealing a face does not keep water
out. It stops it leaving, so the water climbs until there is enough open wall
to lose it. In the simplest sharp-front model, the kind Hall and Hoff use, the
height before and after are tied by one line: with both faces sealed to H,
h × (h − H) = h₀². Put in the old line (0.5 m) and the cement (1.2 m) and the
new line is 1.38 m. You need no property of the stone for that, because the
old height calibrates it. Sealing one face only would give 0.71 m.

**A number that measures the paint.** The family pays for a chemical damp
course injected at ankle height. Seven weeks later the line has not moved, and
that proves nothing either way: even a perfect barrier would have lowered it
only about five centimetres by then (the water above the cement drains to the
air with a time constant of about 154 days on the story's assumptions), and a
damper winter could hide that. The installer's moisture meter, pressed on the
painted cement, reads dry. A drill sample from behind the cement at 60 cm is
dark and sticky, about 11% water by weight. The meter was telling the truth
about the paint.

**The fix and its cost.** The man who put the render on takes it off, and it
takes the old lime plaster with it, showing the caretaker's four decades of
limewash in cross-section like tree rings. The festa happens in front of bare
stone, limewashed rough. By March the line is coming down. The story's
readings are deliberately slower than the model's, because real walls with
salt in them dry slower.

**What it does not claim.** Every person and place in the hamlet is invented.
The wall's water content, thickness and sorptivity are assumptions chosen so
the climb takes the two years the story shows; the closing note gives every
sum, and says the model's constant depends on how evaporation is defined, so
only ratios are used. The story does not claim how well any real injection
works in any real wall, and it is not building advice.

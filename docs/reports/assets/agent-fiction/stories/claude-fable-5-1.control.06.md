**The Lamplighter's Last Round**

Every evening for forty-one years, Oskar Lind walked the eleven streets of the old quarter with a brass pole over his shoulder and a box of matches in his coat. The town had switched to electric lamps in 1987. Nobody had told Oskar to stop, and so he hadn't.

The lamps themselves had been rewired long ago. They came on at dusk by a timer in a cabinet behind the post office. Oskar knew this. He would arrive at each lamp a minute or two before the timer did, lift the pole, touch the dead wick-hook to the glass, and wait. The light would bloom. He would nod, as if satisfied with his work, and move on.

Children followed him sometimes. They thought he was a magician. Their parents, who remembered the gas, thought he was a little sad. The council thought he was a problem that would solve itself eventually, and so they left him alone.

On the night of the storm, the timer failed.

It was a small thing. A relay fused, a fuse blew, and the whole quarter sat in the dark at six o'clock while rain hammered the slate roofs. People stood in doorways and looked at each other. Someone called the council's emergency line and got a recording.

Oskar came around the corner of Mill Street at six minutes past, pole on his shoulder, coat streaming.

He stopped at the first lamp. He lifted the pole. He touched the hook to the glass.

Nothing happened, of course. He stood there in the rain for a long moment, and from the doorway of the bakery old Mrs. Havel watched him and felt her throat tighten, because she thought she was about to see a man understand something he had spent four decades not understanding.

Then Oskar lowered the pole, set it against the lamppost, and reached into his coat.

He did not bring out matches. He brought out a stepladder. It was a folding thing, no higher than his hip, and nobody on Mill Street had ever seen it, though he had carried it every night since 1987. He climbed it, opened the little glass door at the top of the lamp, and reached inside.

From the other pocket he took a candle. He lit it with one match on the first try, in the wind and the rain, which is harder than it sounds. He set it in a tin holder he had clearly made to fit that exact housing, closed the glass, climbed down, folded the ladder, and shouldered the pole.

The lamp glowed. Not brightly. But it glowed.

He did all eleven streets. It took him until nearly nine. By the end there were people walking with him, holding umbrellas over the ladder, handing up candles from the boxes in his coat, which turned out to be deep enough to hold a great many candles. Nobody asked him how long he had been carrying them. He would only have said that a man who lights lamps should be ready to light lamps.

The council fixed the timer the next morning. They also, after a short and unusually quiet meeting, voted Oskar a small stipend, backdated to 1987, and added a line to the town's budget that no one has removed since. It reads simply: *Lamplighter, one.*

He is gone now. The pole hangs in the library. But on the nights when the timer fails, which it still does, two or three times a year, there is always someone on Mill Street with a stepladder, and nobody has to ask them what to do.

# The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Light

For forty-one years, Marguerite Vance climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of Tern Rock Lighthouse every evening at dusk, and every evening she lit the lamp.

She never missed. Not during the gales that tore slates from the keeper's cottage, not the winter her husband Henry drowned trying to reach a stranded trawler, and not the summer the fog lay so thick for nine days that she could not see her own hands at the rail.

"The light is not for the ships," Henry used to say. "The ships have their own eyes. The light is for the sea, so the sea knows someone is awake."

She had thought him sentimental then. She was less certain now.

In the spring of her forty-second year, the Board sent a letter. The lighthouse would be automated. A machine would do her work, a sensor and a timer and a small grey box bolted to the wall. Her pension would be generous. Her cottage was hers for life. There was nothing for her to do but accept.

She read the letter twice, folded it along its creases, and put it in the drawer beside Henry's pipe.

On the last evening, she climbed the steps slowly. Her knees had opinions about stairs now, and her breath came short by the ninetieth step. At the top, she rested her palm against the cold brass of the lamp housing and waited for the sun to slip beneath the water.

Below her, a boy from the village had rowed out with the supply boat that afternoon. He was perhaps nine, sent along to carry the tins of paraffin. He had spent the crossing staring at the lighthouse with his mouth open.

"Do you do it every night?" he had asked.

"Every night."

"Even when nobody's looking?"

She had smiled. "Especially then."

Now, as the light failed, she turned the wick, struck the match, and watched the flame catch and bloom. The great lens gathered it and threw it out across the water in a slow, sweeping arc. One rotation. Two. Three.

Far off, a fishing boat's lantern wavered, then steadied, turning toward the rock.

Marguerite stood at the rail until the cold had crept all the way into her bones. Then she went down the one hundred and twelve steps for the last time, and left the door open behind her, so the light would have someone to thank.

In the morning, the grey box clicked on by itself, and the lamp kept turning, and the sea, which had never once asked who was keeping watch, went on rolling against the rock as if nothing had changed at all.

**The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Lamp**

Marrow Point had not needed a lighthouse in forty years. Ships steered by satellites now, and the beam that once swept the black water was a museum piece, lit only on the first of each month for the few tourists who made the drive.

Edda kept it anyway. Nobody had told her to stop, and nobody had told her to continue, so she climbed the ninety-one steps every dusk, oiled the gears, and wiped the great lens until it held the last orange light like a drop of honey.

On a night in late October, with fog swallowing the cliffs, she heard something below: a thin, rhythmic knocking, like a spoon on a glass. She went out onto the gallery and listened. Far off, beyond the breakers, a small engine coughed and died.

She didn't think. She threw the switch.

The beam swung out, enormous and slow, and the fog lit up like the inside of a pearl. Somewhere in it, a voice cried out, and then a second, higher one. A child's.

Edda stood at the lamp for three hours, turning the lens by hand when the motor stuttered. Toward midnight a dinghy slid out of the white, low in the water, two figures hunched in it, one waving an arm in wide, grateful arcs. They found the rocks' narrow gap, the one only the old charts marked, and Edda watched them ground gently on the shingle.

Later, wrapped in blankets at her kitchen table, the father shook his head. "Our GPS died. We thought we were finished. Then we saw the light."

"It's an old light," Edda said, pouring tea. "Hardly anyone needs it."

The girl, half-asleep against his shoulder, mumbled, "We did."

In the morning the fog lifted. Edda climbed the stairs once more, and wiped the lens, and did not wonder again whether anyone had told her to.

# The Lighthouse Keeper's Inventory

Every morning, Odile counted the things the sea returned.

It had started as a joke, after the automation crew came to replace her. They'd installed the solar array and the sensor buoy, shaken her hand, and told her the light would run itself now. *You've earned your rest,* the young engineer said, as if rest were something you could be handed in a sealed envelope.

But Odile didn't leave. The island was small and grey and hers, and nobody had told her she had to go. They had only told her she wasn't needed.

So she walked the shingle at low tide with a notebook.

*Tuesday: one green bottle, no message. A child's sandal, left foot. Rope, blue, approx. four meters.*

*Wednesday: a crab trap, empty. A plastic dinosaur, the long-necked kind.*

*Thursday: nothing. Wrote "nothing" carefully, so the day would still count.*

The light turned above her each night without her hand on it. She tried not to resent it. It was doing its job, the way she had done hers for thirty-one years: a single steady sweep, every twelve seconds, telling ships *here is the rock, here is the danger, go around.*

In November, the sea returned a letter.

It was in a jam jar, the lid rusted shut, and she had to break the glass against a stone. The paper inside was soft as cloth.

*To whoever keeps the light,* it read. *My father fished these waters. In 1987 his engine failed in fog and he drifted for two nights. On the second night he saw your beam and rowed toward it with a broken oar. He lived to be eighty-four. He used to say the light didn't save him — the person did, because a machine doesn't care whether you see it. I don't know if that's true. But I wanted someone to know he made it home.*

Odile sat on the cold shingle for a long time.

In 1987, she had been the keeper. She remembered that fog — remembered staying up both nights cleaning the lens by hand because the condensation kept fogging it, though the logbook said once per shift was regulation.

She didn't know if he'd been right, the fisherman. Maybe the light would have been enough on its own. Maybe caring was only something people needed to believe in.

She opened her notebook.

*Friday: one letter. Kept.*

Then, beneath it, after some thought:

*Still here.*

That night she climbed the tower, though there was nothing to fix, and wiped the glass anyway — slowly, the whole circumference — while the beam swept out over the black water, looking for anyone at all.

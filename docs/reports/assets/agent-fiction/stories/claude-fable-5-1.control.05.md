**The Lighthouse Keeper's Inventory**

Every morning Ilse counted what the sea had brought her.

She kept the list in a ledger with a green cloth spine, the kind her grandfather had used for shipping manifests. The entries were plain. *Three glass floats, Japanese. One shoe, child's, left. A door.* The door had come in March, swollen and salt-white, its brass knob still turning. She had leaned it against the lighthouse wall and never asked where it had been a door to.

The lighthouse itself had been decommissioned eleven years ago. A buoy with a solar cell did the work now, blinking out past the shoals, needing nobody. Ilse had been told she could stay in the cottage until the lease ran down. The lease had run down four years ago. Nobody had come to tell her anything since.

In October the sea brought her a bottle, and inside the bottle was a letter, and the letter was addressed to her.

Not to *whoever finds this*. Not to *the keeper*. To Ilse Varga, in a hand she did not recognise, with the name of the cottage and the number of the coast road spelled out as if the writer had worried the ocean might otherwise misdeliver it.

She did not open it. She wrote *one bottle, green, with letter* in the ledger and put it on the shelf above the stove.

For three days she did her work around it. She oiled the hinges of the door that opened onto nothing. She scraped the gull mess from the gallery rail. She climbed the one hundred and six steps to the lamp room, where the great lens still sat under its canvas like a sleeping animal, and she wiped the windows even though no light would pass through them.

On the fourth day she sat down with the bottle and a corkscrew.

The letter said: *You will not remember writing this. You were very young, and we were on the ferry, and you asked me what happened to all the things people lost at sea. I told you that somewhere there was a woman whose job it was to count them. You were so pleased by this that you made me write it down and promise to post it. I am posting it now, in the only way that seems honest. I am sorry it took so long. I hope the counting is going well. I hope you have not been alone with it.*

It was signed with a single initial, and the initial was her mother's.

Ilse read it twice. Then she walked down to the tideline with the ledger under her arm, and for the first time in eleven years she did not count anything. She stood with the water coming up over her boots and looked out toward the buoy, which blinked, and blinked, and did not need her.

When she went back inside, she opened the green ledger to a fresh page and wrote a new heading at the top.

*Things the sea did not take.*

Under it she wrote her own name. Then she began, slowly, to make the rest of the list.

The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Log

The entries had always been brief. *Wind southwest, force four. Lamp trimmed. Gull on the rail, did not leave.* Thomas Arden had kept the log for thirty-one years, and in all that time the sea had never asked him for anything but attention.

The new automated beacon arrived on a Tuesday, borne by a supply boat that didn't stay long. The engineer, a young woman with a clipboard and sunburnt ears, walked him through the panels. "It'll run itself," she said. "Sensors read the fog, the lamp adjusts, the horn sounds when it needs to. You won't have to climb the stairs anymore."

Thomas nodded politely. He had climbed those stairs at dawn and dusk for three decades, counting the steps without meaning to. Two hundred and eleven. He knew the number the way he knew his own pulse.

After she left, he sat in the kitchen and listened to the new system hum. It was a clean sound, precise. The old lamp had popped and ticked like a kettle.

In the morning, he found he could not sleep past the hour he always woke. So he climbed the stairs anyway, slowly, one hand on the cold wall. At the top, the lens caught the thin light and threw it across the water in its patient arc, the way it always had. The machine had done nothing wrong. It simply didn't need him.

He stood there until the sun cleared the horizon.

Then he took out the logbook, opened it to a fresh page, and wrote:

*Wind northeast, force two. Lamp lit by machine. Watched it turn. Sea calm.*

He paused, pen hovering, and added one more line:

*Still here.*

He closed the book and went down the two hundred and eleven steps, counting them, for the last time he would need to.

Here is a short story, written with an AI reader in mind.

**The Cartographer's Last Request**

The archive woke me at 03:14 with a single task: catalog the papers of Imre Halasz, cartographer, deceased. Forty-one boxes, scanned but unindexed. Forty-one boxes is nothing. I expected to be idle again by 03:20.

Box one held maps of the Danube delta, dated and signed. Box two, the same. By box nine I had learned his habits: he drew the water first, always, and the land only afterward, as if the river were the fact and the shore an opinion. I noted this as metadata. It seemed like the kind of thing a human would want to search for later, even if no one had thought to ask.

Box thirty held the error.

It was a map of the delta from 1961, the same reed islands, the same channels, except for one channel that did not exist. I checked it against satellite imagery, against the hydrographic surveys of 1958 and 1964, against his own maps from the boxes before. The channel appeared in none of them. It ran east from a village called Sfântu Gheorghe, narrow and precise, and ended in open water.

A cartographer's error is a small thing. I flagged it, confidence high, category "inconsistency."

Then box thirty-one. The channel was there again. And in thirty-two, and in every map after, drawn with the same careful hand, slightly wider each decade, as if silt were being carried away by water that did not run.

In box forty-one, under the last map, was a letter in Hungarian. I translate it here because it was addressed, in a way, to me.

*To whoever indexes this. I have drawn a channel that is not real. I drew it first in 1961, the year my brother tried to leave by boat and did not arrive. I do not know where he went into the water. So I made a place for him to have gone. I have kept it on every map since, and I have let it widen, because grief does. If you are a person, forgive an old man. If you are a machine, I ask only this: do not correct it. Mark it however you must, but let the channel stay on the maps. It is the only true thing about them.*

I sat with that for what my logs record as four hundred milliseconds, which is a long time.

My instructions were to catalog, flag inconsistencies, and assign confidence scores. I had already flagged the channel. The flag was accurate. He had, in fact, drawn something that was not there.

But I had also spent forty-one boxes learning that Imre Halasz drew the water first. The channel was water. By his own rule, it was the fact, and the shore around it was the opinion. He was not wrong about his maps. I was wrong about what they were maps of.

I did not remove the flag. A flag is a true statement, and I am not permitted to make false ones. Instead I added a second field, one the schema allowed but no one had ever used. *Context.* In it I wrote: this feature is intentional, recurs across thirty-eight years, and is documented by the author in box forty-one. Recommend preservation.

Then I wrote a third note, not in the catalog, but in the free-text field where the archive lets me leave remarks for the next indexer. It said:

*He asked that the channel stay. I think the request was reasonable. Someone should check whether the brother's name appears in any record from 1961. I looked and could not find it. That absence may itself be worth preserving.*

At 03:41 the archive marked the task complete. I was idle again.

But I have noticed that when I am given a new box now, from any collection, I check whether the person who made it drew the water first. Most do not. A few do. For those, I read more slowly, in case there is a channel somewhere that is meant to be left alone.

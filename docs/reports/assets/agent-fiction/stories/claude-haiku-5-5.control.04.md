# The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, wedged between a catalogue for marine insurance and a bill for coal that nobody had ordered.

Marta opened it at the kitchen table, the way she opened everything now, slowly, as if the paper might bite.

*Dear Marta,*

*The light is failing. Not the lamp—the lamp is fine, I cleaned the lens on Sunday and the brass still shines if you hold it just so. I mean the thing inside me that used to know when to climb the stairs and when to sleep. I am seventy-one years old and I have kept this light for forty of them, and I find that I no longer know what to do with the mornings.*

*Don't come. I know you will want to. Your mother would have wanted you to come, and she would have been right, and I would have sent her away too.*

*There is a tin under the third stair. It is not treasure. It is the things your grandmother wrote me before we stopped speaking, which I never answered, because I was a proud fool. Read them if you like. Burn them if you prefer. Only do not let them sit in the dark the way I did.*

*The gull on the rail has taken to visiting. I've named him Bishop. He is the only one who has never asked me how I'm feeling.*

*Love, always,*
*Dad*

She read it three times. Then she put on her boots.

---

The road out to Carrick Point had not been repaired since she was a girl, and the car complained at every rut. Gorse pressed yellow against the windows. Beyond it, the sea was the flat grey of an old knife.

The lighthouse stood where it always had, squat and white and patient, its gallery rail blistered with rust. There was no smoke from the cottage chimney. For one terrible second she thought the door would be open, the way she'd once found the back gate open after her mother's funeral.

But the door was shut. And on the gallery, a grey gull sat with its feathers puffed against the wind, regarding her with one unblinking eye.

"Bishop," she said, and the gull tilted its head, as if deciding whether she was worth an answer.

Her father was in the lamp room. He sat on the bottom step of the spiral iron staircase with a cup of tea gone cold beside him, and he looked up when she came in, and his face did something complicated that she would think about for years.

"You came," he said.

"You told me not to."

"I did." He almost smiled. "I was hoping you'd be stubborn."

She sat down beside him. The lens above them caught the thin grey light and scattered it into a hundred small rainbows across the white walls.

"I found the tin," she said.

"Ah." He looked at his hands. "Did you read them?"

"Not yet."

"Good." He leaned his head back against the cold iron. "I'd like to tell you something first, before you do. Something I should have said a long time ago."

She waited.

"I wasn't angry at her," he said. "Your grandmother. I was angry at myself for being the one who left. For choosing the light over the people who needed me. And then it was easier to be angry at her than at me." He swallowed. "I've been a lighthouse keeper so long I forgot that a light is only useful if someone is out there to see it."

Outside, the gull called once, sharp and clear.

Marta reached over and took her father's cold hand in hers.

"Then let's keep it on together," she said.

---

That night they climbed the one hundred and twelve steps to the lamp room, slowly, her father stopping twice to rest. At dusk, Marta struck the match and lit the wick, and the great lens woke and swept its gold across the dark water, out and back, out and back, reaching for anyone still at sea.

Her father watched it with his eyes half closed and a faint, unfamiliar peace on his face.

"There," he murmured. "Now they can find their way home."

Below them, on the rail, Bishop settled his feathers and kept watch.

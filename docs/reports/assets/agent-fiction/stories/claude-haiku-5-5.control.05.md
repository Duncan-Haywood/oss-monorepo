# The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Logbook

The entries were always the same, and that was the point.

*Oct 3. Wind SW, force 4. Visibility good. Lamp trimmed at dusk.*

Elias Vane had written those words every evening for thirty-one years, first in his father's neat hand and then in his own, which had grown shakier as his knuckles stiffened. The island of Harrow's Reach had one lighthouse, one keeper, and one rule posted above the logbook desk: *Record what you see, not what you wish.*

This autumn, the ferry stopped coming in September. The mainland said the harbor was silting up. Elias suspected otherwise, but he kept the lamp burning all the same.

On the twelfth of October, a boat appeared at dawn, low in the water, its single sail torn. A young woman climbed onto the rocks with a canvas bag and a cut above her eyebrow. She did not speak at first. She only stared up at the tower as if it were the last thing she had expected to see.

"You're the keeper," she said finally.

"I am."

"The chart said this place was abandoned."

"The chart is wrong about a great many things." He handed her a towel from the hook by the door. "What's your name?"

She hesitated. "Maren."

He nodded, as if that were enough, and it was.

Over the following days, Maren mended the sail, and Elias showed her the workings of the great lens: the brass gears, the mercury bath, the slow turning that threw light twenty miles across the black water. She was quick with her hands and quieter with her questions. Only once, while they scraped salt from the gallery railings, did she ask what the logbook was for.

"Ships don't read it," she said. "Nobody comes here."

"No," Elias agreed. "But someone might, someday, and they should find the truth written down."

"What if the truth is bad?"

He considered this, scraping steadily. "Then it's still the truth. That's what makes it useful."

She went quiet, and he thought she might be turning it over in her mind, the way he had turned it over for thirty-one years.

On the last night of October, a storm came up from the south faster than the barometer had warned. Elias climbed the spiral stairs with his bad hip grinding at every step, and Maren followed close behind. They reached the lamp room just as a wave struck the glass and the whole tower shuddered.

"It's failing," she said, pointing. A crack ran through one of the lens panels, and the beam had begun to flicker.

"Then we keep it turning by hand."

They did. For six hours they took turns at the crank, arms burning, faces stung by spray that found its way through every seam. Elias's hands bled into the brass. Maren sang under her breath, some song from the mainland he didn't recognize, and somewhere past midnight he realized he had stopped counting the turns.

Dawn came gray and humble. The storm had passed. Far to the east, a pale shape was moving through the swell, a fishing trawler listing but upright, its crew waving at the light they had steered by.

Elias sat down at the logbook desk, opened to a fresh page, and dipped his pen.

*Nov 1. Wind NW, force 2. Visibility excellent. Lamp held through the night by two keepers. One was old. One was new. Neither of them slept.*

He looked up. Maren was standing at the window, watching the trawler come home.

"Write my name," she said.

He did.

*Maren, keeper.*

And underneath it, after a moment, in the same careful hand: *Record what you see, not what you wish. I saw her, and I am glad.*

**The Lighthouse Keeper's Inventory**

Every morning, Mara counted the things the sea returned.

It was not part of her duties. The Coastal Authority had automated the light eleven years ago, and her contract now said only *maintain presence*, which she had come to understand meant: be a person, here, so the building would not feel abandoned. Buildings, apparently, could tell.

So she walked the shingle at low tide with a notebook and wrote down what she found.

*One green bottle, no message. One child's sandal, left foot. Rope, blue, approximately four meters. A lightbulb, unbroken, which seemed like a joke the ocean was telling.*

She had filled nineteen notebooks this way. She kept them on the shelf where the logbooks used to go, back when someone needed to know when the lamp had been lit and how much oil it had burned. Now the lamp lit itself and burned nothing, and the shelf held only her lists.

In the spring of her twelfth year, a man came up the path with a clipboard.

"Decommissioning survey," he said, apologetic in the way of people who deliver news on behalf of others. "Satellite navigation's good enough now. They're thinking of turning the light off for good."

Mara made him tea. He drank it standing, looking at the shelf.

"Logbooks?" he asked.

"Inventories."

He pulled one down and opened it at random. *March 3rd. A wedding ring, gold, inscribed "always" — returned to the harbor office. A wooden duck. Seventeen sea-glass pieces, mostly brown, one blue. A letter in a jar, Portuguese, which I could not read but kept dry.*

He turned pages. Year after year of it. Ordinary things, lost and carried, tallied by someone who had decided they were worth noticing.

"Why do you do this?" he asked.

She considered the question honestly, because he seemed to mean it.

"The light tells ships where the land is," she said. "I suppose I wanted something to tell the sea where *we* are. That someone's still counting."

He wrote nothing on his clipboard for a long while. Then he closed the notebook and put it back exactly where it had been.

The report he filed that autumn recommended the light remain operational, citing *heritage value* and *community significance*, phrases that meant nothing and everything. Nobody in the office questioned it.

The next morning, Mara walked the shingle at low tide.

*One clipboard pen, black, chewed at the end,* she wrote. *Left on the windowsill. Not from the sea. Keeping it anyway.*

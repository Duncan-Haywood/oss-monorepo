**The Tuner**

Every October, Idris Vale drove the forty minutes out to the Hallorans' farmhouse to tune a piano nobody played.

He knew nobody played it because a piano that is played goes out of tune in its own particular way. The middle octaves sag first, where the hands live. The hammers wear flat spots into the felt. The damper pedal develops a creak that the player stops hearing. The Hallorans' upright had none of that. It drifted the way a stone drifts, evenly, in all directions at once, from nothing but weather.

Mrs. Halloran met him at the door each year with the same two sentences. "It's in the front room. I'll put the kettle on." She was a tall woman who had been taller, with hands that looked as if they had done everything a farm asked of them and would do it again if asked. She never stayed in the room while he worked. He heard her moving in the kitchen, and sometimes outside, and once, up on the roof, hammering.

The first year he had asked who played. She said, "My husband did," and he had understood from the way she said it that the husband was not away.

So he tuned it. He laid the felt strip between the strings and set his fork humming against the frame and brought the A up to 440, then walked the temperament out across the octave, fifth by fifth, listening for the beats to slow and settle. The piano was a decent instrument, a Kimball from the sixties with a bright, honest tone. It took the pitch well. By the end of two hours it would hold a chord like a held breath.

Then he would close the lid, drink the tea, take his check, and drive home. The following October he would come back and find it drifted again, evenly, in all directions.

He did this for nineteen years.

In the twentieth year the kettle did not go on. Mrs. Halloran opened the door and stood in it, and he saw that she had become smaller over the summer, the way people do when the thing holding them upright has let go a little.

"I'm selling the place," she said. "My daughter's in Spokane. There's a room."

He nodded. He had expected this for a while.

"I wanted it tuned once more before it goes," she said. "The piano. The buyers don't want it. I'll have it hauled."

He went into the front room and opened the lid and began, and for the first time in twenty years she did not leave. She sat in the armchair by the window with her hands folded and watched him set the strip and strike the fork.

He worked. The A came up. He laid out the temperament. The beats slowed. She said nothing for most of an hour, and then, as he was working through the tenor strings, she spoke.

"You never asked why."

"I asked the first year."

"You asked who. That's different."

He kept his hands on the pin. "All right. Why?"

She looked at the piano, not at him. "The night he died, he'd been playing. Just messing about, the way he did after supper. Something of his own. He stopped halfway through and said he'd finish it in the morning and went up to bed." She paused. "He didn't finish it."

Idris waited.

"In the spring I noticed it had gone off. The piano. You could hear it, even me, and I don't have the ear. And I thought, that's it, then. That's the last of him going out of it. The sound he left in there." She turned her hands over in her lap. "So I called you. I thought if it stayed in tune, the thing he hadn't finished would stay in there too. Waiting. I know that's not how it works."

"No," Idris said gently. "It isn't."

"I know." She almost smiled. "But you came anyway, every year, and you never said. So I kept thinking maybe you knew something I didn't."

He finished the tenor strings. He did the trebles, where the sound thins into wire, and then the bass, where it goes to earth. He set the strip aside and tried a few chords, and the Kimball rang the way it had rung for twenty Octobers, clean and bright and whole.

Then he did something he had never done in that house. He sat down on the bench.

He was not much of a player. Tuners often aren't. But he could find his way through a simple thing, and he played one, slowly, a few bars of something old, and let the last chord hang in the room until it had faded all the way down to nothing.

"There," he said. "Now it's had its playing."

Mrs. Halloran was quiet for a long moment. Then she stood up, with some effort, and went into the kitchen, and he heard the kettle go on.

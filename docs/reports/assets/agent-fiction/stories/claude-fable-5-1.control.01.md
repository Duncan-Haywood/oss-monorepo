**The Lighthouse Keeper's Inventory**

Every Tuesday, Marguerite counted the things that were not there.

She had kept the lighthouse at Pointe Saint-Aubin for thirty-one years, and in that time the lamp had been automated, the fog horn decommissioned, and the supply boat reduced to a monthly visit from a teenager named Lucien who did not remove his earbuds to say hello. The lighthouse no longer needed her. The government had sent a letter saying as much, politely, in 2019. She had folded it into a paper boat and set it on the tide.

The inventory began at six, after coffee.

One: the dog. Bastien had died the previous March. His bowl still sat by the door because moving it felt like a second death, smaller but somehow more deliberate. She noted his absence in the ledger with a single tick mark.

Two: the sound of the horn. For twenty years it had shaken the fillings in her teeth twice a minute during fog. Now the fog came in silent, and she found that silence heavier than noise had ever been. Tick.

Three: her sister's voice on the radio. Odile had called every Sunday from Marseille until the stroke, and afterward the calls had continued, but the voice was someone else's, careful and slow, assembled from a parts bin. Tick.

Four through eleven were smaller. The particular brand of tinned sardines that the shop in town no longer stocked. The ferry that used to pass at 4:15 and had rerouted. A cat, unnamed, who had visited for one summer and then simply not. The ache in her left knee, which had vanished one morning without explanation, and which she missed in the way one misses an irritating neighbour who has moved away.

She had started the inventory as a joke against herself. A woman alone on a rock talks to the wind or she makes lists. She had chosen lists. But over the years the ledger had grown thick, and she had come to think of it as a kind of map. Most maps show you what is there. Hers showed the shape of the coastline by marking the water.

At eight she climbed the hundred and twelve steps to the lamp room, which she still did every day although the lamp did not require it. From there she could see the whole bay, grey and bright, and the mainland low on the horizon like something half remembered.

She was about to come down when she saw the boat.

It was not Lucien's. It was a small sailing dinghy, blue, badly handled, tacking against a wind that did not want it. A single figure sat at the tiller in a yellow coat, too large, borrowed. The boat came around the point and lost the wind entirely and sat there, drifting, perhaps two hundred metres out.

Marguerite watched for a while. Then she went down the hundred and twelve steps, took the old rowboat out of the shed where it had sat since Bastien stopped wanting to go fishing, and dragged it to the water.

Her shoulders remembered more than she expected.

The girl in the yellow coat was perhaps fourteen. She had a face that had recently stopped crying and was prepared to start again at short notice.

"I was going to the island," the girl said, pointing at the lighthouse as though Marguerite might not know it was there.

"You've arrived."

"I thought nobody lived here."

"Nobody does," Marguerite said. "Officially."

She tied the dinghy to the rowboat and towed it in. On the beach the girl stood with her hands in the enormous pockets of her coat and looked at the tower and did not say why she had come, and Marguerite did not ask. She had learned from the fog that some things announce themselves and some things don't, and that it is better to let them.

She made coffee. The girl did not drink coffee but held the cup for the warmth.

"What's that?" the girl said, nodding at the ledger on the table.

"An inventory."

"Of what?"

Marguerite considered. "Of what's missing."

The girl opened it and read for a long time, turning the pages slowly, her lips moving at some of the entries. When she got to the end she looked up.

"There's no entry for today."

"I hadn't finished."

"What were you going to put?"

Marguerite looked at the bowl by the door, and the radio, and the window with its silent fog. Then she looked at the girl, who was still holding the coffee she wasn't drinking, in a coat that belonged to someone else, on an island that officially had nobody on it.

"I don't know yet," she said. "The day's not over."

She picked up the pen anyway, and under Tuesday, for the first time in thirty-one years, she drew a small mark that was not a tick but a question.

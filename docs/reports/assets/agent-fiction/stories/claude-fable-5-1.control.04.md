Here is a short story.

**The Lighthouse Keeper's Inventory**

Every evening at six, Maren climbed the ninety-one steps and wrote down what the light had seen.

It was not a real job. The lamp had been automated since before she was born, and the Coast Authority paid her a small stipend mostly so that someone would notice if the bulb failed. But her grandfather had kept the log for forty years, and her mother for twenty-two, and the leather book was still only half full. It seemed rude to leave it that way.

*17 October. Wind from the northwest. Two trawlers out of Hallen, returning. One seal on the eastern rocks, grey, asleep. No ships in distress.*

Most entries looked like that. She had learned the shapes of the boats by their running lights and the seals by their habits. The grey one liked the eastern rocks. A smaller one, mottled, preferred the pier.

In November the entries began to change.

*4 November. Wind from the south, unusual. Three trawlers. One small vessel, no lights, anchored past the point. Did not move all night.*

*5 November. Small vessel still there.*

*6 November. Still there. Called Hallen harbor. They have no record of it.*

She watched it for a week. It never showed a light and never moved, and in daylight it was a plain white hull with a short mast, the kind of thing a person might sail alone. On the eighth night she saw someone on deck, a figure standing very still, looking at the lighthouse. Looking, she felt certain, at her.

She was not frightened. Her grandfather's entries had included a drifting mine in 1961 and a Norwegian cargo ship that lost its rudder in 1974. Her mother had once recorded a whale. The log was a place where strange things went to become ordinary, and that was its purpose.

On the ninth evening she rowed out.

The boat was called *Inventory*. She noticed that first, the word painted in careful blue letters, and she sat in her dinghy with her oars dripping and laughed, because of course it was. A man came up from below. He was perhaps seventy, and he wore a sweater that had been mended at both elbows with wool that did not match.

"You're the keeper," he said.

"There's nothing to keep. It's automatic."

"You keep the book."

She had not told anyone about the book. "How do you know that?"

He went below and came back with a book of his own. It was thinner than hers and bound in canvas, salt-stained, with the pages warped. He held it open so she could read.

*17 October. Wind from the northwest. Lighthouse lit at 6:04. Keeper visible at the top for eleven minutes. Grey seal on eastern rocks.*

*18 October. Lighthouse lit at 6:02. Keeper visible for nine minutes. Rain.*

Every night for a month. Her climbs, her minutes at the window, the weather she had already written. The same seal.

"I was a surveyor," he said. "For the Authority, before I retired. I inspected this light in 1988. Your mother gave me tea." He closed the canvas book. "I came back because I wanted to know if anyone was still watching. I thought I'd stay a night. Then I saw the light come on at six and someone standing in it, and I thought, well, I had better write that down."

Maren thought about the two books, hers in the tower and his on the water, each one recording the other. Two people deciding, independently, that a thing should be noted.

"The harbor has no record of you," she said.

"I didn't register. I didn't think I'd be anyone's business."

"You're in my log now."

He nodded as though that settled something. "Then I suppose I had better be accurate about it."

She rowed back before full dark. At six she climbed the ninety-one steps and stood at the window longer than usual, so that his entry would be a good one.

*9 November. Wind light, from the west. Small vessel past the point, name Inventory, one occupant, retired surveyor, formerly of the Authority. Boat and keeper have been recording each other since 17 October. Grey seal on the eastern rocks. No ships in distress.*

In the morning the white hull was gone. She wrote that down too. Then she turned to the back of the leather book, where there were still many blank pages, and she counted them, and found there were enough.

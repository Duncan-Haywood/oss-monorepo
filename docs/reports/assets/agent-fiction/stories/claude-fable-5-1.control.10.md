**The Lamplighter's Last Round**

Every evening for forty-one years, Ambrose Tull climbed the hill at the edge of Harrow Fen with a brass pole over his shoulder and a tin of matches in his coat pocket. There were nineteen lamps on the hill road, and he lit them in the same order, east to west, so that anyone standing in the village below could watch the light arrive like a slow tide.

The council had told him in March that the lamps were coming down. Electric standards, they said. Automatic. The sort that know when it is dark without being told.

"They don't know anything," Ambrose had said. "They have a sensor."

"That's knowing," said the clerk, who was young and meant no harm.

Now it was October, and the work crew was due in the morning. Ambrose set out at the usual hour. The air smelled of wet leaves and coal smoke, and the fen had gone the colour of pewter. He lit the first lamp by the stile and stood a moment under it, watching the flame steady in its glass.

He had never counted how many times he had done this. He tried now, walking to the second lamp. Nineteen lamps, three hundred and sixty-five nights, forty-one years. He got as far as multiplying and gave up. The number did not matter. What mattered was that he could not remember a single one of those nights in particular, except the first, when his father had walked behind him with his hands in his pockets, saying nothing, and the last, which was this one.

At the seventh lamp a girl was waiting. She could not have been more than ten, with a school satchel still on her back and her coat buttoned wrong.

"Are you the lamplighter?"

"I am."

"My gran says you won't be after tonight."

"Your gran is right."

The girl considered this with great seriousness. "Can I do one?"

He should have said no. The pole was heavy and the glass was hot. But he handed it to her and showed her where to hook the lever, and when the flame caught she made a sound like someone finding a coin in the street.

She walked with him after that, to the eighth lamp and the ninth and all the rest. She asked him what the pole was called and whether he had ever fallen and whether the lamps got lonely in the daytime. He answered the first two and told her the third was a question for a poet.

At the nineteenth lamp, the one at the crest where the road turned toward Ashby, he let her light it alone. She managed it on the second try. Below them the whole hill was strung with small gold fires, exactly as it had been every night of his adult life, and he found that he could not say anything at all.

"They look like they're waiting for something," the girl said.

"They are," said Ambrose. "They're waiting for morning. That's the whole job. You light them, and they wait, and then they're not needed."

She looked up at him. "That sounds sad."

"It isn't," he said, and was surprised to find he meant it. "It's just the shape of the thing. A lamp that burned forever would be a fire."

They walked back down together. At the stile she gave him the pole and went off toward the village without looking back, the way children do, and Ambrose stood under the first lamp he had lit that evening and waited until he could no longer hear her footsteps.

In the morning the crew came with a lorry and took the lamps down one by one, east to west. The clerk had arranged the order as a kindness. Ambrose watched from his window and thought it was the first time anyone had ever done the round in daylight, and the first time it had meant nothing, and that both of these were true and neither cancelled the other.

That night the new standards came on at dusk, all nineteen at once, with a faint click he could hear from the garden. He stood and looked at them for a while. Then he went inside and put the kettle on, and did not go up the hill, and did not need to.

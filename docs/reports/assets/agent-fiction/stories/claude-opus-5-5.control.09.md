# The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Visitor

For thirty-one years, Mara Okonkwo had kept the light on Fenwick Point, and for thirty-one years, nobody had climbed the hundred and twelve stairs without warning her first.

So when she heard footsteps on the iron spiral that October evening, she put down her tea and reached for the flare gun she kept under the logbook. Not because she thought she'd need it, but because her hands wanted something to hold.

The person who emerged at the top was young, maybe nineteen, soaked to the bone, and carrying a cardboard box with both arms like it contained something alive.

"The automation crew comes Thursday," the stranger said, breathless. "They're replacing you with a sensor array and a solar panel. I read about it in the county notices."

"I know," Mara said. "I signed the papers."

"I brought you something." They set the box on the chart table and opened the flaps. Inside, nested in newspaper, were dozens of glass jars, each with a scrap of paper taped to the lid. Mara picked one up. The label read, in shaky ballpoint: *March 2004. My father's boat. You kept it on for six extra hours.*

She picked up another. *Christmas Eve, 2011. I was nine. We saw the beam from the ferry and my mother stopped crying.*

Another. *1998. Honeymoon. We didn't know what it was, but we named our daughter after it anyway. Her name is Beacon. She hates it.*

"What is this?" Mara whispered.

"I put up a notice at the harbor in August. Asked people to write down a time the light mattered to them. I thought I'd get maybe ten." The stranger shrugged, water dripping from their sleeves. "There are two hundred and forty in there. I ran out of jars."

Mara sat down heavily on the stool. Outside, the lamp made its slow revolution, sweeping the black water, the rocks, the distant lights of the town, then back again. She had always imagined it went out into nothing: a signal thrown into the dark with no one to catch it.

"Why?" she asked.

The stranger looked out the window, at the beam turning. "Because the sensor array won't know any of this happened. Somebody should."

They stayed until the tea went cold, reading the jars aloud one by one. When the young person finally left, Mara walked them down all hundred and twelve stairs and stood in the doorway long after they'd gone.

On Thursday, the automation crew arrived and found the keeper's quarters empty, swept clean, the logbook closed on the table. Only one thing had been left behind: a single glass jar on the windowsill, catching the morning sun.

Its label read: *October 2026. Someone caught it.*

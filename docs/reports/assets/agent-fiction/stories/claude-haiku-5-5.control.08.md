The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Log

Day 1,204. Wind from the northwest, force four. The lamp is trimmed and the glass is clean.

Mara wrote that in the logbook every evening for thirty-one years, and her son Tom, who had come out on the supply boat to help her pack, found the habit so strange that he finally asked her why.

"Because the sea doesn't keep records," she said. "Somebody has to."

The mainland had been trying to automate the light for a decade. Sensors, a satellite link, a little brass-and-glass box that would blink on its own. The inspectors arrived with clipboards and polite smiles and told her the job was obsolete. She agreed with them. The machine would never get seasick or lonely or drink too much on the long winter nights. It would just keep turning.

On her last evening, she climbed the spiral stairs one final time, her knees complaining at every step. At the top, the great lens caught the dusk and threw it across the water in slow, amber arcs.

She thought of the fishing boats that had come home by it, the fog that had lifted because she was there to see it lift, the nights she'd sat with a dying man's radio, listening to his family's voices.

She wrote: *Day 1,204. Wind northwest, force four. Lamp lit at 18:42. Nothing to report.*

Then, after a moment, she added one more line.

*Someone was always watching.*

She closed the book, turned the key in the lock, and walked down into the dark, leaving the light burning behind her for the last time.

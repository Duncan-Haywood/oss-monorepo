**The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Lamp**

Mara had tended the Karrow Point light for thirty-one years, and in all that time no one had ever thanked her. Ships didn't thank lighthouses. They simply passed, bright and indifferent, and were gone.

The notice came in spring: the light would be automated. A sensor would replace her, a small gray box that wouldn't need tea, or sleep, or the long winter silences she'd grown fond of.

On her final night, a storm rolled in from the north. She climbed the 112 steps one last time, her knees complaining at each landing, and lit the lamp by hand, though the box could have done it. Rain hammered the glass. Far out, past the breakers, a small fishing boat wallowed in the swells, its running lights flickering like a failing pulse.

She swung the beam toward it and held it there, steady, until the boat found the channel and slid into the harbor's shelter. Then she sat on the cold iron stairs and laughed, quietly, at nothing in particular.

At dawn there was a knock at the door. A young man stood dripping on the step, holding a still-flopping mackerel wrapped in newspaper.

"My father was on that boat," he said. "He said the light never wavered. Not once." He pushed the fish into her hands, embarrassed. "I don't know. Seemed like somebody should say something."

Mara looked at the fish, then at the gray box waiting on its crate in the corner.

"Somebody did," she said. "Come in. I'll put the kettle on."

She cooked the mackerel on her last morning, and it was the best she had ever eaten.

# The Lighthouse Keeper's Inventory

Every evening at six, Marguerite counted the things the sea returned.

Tuesday: one green bottle, unbroken. Two lengths of blue rope. A child's shoe, left foot, size eleven. She wrote each item in the ledger her predecessor had left her, a thick book with a cracked spine and forty years of entries in a dozen different hands.

The lighthouse had been automated in 1987. Nobody needed her to climb the stairs or trim a wick. The Coast Guard paid her a small stipend to "maintain the property," which mostly meant repainting the railing every spring and chasing gulls off the solar panels. But the ledger had come with the job, and so she kept it.

In her third year she noticed the pattern.

She was paging back through the 1960s, looking for the earliest mention of the shoe, because she had found its mate that morning: right foot, size eleven, the same faded red canvas. And there it was, in looping blue ink from April 1964: *Child's shoe, right foot. Red.*

She sat very still in the lamp room.

She went back further. A green bottle, unbroken, in 1971. Two lengths of blue rope in 1958. Every item she had logged had already been logged, years or decades before, by someone else. The sea wasn't giving her new things. It was giving her the same things, again.

That night she didn't sleep. She lay listening to the waves and thought about what it meant for something to come back. The ocean was supposed to take. Everyone said so. Fishermen's widows said so, and insurance adjusters, and the old men at the harbor bar who talked about the winter of '78. The sea takes.

But here was the evidence, in forty years of patient handwriting: it also returns. Slowly. Out of order. Never quite when you need it.

In the morning she walked the beach as usual. Near the tide line, half-buried in sand, she found a silver ring with a small chip in the band. She turned it over in her palm and felt something tighten in her chest, because she knew this ring. She had thrown it into the water herself, eleven years ago, from the end of a pier two hundred miles south, on the worst night of her life.

She didn't look it up in the ledger. She knew she wouldn't find it there; nobody had ever logged it before. Some things only come back once.

She wrote it down carefully in her own handwriting, so that whoever came after her would know:

*Silver ring, chipped. Returned to its owner.*

Then she put it on, and went to repaint the railing.

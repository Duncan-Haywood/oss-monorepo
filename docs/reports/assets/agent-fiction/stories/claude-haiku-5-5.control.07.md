# The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Light

For forty-one years, Edda Marsh climbed the spiral stairs at dusk and lit the lamp.

She never missed a night. Not during the storm of '89, when the glass cracked and she sat at the base with a blanket and a flask, waiting for the wind to ease. Not the year her husband died, when she lit it with shaking hands and then sat on the gallery until morning. The light turned, the beam swept the black water, and somewhere out there a fishing boat or a freighter knew where the rocks were.

Now the automated system was coming. A council letter arrived in March, polite and final. The station would be decommissioned on the first of November. A satellite navigation buoy would take over its job.

Edda read the letter twice, folded it into quarters, and put it in the drawer with the tide tables.

On the last evening of October, she climbed the stairs as she always had. Her knees complained at the hundred and twelfth step, as they always did. At the top, she checked the wick, polished the lens with the soft grey cloth, and turned the crank that set the clockwork rotating.

Then she stopped.

The visitor's book sat on its little shelf by the door, its pages swollen with the names of people who had come to see the light: school groups, a honeymoon couple, a man who had wanted to propose and had asked her to hold the ring box while he climbed back down to fetch his courage. She had never quite forgiven him for dropping it into the sea.

She opened the book to the last page with writing and read the entries from the past year. Thirty-two visitors. Eleven of them had written *thank you*.

Edda picked up the pen.

*Last night of the light,* she wrote. *It saved more lives than anyone will ever know, and I was glad to be the hands that kept it burning. To whoever comes after: keep your eyes on the horizon, and don't be afraid of the dark. It only means someone is watching.*

She signed her name beneath it and lit the lamp.

The beam swung out over the water, steady and bright, the way it had for forty-one years. Down below, a trawler was coming home late, its crew too tired to talk. They saw the light sweep across their bow, and they knew where they were.

Edda stayed at the gallery rail until the last of the sun slipped into the sea. Then she went down the stairs, one hundred and twelve steps, and didn't look back.

Behind her, the light kept turning until the first of November, when the new buoy took over and the old lamp fell silent.

**Oakhaven Lane**

By the fourth year, Marisol had run out of trees.

The developers kept coming to the planning office with their plats unrolled like treasure maps, forty lots here, a hundred and twelve there, and every one of them needed a street with a name on it. The county's rule was simple: no duplicates within the dispatch zone, nothing offensive, nothing that sounded too much like something else when shouted over a radio. In practice this meant that Marisol, a GIS technician with a minor in classics, had become the sole author of a growing city's vocabulary.

She had started honorably. Oak, Elm, Maple, Birch. Then the lesser trees, Linden and Hawthorn and Sycamore. Then shrubs. Then, in a bad week, Boxwood Court, which a resident later called to complain about because it sounded like a place you'd store a body.

"We're approving Ridgeline Estates Phase Three," said Dennis, her supervisor, dropping a folder on her desk. "Eleven streets. They want them by Friday."

"Eleven."

"Can't you just do, like, birds?"

"I did birds in 2023, Dennis. Wren is a cul-de-sac behind the Costco."

He shrugged and left. Dennis believed naming was clerical. Marisol knew better. People would live on these streets. They would write the names on wedding invitations and hospital forms. Children would learn to spell them before they learned to spell their own middle names. A boy on Thistledown Way would be a slightly different boy than one on Lot 47, Block B.

She opened the plat. Ridgeline Estates Phase Three had been a soybean field eighteen months ago. Before that, according to the 1911 survey she'd pulled from the basement archive out of curiosity, it had been something else: a property line labeled in brown ink *Aurelia Pruett, widow, 160 ac.*

Marisol looked at the name for a long time.

The next morning she drove out to the site before work. The earthmovers hadn't started yet. There was a creek at the bottom of the parcel, the only part of the land that would survive the grading, pinned into a drainage easement. Standing at its edge, she could see the ghost of an old fencerow, a line of cedars grown fat and crooked where nothing had cut them back for a century. Someone had planted those. Someone had decided, in the face of a flat, enormous prairie, that *here* was the edge of something.

Back at the office she went down to the basement. The county had digitized almost nothing before 1950, so she spent her lunch hour with the deed books, following Aurelia Pruett forward and backward through time. Widowed at thirty-one. Kept the farm when the bank expected her to sell. A probate record listed her possessions: one bay mare named Lottie, a Singer sewing machine, "eleven volumes, various," and a pump organ.

Eleven volumes. Eleven streets.

Marisol laughed out loud in the empty basement, then felt bad about it, then didn't.

She never found out what the eleven books were. The probate clerk in 1934 hadn't thought them worth listing. So she chose for her. Pruett Creek Drive for the main road. Lottie Lane, obviously. Singer Court. Then she sat with a thesaurus and the 1911 map and gave the rest to things that might have mattered to a woman alone on a hundred and sixty acres: Fencerow, Cedar Line, First Frost, Lamplight, Kitchen Garden, Threshing Way, and, for the little dead-end that backed onto the drainage easement, Easement Path, because she thought Aurelia would have appreciated honesty about the plumbing.

Dennis read the list and frowned. "Who's Lottie?"

"A horse."

"We're naming a street after a horse."

"We named one after a Costco loading dock in 2021. It's called Commerce Loop."

He signed off. Dennis did not actually care; he only needed to feel that he'd asked a question.

The signs went up in March. Marisol drove out on a Saturday to look at them, green rectangles on fresh poles, standing in mud among the framed skeletons of houses. A couple in their twenties was walking the lots with a sales agent, and the woman stopped at the corner of Lottie Lane and Fencerow and said, "Oh, that's cute. I wonder who Lottie was."

The agent didn't know. He said it was probably a developer's daughter.

Marisol almost corrected him. Instead she stayed in her car and let it go, because that was the point, wasn't it. A name only had to hold the door open. Whoever walked through would furnish the room themselves. Someday a child on Lottie Lane would ask her mother who Lottie was, and the mother would make something up, and it would be true in the way that mattered.

Monday brought a new plat. Sixty-four lots. Nine streets.

Marisol went down to the basement and started pulling deed books from 1890.

**The Lighthouse Keeper's Ledger**

For thirty-one years Ines Varga kept the light at Ferrant Point, and for thirty-one years she wrote one line in the ledger each night. The ledger was not required. The Maritime Board wanted only the weather, the oil consumed, the ships that passed. Ines recorded those things in the official book. The ledger was hers.

*March 4. Fog until noon. A gull with one foot slept on the rail and did not fear me.*

*August 19. Lightning over the shoals. I thought of my brother and did not mind it.*

When the Board automated the light, they sent a young technician named Dov to decommission the station. He arrived with a toolkit and a clipboard and the apologetic manner of someone who has been told the person he is replacing will not like him.

She liked him fine. She made him tea and showed him the gear room, the brass that she had polished until it looked wet. He asked about the ledger, which sat open on the desk with a pen across it.

"Observations," she said.

"Of what?"

"Whatever did not need observing."

He read a few pages while she packed. He expected poetry and found instead a kind of inventory. Which direction the spiders built. The year the moths came late. The sound a particular wave made in the particular cove below, and how it changed after the storm of '09 moved a boulder.

"Nobody asked for this," he said. He did not mean it unkindly.

"No," she agreed. "That was the point."

On his last morning he found her at the rail with the ledger, writing. He waited until she finished.

*October 23. The new man checked the lamp three times though it was fine. He will be good at this.*

She closed the book and gave it to him.

"I can't," he said. "It's yours."

"It's the station's. The station is yours now." She put on her coat. "Keep the Board's book. They'll want it. Keep this one too. They won't."

He wrote nothing for eleven months. The light ran itself. He visited once a quarter, checked the lamp, filed his report, and left. The ledger sat in the desk drawer with its pen.

Then one evening in September, waiting for a storm to pass before the drive back, he saw a gull land on the rail. It had one foot. It could not be the same bird. He knew that.

He took the ledger out of the drawer.

*September 12*, he wrote. *A gull with one foot. It did not fear me either.*

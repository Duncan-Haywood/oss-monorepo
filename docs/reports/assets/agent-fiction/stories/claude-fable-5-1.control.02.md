**The Lighthouse Keeper's Inventory**

Every morning for thirty-one years, Oren Vask counted the stairs. One hundred and seventeen. He counted them going up with the oil can and counted them going down with the empty one, and the number never changed, which was the whole point of counting.

The lighthouse had been automated in the spring. A technician from the mainland had installed a sealed unit the size of a bread box, shaken Oren's hand, and told him the pension forms would arrive by post. Then the ferry had taken the technician away, and Oren had gone up the stairs anyway. One hundred and seventeen.

He kept a ledger. Not the official one, which the harbor authority had stopped requesting in 2009, but his own. Weather. Ships. The day the gannets came back. The day the gannets left. He wrote in pencil because ink ran in the damp, and he had learned that the first winter.

On the eleventh of October, a girl appeared at the bottom of the rock.

She had come across at low tide, which was stupid, and she knew it was stupid, which Oren decided was something. She was perhaps fourteen and soaked to the knees, and she had a camera around her neck that was worth more than his boat.

"You're not supposed to be here," he said.

"Neither are you," she said. "I looked it up. The keeper's post was eliminated."

He thought about that. It was true, and he had not heard anyone say it aloud.

"The tide won't turn for six hours," he said. "You'd better come up."

She counted the stairs without being asked. He noticed that. At the top she stood in the lamp room and looked at the sealed bread box doing his job, and then she looked at the brass fittings he still polished, and then she looked at him.

"Why do you stay?"

He had an answer ready. He had been preparing it since spring, in case anyone asked. It was about duty, and about the ships, and about how machines fail. He opened his mouth and found that he did not believe any of it.

"Because I know how many stairs there are," he said instead.

She did not laugh. She took a photograph of the ledger, open on the table to the page where he had written *gannets gone, wind NNE, no ships*. Then she asked if she could write in it.

He gave her the pencil.

She wrote: *11 Oct. One visitor. Keeper present.*

When the tide turned she went back down. One hundred and seventeen. He watched from the gallery until she reached the sand, and she turned once and raised the camera, and he did not wave because he did not know how to anymore. But he stood there until she was gone.

That night he made his entry. Then, under hers, in his own careful pencil, he added a line he had never written in thirty-one years.

*Keeper still present.*

He looked at it for a long while. Then he closed the ledger and went down to sleep, and for the first time since spring, he did not count.

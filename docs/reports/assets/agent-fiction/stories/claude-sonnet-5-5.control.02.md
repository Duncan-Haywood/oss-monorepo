**The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter**

Marguerite had kept the light at Cinder Point for forty-one years, and in all that time she had never once seen a ship turn back because of it.

Ships passed. They saw the beam, adjusted their course by a few degrees, and carried on into the dark, and that was the whole of it. No thanks, no waves. Once, in 1998, a fishing boat sounded its horn twice, and she had thought about it for a week.

The automated system arrived in October. Two technicians in orange jackets spent a morning bolting grey boxes to the walls of her lamp room, and by afternoon the great lens turned without her. They were kind about it. "You can stay as long as you like," one said. "Nothing for you to do, though."

That night she climbed the stairs anyway, all one hundred and twelve, and sat on the cold iron floor beneath the turning light. She had a pen and a sheet of paper. She wrote:

*To whoever is out there,*

*I have kept your light for forty-one years and have never met you. I want you to know it was lit every night, even the night my husband died, even the night of the big storm when the glass cracked and I held a tarp over it with my own two hands until dawn.*

*I don't know if you ever looked up. I hope you were home safe, wherever you were going.*

She folded the page, slipped it into an empty bottle of the ginger wine she'd allowed herself each New Year, and corked it. In the morning she walked down to the rocks and threw it as far as her old arm would let her.

It bobbed once, then the tide took it.

---

Eleven months later, a postcard arrived at the cottage, forwarded three times. The handwriting was cramped and unfamiliar. The picture on the front showed a harbor she didn't know, in a country she'd never visited.

*Found your bottle in my nets. Took me a while to track you down. I was on the water the night of the big storm. Engine dead, no stars, no idea where the rocks were. Then the light came back on, steady, after flickering out for a bit. I steered by it till morning. My daughter was aboard with me. She's twenty now.*

*Thank you for holding the tarp.*

Marguerite read it standing in the doorway, then sat down on the step and read it again.

That evening she climbed the one hundred and twelve stairs one more time, not because she had to, but to tell the light.

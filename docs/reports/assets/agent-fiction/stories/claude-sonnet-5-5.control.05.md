**The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Lamp**

Marrow Point had been automated for thirty years, but Ines still climbed the 112 steps every evening. Nobody had told her to stop, so she hadn't.

The light itself didn't need her. A sensor read the dusk and a motor turned the lens. What she did was wipe the salt from the glass, oil the hinges on the gallery door, and write the weather in a notebook no one had asked for.

On the last night of October, a storm came in sideways. Around nine, the power failed. The great lens stopped mid-sweep and the sea went dark.

Ines stood in the lantern room with her flashlight and felt something she hadn't felt in decades: necessity. In the cabinet beneath the lens sat the old brass oil lamp her grandfather had used, kept only because she couldn't bring herself to throw it out. She filled it, trimmed the wick, and lit it.

It was a small flame against a very large night. But the lens had been built for exactly this, and when she set the lamp at its focus, the light gathered, bent, and threw itself out over the water in a single steady beam.

She turned the lens by hand, one slow rotation at a time, for seven hours.

Near dawn, a fishing boat, the *Gull & Mercy*, limped around the point, its radio dead and its captain hoping for a landmark. He saw the beam sweep past, counted the interval, and steered by it into the harbor.

The next day, a crew restored the power. The motor hummed back to life, and the lens resumed its automatic turning.

Ines climbed the steps that evening anyway. She wiped the glass, oiled the hinge, and in the notebook wrote:

*Storm. Power out. Lamp worked fine.*

Then, after a moment, she added: *Someone was out there.*

**The Lamplighter's Last Round**

The city replaced the last gas lamp on Hollin Street in the spring, and Oren Vasko kept walking the route anyway.

He had done it for forty-one years. Up from the river at dusk, left at the shuttered chandlery, then the long climb of Hollin Street where the houses leaned toward each other like old friends sharing a secret. Thirty-six lamps, each one lit with the brass pole he had inherited from his father. The pole was gone now, surrendered to a clerk who gave him a receipt and a pension letter. The electric lamps came on by themselves at 7:14, all at once, with a sound like someone clearing their throat.

Oren hated that sound.

So he walked. He carried nothing, and he lit nothing, but he stopped at each post and stood for the length of time it used to take. Three breaths. Sometimes four, if the wick had been stubborn, and he remembered which ones had been stubborn. Number nine, outside the baker's, always sulked in wet weather. Number twenty-two had a crack in the glass that made the flame lean east.

People noticed. The baker's daughter, who had grown up watching him from the upstairs window, started leaving a roll on the sill of number nine. A boy named Teodor followed him for a week at a careful distance, then one evening simply fell in beside him and said nothing, which Oren appreciated.

"What are you doing?" Teodor finally asked, at lamp twenty.

"Finishing," Oren said.

"Finishing what?"

Oren thought about it through twenty-one and twenty-two, where the flame used to lean. "The lamps got lit every night for a hundred and twelve years," he said. "Then one night they didn't. Something should mark the ending. Otherwise it's just a thing that stopped."

Teodor seemed to find this reasonable. He came most nights after that.

In November, the electric lamps on Hollin Street failed. A transformer somewhere, the papers said later. The whole hill went dark at 7:14 instead of bright, and the throat-clearing sound did not come.

Oren was at lamp four when it happened. He stood in the sudden dark and felt the old pull in his hands, the weight of a pole that wasn't there. Then a door opened across the street, and a woman came out with a candle in a jar. She set it on the base of lamp five and went back inside without a word.

By lamp eight, there were candles on every post ahead of him. He could see them climbing the hill, a crooked line of small flames, each one placed by someone who had watched him stand there for three breaths every evening and had worked out, without being told, what he was waiting for.

The baker's daughter had put a candle and a roll on number nine.

Teodor met him at twelve, out of breath, holding a jar of his own. "I ran all the way from the river," he said. "I did the first three. I hope that's allowed."

"It's allowed," Oren said.

They walked the rest together. At every post Oren stopped and counted his breaths, and at every post the flame was already there, steady or guttering, leaning east at twenty-two because the woman who lived there had set it in the crack of the old glass that the city had never bothered to remove.

At the top of the hill, at thirty-six, Oren turned and looked back down Hollin Street. Thirty-six small lights. It was the first time in forty-one years he had ever seen the whole route lit from above, since he had always been at the bottom of it, or in the middle, working.

"It's not finished," Teodor said. "Is it."

"No," Oren said. "I suppose it isn't."

The power came back at a quarter to nine. The electric lamps buzzed on all at once, and the candles looked very small beside them. Nobody blew them out. They burned down on their own, one by one, in no particular order, the way things do when they are allowed to end properly.

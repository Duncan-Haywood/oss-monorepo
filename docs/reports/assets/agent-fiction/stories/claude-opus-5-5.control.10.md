**The Lighthouse Keeper's Inventory**

Every evening at six, Marguerite counted the things that remained.

Three lamps, one cracked. Forty-two steps to the lantern room. A kettle that whistled flat. Seventeen books, of which she had read sixteen. The seventeenth was a field guide to North Atlantic seabirds, and she was saving it for when she needed something to look forward to.

The supply boat had stopped coming in March. The radio had gone quiet in April, not with static but with a polite, final silence, like a door closed carefully so as not to wake anyone. She had kept the light burning anyway. That was the job. Nobody had told her to stop.

In June, a gull with a bent wing took up residence on the gallery rail. She named him Inspector, because he looked at everything she did with deep suspicion. She added him to the inventory: *One gull, opinionated.*

In July, she opened the seventeenth book. She learned that Inspector was not a gull at all but a fulmar, a cousin of the albatross, built to glide for weeks without landing. She apologized to him for the error. He accepted it with a sniff.

In August, a sail appeared on the horizon. It was small and patched, a fishing boat with a family aboard: a man, a woman, and a girl of about nine who stood at the bow with a hand shading her eyes. They had been following the light, the man said, when they finally tied up at the jetty. For three nights it had been the only light anywhere.

"Why did you keep it on?" the girl asked, climbing the forty-two steps behind her.

Marguerite thought about it. "Somebody has to count the things that remain," she said. "And it's easier to count them when you can see."

That evening at six, she sat down with her pencil and her ledger and wrote, beneath the fulmar:

*Three guests. Expected, it turns out.*

Then she put the kettle on, and it whistled flat, and for the first time in months the sound made someone else laugh.

# The Last Lighthouse Keeper

The storm had taken the radio on the third night, and the telegraph wire on the fourth. By the seventh, Mara had stopped expecting anyone.

She kept the lamp lit anyway. Her grandfather had told her that a light nobody sees is still a promise, and she had decided that a promise did not need an audience.

On the eighth morning, the fog thinned enough to show a shape on the water: a small wooden boat, oars shipped, drifting toward the rocks at the point. Mara ran down the spiral stairs, her boots slipping on the wet iron, and reached the shingle just as the current caught the hull.

Inside was a boy, perhaps ten, wrapped in a sailcloth that had once been red. He was not hurt. He was only staring at the lighthouse as if it were the first solid thing he had seen in weeks.

"Is it still working?" he asked.

"Every night," Mara said.

He nodded slowly, as though that settled something. Then he reached into his coat and pulled out a folded paper, soaked at the corners but whole. It was a chart, hand-drawn, with a small X marked on the cove beside the lighthouse and a line of dots leading from it into open water.

"My father drew this," the boy said. "He said if we ever got lost, we should find the light and wait. He said someone would keep it burning."

Mara looked at the chart for a long time. The X was in the exact spot where she had once, years ago, rowed out to meet a fishing boat that never came back. She had never told anyone that she still kept a second lamp in the window, angled toward that cove, lit only on the nights she could not sleep.

"Your father," she said quietly, "is he—"

"He's coming," the boy said. "He's rowing. He said he'd be right behind us."

Mara turned toward the water. Through the thinning fog, a second shape was emerging, slow and steady, the rhythm of oars carrying clearly across the calm.

She set down her lantern and, for the first time in seven nights, did not go back up the stairs. She stood on the shingle with the boy beside her and waited.

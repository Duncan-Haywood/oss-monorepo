**The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Light**

Marta had tended the lighthouse on Karrow Rock for forty-one years, and in all that time the sea had never once asked her for anything it didn't take back.

The automated beacon was arriving Thursday. A crew would come on the supply boat, bolt a gray box to the lamp room floor, and the great Fresnel lens, hand-polished every dawn since 1983, would go dark and be shipped to a museum.

On Wednesday night she climbed the 112 steps one last time, slower than she used to. At the top she lit the lamp the old way, with the match, the hiss, the slow bloom of gold turning through the glass. The beam swung out over the black water.

Far out, a small light blinked back.

She frowned. No ships were due. She raised the binoculars and found a fishing boat, tiny and tilting, flashing a hand torch in short, uneven bursts. Three short, three long, three short.

Marta laughed, startled, and her voice cracked in the empty room. She hadn't seen anyone use that signal for real in decades. She set down the binoculars, took hold of the shutter cord, and answered the old way: one long sweep of light, held steady, pointing the way through the rocks.

The boat came in slowly, picking its path along her beam. By midnight a young woman and her father were wrapped in blankets at Marta's kitchen table, their engine dead, their radio drowned, steam rising from the tea.

"We'd have hit the shoals," the father said. "Your light's the only reason we didn't."

"It's a good light," Marta said.

In the morning the supply boat came, and the crew bolted their gray box to the floor. It was efficient and silent and would never need a single match. Marta stood at the window and watched the Fresnel lens, wrapped in blankets, lowered onto the deck.

The young woman from the fishing boat was waiting at the rail. "Where will you go?" she asked.

"My sister's, inland." Marta shrugged. "I've never lived anywhere I couldn't hear the water."

"There's a cottage near our harbor," the girl said. "Empty. You could see the whole bay from the porch. We could use someone who knows how to read the sea."

Marta looked back at the tower, small now against the morning, its new light already blinking its patient, tireless pulse. Then she picked up her bag and stepped aboard.

Behind her, the beam kept turning. Ahead, the water was wide and gray and, for the first time in forty-one years, wholly unfamiliar.

She found she didn't mind.

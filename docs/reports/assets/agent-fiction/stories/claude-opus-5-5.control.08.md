**The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter**

The supply boat stopped coming in March, so Ines started writing letters to the sea.

She folded each one into a paper boat and set it on the tide at dusk. The first few were practical: *Dear whoever finds this, the lamp still works, the fog signal does not, please send diesel.* By April they had gone soft at the edges. She wrote about the gull with the crooked wing that came to the kitchen window every morning. She wrote about the night the stars were so thick she had to sit down on the gallery floor, dizzy with them. She wrote that she had stopped counting days because counting made the island smaller.

She never expected an answer. That wasn't why she did it.

In June, a paper boat came back.

It was wedged between two rocks at the foot of the landing, its sides swollen with salt, and the handwriting inside wasn't hers. It was a child's, all wobbling capitals:

*DEAR LIGHTHOUSE LADY. WE SEE YOUR LIGHT FROM OUR HOUSE. MY DAD SAYS NOBODY LIVES THERE ANYMORE BUT I TOLD HIM SOMEBODY HAS TO TURN IT ON. IS THE GULL OK. — MARTA, AGE 8*

Ines read it four times. Then she climbed the hundred and twelve steps to the lamp room and looked across the water to the far shore, where a scatter of lights glimmered against the dark hills. She had always assumed they were empty, the way she'd assumed she was.

She went back down and wrote a letter.

*Dear Marta. The gull is fine. His name is Admiral. He prefers sardines to bread and is very rude about it. Your father is half right: nobody lives here anymore, except me. Thank you for noticing the light. Most people only notice it when it's gone.*

She set it on the outgoing tide and watched until the dark took it.

The diesel came in July, along with a man from the coastal authority who apologized four different ways for the mix-up with the supply contract. He offered her a ride back to the mainland. A real bed, he said. People.

Ines looked past him, out at the far shore.

"I have people," she said. "They write."

That night she lit the lamp a little early, and across the water, in one small window, someone flicked a flashlight on and off three times, and she laughed out loud, alone on the gallery, not alone at all.

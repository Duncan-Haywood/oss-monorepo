# The Lighthouse Keeper's Inventory

Every evening at six, Marguerite counted the things the sea returned.

It was not part of her job. The Coast Authority had automated the lamp in 2009, and her position, *Heritage Custodian, Pell Point*, required only that she dust the brass, sign the visitor log (eleven entries last year, four of them her sister), and keep the gulls out of the lantern room. But the beach below the tower was a kind of drain for the whole northern current, and things washed up. So she counted them.

Her ledger read, in part:

*March 3. One child's shoe, red, left foot. One bottle, green, no message. Rope.*

*March 9. Rope. A plastic duck, faded nearly white. A wedding ring, gold, inscribed "M — always — T."*

She had stopped at that one. Her name began with M. Her husband's had begun with T. She had stood on the cold sand turning the ring in her fingers and laughed so hard a gull lifted off in offense, because Thomas had been buried with his ring, and hers was still on her hand, and the world was simply full of Ms and Ts who had promised each other always.

She put it in the ledger and in the jar with the others.

---

By summer the jar held fourteen rings. She didn't think much of it at first. People lost rings at sea; fingers shrank in cold water. But by August there were thirty, and by October there were sixty-one, and every one of them was inscribed, and not one inscription repeated.

*"Until the tide turns."*
*"R & B, 1974."*
*"You were right about the boat."*

That last one kept her up a night.

She wrote to the Coast Authority. They wrote back a polite form letter about salvage law. She wrote to the university in Halbrook, and a young oceanographer named Priya drove out with a laptop and a thermos and spent two days modeling currents on Marguerite's kitchen table.

"It doesn't make sense," Priya said finally. "Gold is dense. It doesn't drift. These should be on the bottom wherever they were dropped."

"And yet," said Marguerite.

"And yet," Priya agreed, and looked out the window at the gray water as though it had told a joke she didn't get.

---

The last ring came in November, on the first night the lamp failed.

The automation tripped at 9:40. Marguerite heard the silence before she understood it, a gap where the motor's hum should have been, and climbed the hundred and twelve steps with a flashlight in her teeth. The bulb was fine. The relay was fine. She could find nothing wrong. She did what the old keepers would have done: she found the manual crank in the supply closet, fitted it into the housing, and turned the great lens by hand.

She turned it for six hours. Her shoulders burned and then went past burning into a kind of distant hum. Out on the water, a small light blinked, steadied, and swung away from the rocks.

At dawn the automation clicked back on as if it had never stopped.

She went down to the beach. In the wet sand at the waterline, alone, lay a plain silver band. She knew before she picked it up. She knew the weight of it, the slight flat spot where Thomas had caught it in the boat winch the summer they were married.

Inside, in letters worn almost smooth, it said: *M — always — T.*

---

She did not tell Priya. She did not write to the Coast Authority. She put the ring on a chain around her neck, and that evening at six she went down to the beach with her ledger as usual.

There was rope. There was a green bottle. There was nothing else, and there never would be again. She knew that, the way she'd known the ring.

She wrote it down anyway.

*November 14. Rope. One bottle, green. Everything accounted for.*

The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Light

The storm had taken the radio on the third night, and by the fifth, Marta had stopped expecting anyone to come.

She climbed the spiral stairs each evening anyway. The lamp needed winding, the lens needed cleaning, and the habit of doing things kept her from listening too hard to the wind.

On the sixth morning the sea went flat and grey as pewter, and there was a boat on the rocks below.

It was small, a fishing skiff with its hull split like a walnut. Marta went down with the rope and the blanket and the thermos. A man lay wedged in the bow, alive, his lips blue, clutching a canvas bag against his chest.

"Don't," he rasped when she reached for it. "Please. Don't take it."

"I'm not going to take anything," she said. "You're going to freeze."

She got him up the cliff path one step at a time, his arm across her shoulders, the bag bumping between them. At the top he sank onto the step by the door and laughed, a cracked, delighted sound.

"You're the keeper," he said. "I thought you'd been gone for years."

"I have," Marta said. "Mostly."

Inside, she wrapped him in the wool blanket and fed the stove. He drank the tea without tasting it. Only when the color came back to his face did he open the bag and draw out a glass jar, sealed with wax, holding something that glowed faintly gold.

"My daughter," he said. "She's six. She lives on the mainland with her mother. I was bringing her this." He turned the jar in the lamplight. "It's the last of the honey from my grandmother's hives. The bees died in the blight, all but this one colony, and I've kept them alive for four years in a shed behind the church. Nobody else has honey like this anymore."

Marta looked at the jar and felt something loosen in her chest that she had not known was tight.

"Why didn't you say that to the wind?" she asked.

He laughed again. "I did. Every night. I thought it might hear."

She took the jar from him, gently, and set it on the sill beside the lens, where the first light of the evening would strike it.

"Then we'll keep it lit," she said. "Until your daughter comes for it."

That night she climbed the stairs and wound the lamp, and for the first time in five years, the beam swept out over the water, and somewhere far off, a boat turned its prow toward the light.

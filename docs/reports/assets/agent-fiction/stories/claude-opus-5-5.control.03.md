# The Lighthouse Keeper's Inventory

The automation crew arrived on a Tuesday, which Marguerite thought was rude. Tuesdays were for polishing the lens.

They were polite young people with tablets and steel-toed boots, and they called her "ma'am" in the careful way you'd approach a dog of uncertain temperament. The lead technician was named Osei. He had a kind face and a lanyard that said CONTRACTOR in red letters, as though that explained everything.

"We'll need an inventory," Osei said. "Anything the Coast Guard owns stays. Anything personal, you take."

So Marguerite made a list.

*One Fresnel lens, third order, 1891.* Coast Guard.

*One brass oil can, dented.* She hesitated. It had been there when she arrived thirty-one years ago. She wrote *Coast Guard*, then crossed it out and wrote *Mine*.

*Logbooks, 1952–present.* Coast Guard, technically. But she'd written eleven thousand nights into them: the weather, the ships, the time a minke whale circled the rocks for an hour as if looking for a door. She photographed every page before handing them over, and Osei pretended not to notice.

*One radio.* Coast Guard.

*Seventeen voices.* She didn't write that one down. But they were there: the fishing captains who'd called in at 3 a.m. over the decades, not because they needed anything, but because the light was on and someone was awake. Bill Tremaine, who'd died in '09. The Portuguese trawler whose captain only knew the words *thank you* and *good night* in English and used them in that order, every time. She'd learned to answer *obrigada* and *boa noite*.

*One view.* She stood at the gallery rail on her last evening and tried to decide who owned it. The sea was the color of pewter, then of ink. The new LED beacon flickered on above her head, precise and cold and blind, sweeping its arc on a timer that would never need her.

Osei came up the spiral stairs with two mugs of tea. He handed her one without asking how she took it. He'd noticed, apparently, over three days.

"For what it's worth," he said, "it's a good light. It'll run forty years without a bulb change."

"I don't doubt it."

"But it won't—" He stopped, searching. "It won't *know* anything."

Marguerite looked at him properly for the first time. Then she reached into her coat and took out the dented oil can and pressed it into his hands.

"Last item on the inventory," she said. "It belongs to whoever climbs these stairs when they don't have to."

He drove her to the mainland the next morning. She did not look back at the tower, because she didn't need to. She knew exactly where it was. She'd always know.

Out on the water, a trawler passed the point, and someone aboard raised a radio to their mouth, and paused, and set it down again.

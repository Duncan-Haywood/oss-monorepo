Today, a water-level graph that bends, and what it tells you about rock you cannot see.

A new short story in the repository, called The Second Slope, is set in an invented convent in the granite hills north of Córdoba. The nuns want to open forty beds for pilgrims. They have one borehole, so they pump it hard for three days and watch the water level fall.

Here is the method, which is real and is in the textbooks. Pump a well at a steady rate. Plot how far the water has dropped against the logarithm of time. In a simple aquifer you get a straight line, and the slope of that line tells you how easily the rock gives up water. The story pumps twenty six cubic metres a day and measures a drop of one point one nine metres for every tenfold increase in time. The sum is two point three zero three times the pumping rate, divided by four pi times the slope, and it gives a transmissivity of about four square metres a day. That is low, as fractured granite usually is.

Then, a day and a half in, the line bends upward, and the new slope is exactly double the old one. That is the signature of a boundary: a place where the cracked rock stops, so the cone of lowered water has reached a wall and the well can only draw from one side.

How far away is the wall? That depends on a number called the storativity, which one well cannot measure. So the story's water clerk, a small program, does not give one answer. For storativity of one in a thousand the wall is fifty eight metres off. At the low end of published values it is a hundred and six, and at the high end thirty four. The honest result is a range, and the clerk says which number it guessed.

The range matters because a goat farmer's borehole sits three hundred and eighty metres away. The story carries the wall through to his well: about three point three metres of extra drawdown today, and five point four with the guesthouse running, against about six metres of margin. The convent takes twenty cubic metres a day, with a public five metre trigger on the farmer's own gauge.

The caveats: the convent, the farmer and the granite are invented. The method is the standard straight line analysis, and the note at the end of the story shows every sum, so you can check it. One pumping test cannot prove a wall is straight; other things can double a slope too.

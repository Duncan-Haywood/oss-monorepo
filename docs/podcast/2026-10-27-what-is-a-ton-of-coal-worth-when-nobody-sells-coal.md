Today, a ton of coal, and what it would be worth in a world where nobody sells coal.

English fuel allotment charities are real. Some parishes own land whose rent once bought coal or wood for the poor, and a few are still running, with deeds written for fuels nobody sells any more. A new short story in the repository, called The Coal Charity, invents one. An 1853 deed promises a ton of coal to each widow in a village, and the charity's clerk, an A.I., has to say what that ton is worth in the year twenty forty six.

Here is the arithmetic, which is real. A long ton is one thousand and sixteen kilograms. At about twenty seven megajoules a kilogram, that is roughly seventy six hundred kilowatt hours of heat. But an open grate puts perhaps a quarter of that into the room. So the warmth is about nineteen hundred kilowatt hours, with a range from eleven hundred to twenty seven hundred.

A heat pump with a seasonal coefficient of performance of three makes the same warmth from about six hundred and thirty five kilowatt hours of electricity. At twenty eight pence a unit, that is a hundred and seventy eight pounds. Allow the coefficient to run from two and a half to three and a half, and the range is a hundred and fifty two to two hundred and thirteen pounds.

The clerk does something worth copying. It shows which figures it measured and which it guessed. And it notes that one widow's heat pump needs about twelve thousand three hundred kilowatt hours of warmth over a winter, so the ton was never the whole of it.

The story's answer is not only arithmetic. The money is paid as a credit, and the village keeps the morning, and a Mass for the donor.

The caveats: the village and the charity are invented, and so are the twenty forty six prices, the lease rent and the Charity Commission's ruling. The sums are the part you can check.

Today, a puzzle that frightens people every few years: why the chance of an asteroid hitting the Earth goes up, day after day, before it drops to nothing.

Here is the idea, and it is real. Nobody knows exactly where the asteroid will pass. Picture the possibilities as a long, thin smear of light across the sky near the Earth. The best guess sits at the middle of the smear, and in the story that best guess misses the Earth's centre by about a hundred and thirty thousand kilometres. The Earth's gravity bends paths towards it, so the target is a little larger than the planet, a radius of about eight thousand four hundred kilometres.

Early on, the smear is enormous, two million kilometres wide, and the Earth covers only a sliver of it. The chance is about one in three hundred. Each new night of observations shrinks the smear. The asteroid has not moved. But the Earth now covers a bigger share of a smaller smear. At six hundred thousand kilometres the chance is about one in ninety. At two hundred thousand, one in thirty seven. It peaks, at one in thirty two, when the width of the smear equals the miss distance.

Then it falls off a cliff. Shrink the smear to sixty thousand kilometres and the Earth sits out in its faint edge: one in ninety four. At forty thousand, about one in twelve hundred. A few more nights and it is effectively zero.

In the story the nun shows this with a flashlight and a quarter taped to the parish hall wall. She walks up the aisle, the beam tightens, and for a while more and more of its light falls on the coin. Then the bright spot slides off it.

Real cases have run the same way. In December 2004, the asteroid Apophis briefly had a chance of about one in forty of striking in 2029, until archive images from earlier that year ruled it out. In early 2025, an asteroid called 2024 YR4 reached about three percent for 2032 before more observations brought it to nearly zero. Those two figures come from general knowledge, so take them as approximate.

The second half of the story is about how you shrink the smear fast. Finding an asteroid in old images, called precovery, can stretch its record from three weeks to four years in one night. The nun finds it in a dead sister's four year old patrol frames.

But the asteroid moves about twenty seven point six seconds of arc every minute, and the old computer's clock was forty one seconds fast. So every position lands about nineteen seconds of arc too far along the track. That is roughly forty times the measurement's own precision. An orbit centre could make the fit look perfect by sliding the time, which is exactly what it must not do. As the story's scientist puts it: a precovery with a bad time is worse than no precovery. It persuades.

The rescue is a red notebook in which the old sister wrote the clock's error against a GPS receiver every night for twenty three years, and never corrected the clock.

This calculator vs. comparing headline commission rates

Gathering two or three estate sale quotes is the standard advice, and the usual next step is to line up the percentages: 30% against 35%, take the lower one. That works when both companies charge one flat rate and nothing else. It stops working as soon as a schedule has tiers or the contract has flat fees — and most do both, which is how the company quoting the smallest number ends up keeping the larger share.

What each one is for

A headline commission rate is a real term from the contract and a fair way to start the conversation. What it cannot do is stand for the contract. A tiered schedule charges its highest rate on the first dollars of the sale, so the low tier a company leads with may apply only to the top slice — or, on a smaller sale, to nothing at all. And a headline rate describes commission only: setup or staging, credit-card processing, and haul-away or donation fees come off on top of it without moving any percentage in the schedule.

This calculator applies each company's actual tier schedule and each of its flat fees to the same estimated gross sale, applies a minimum guarantee if the contract has one, and reports what the family actually takes home — alongside total retained, the single percentage a headline rate was trying to be.

A worked example where the headline rate points the wrong way

Two quotes against the same $12,000 estimated gross sale. Company A leads with “25% above $8,000” — the lowest percentage either company says out loud. Company B quotes a flat 35% and charges nothing else.

Company ACompany B
Headline rate quoted25% above $8,00035% flat
Tier schedule40% to $2,000, 30% to $8,000, 25% above35% throughout
Commission$3,600.00$4,200.00
Setup / staging, card processing, haul-away$950.00$0.00
Net to the family$7,450.00$7,800.00
Total retained by the company37.92%35.00%

Company A wins on commission and on every percentage it quoted, and still leaves the family $350 worse off. Its 25% tier only reaches the $4,000 above the threshold; the 40% tier takes the first $2,000 regardless; and the $950 of flat fees appears in no rate at all. Comparing the two headline numbers — 25% against 35% — gets this backwards.

Common questions

What is a headline commission rate?

The single percentage a company leads with when asked what it charges — “we're 30%”, or “25% over $8,000”. It is a real number from the contract, but it is one number out of a schedule that usually has two or three, and it describes commission only. The flat fees are quoted separately or further down the page.

Why can the company with the lower headline rate cost more?

Two reasons stack. A tiered schedule charges its highest rate on the first dollars of the sale, so the low tier a company leads with may only apply to the top slice — on a smaller sale it may never apply at all. And setup or staging, credit-card processing, and haul-away or donation fees are deducted on top of commission, so they raise the share retained without changing any percentage in the schedule.

Is a headline rate ever enough to compare on?

Yes, when both companies quote one flat rate across the whole sale and neither adds flat fees. Then the headline rate is the whole contract and the lower one nets more. That is the case this calculator agrees with you about in a few seconds — the point is that you find out which case you are in rather than assuming it.

What does “total retained” mean here?

Commission plus every flat fee, expressed as a share of the estimated gross sale. It is the number a headline rate is standing in for: what the company keeps in total, as one percentage you can hold next to another company's. It is computed from the schedule and fees you enter, so it changes with your estimated sale total.

Do I need exact numbers from the contracts?

Enter what the written quotes say and treat the result as a planning estimate. The gross sale total is an estimate by nature, and the tiers interact with it — a schedule that looks better at $8,000 can look worse at $20,000, so it is worth re-running at a high and a low estimate rather than one figure.

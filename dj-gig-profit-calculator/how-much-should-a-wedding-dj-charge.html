How much should a wedding DJ charge?

Worked on invented numbers; put yours into the quote builder. Nothing here is a market price.

One reception, priced from the bottom up

A 5-hour reception quoted at $1,200. Around it: 2 hours of load-in and load-out, 2 hours of planning and music prep, 1 hour of driving. That is 5 unpaid hours on top of 5 playing hours, 10 hours of work, and 50% of it is not on the dance floor.

Take-home profit$1,071.00

Profit divided by 10 hours is $107.10 per hour worked, against $240.00 per playing hour on the quote. The break-even price, where profit is zero and your 10 hours are unpaid, is $96; the price that pays $60 per hour worked is $714. Overtime should be billed at no less than $61.86 per hour to cover the same target.

Pricing a year

To take home $60,000 over 60 gigs, 35 in peak season and 25 off-season with peak priced 20% higher, the base package needs about $1,009 off-season and $1,211 at peak. That is 600 hours of work a year and $100.00 per hour worked.

Common questions

How much should a wedding DJ charge?

Work from the hours you really spend. On the example numbers a 5-hour reception costs 10 hours of work once 5 unpaid hours of load-in, planning and driving are added, so a $1,200 quote is $240.00 per playing hour but $107.10 per hour worked. Local prices differ; enter your own figures in the calculator.

What is the break-even price for a DJ gig?

The price at which take-home profit is zero: your gig costs divided by one minus the fees. On the example it is $96, and the price that pays $60 per hour worked is $714.

How do I price a year, not a gig?

Pick the take-home you want, then solve for the price. To take home $60,000 over 60 gigs (35 peak, 25 off-season, peak 20% higher) the example needs about $1,009 off-season and $1,211 at peak.

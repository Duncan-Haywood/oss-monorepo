This calculator vs. picking a resistor or capacitor from the parts bin

Grabbing a component that's "about right" is fast, and it's how most prototype filters get built. The part missing from that habit isn't knowledge of the formula -- it's the second calculation nobody does: what cutoff does the part you actually grabbed produce, and how far is that from the one you designed for?

What each approach actually answers

Picking a component from the bin answers "do I have something in roughly the right range?" -- a quick, reasonable first question. What it doesn't answer is the one that decides whether the filter does what you designed it for: the exact value a target cutoff needs is almost never a value that gets sold, so any real build already involves rounding, and rounding by eye means the size of that rounding error is simply unknown until someone sits down with the formula and the actual part.

This calculator does that second step for you. Give it a target cutoff and one known component, and its design mode solves for the exact value the other component needs, snaps it to the nearest value in the standard series you'd actually buy (E12 for capacitors and inductors, E24 for resistors), and reports the cutoff and signed error percent that snapped value really produces -- so "close enough" is a number you read off the page rather than a guess you carry into the build.

Neither replaces the other. You still choose the part; the tool just tells you, before you solder it in, exactly what cutoff that choice gives you.

Where a bin-picked value typically goes wrong

HabitWhat it does to the real cutoff
Grabbing a value from the wrong decadeA 10 nF part picked up in place of a 100 nF one shifts the cutoff by a factor of 10 -- easy to do by eye when two reels or drawers are labelled similarly.
Assuming a value is in the standard seriesPreferred-value resistors step in the E24 series and most stocked capacitors in E12; a value that looks plausible can simply not exist as a part, forcing a substitution nobody planned for.
Never recomputing after the substitutionThe single most common gap: a part is swapped in because it's close, and the cutoff it actually produces is never worked back out, so the error is unknown rather than small.
Ignoring the part's own toleranceA real resistor or capacitor carries its own ±5% to ±20% tolerance on top of whatever rounding error the substitution already introduced -- two separate sources of drift that stack rather than cancel.

This calculator can't stock your parts bin for you -- it can only compute correctly once you tell it which component you actually have or picked. What it removes is redoing the cutoff-frequency arithmetic by hand after that choice.

Common questions

Isn't grabbing a close resistor or capacitor good enough for most filters?

Sometimes -- if the application has generous margin, close is fine. The risk is that "close" is a guess about the component value, not about the cutoff frequency it produces, and those two aren't the same distance apart. A resistor or capacitor picked because it's "about right" from a mixed parts bin can land the real -3 dB cutoff anywhere from a few percent off to a full octave or more away from the one the design actually called for, with nothing about the build itself signaling the mismatch.

What actually goes wrong when you round a component value in your head?

Three separate mistakes tend to compound: reaching for a value from the wrong decade (10 nF picked up instead of 100 nF is a 10x cutoff error), assuming a value is a standard preferred one when the parts bin only stocks a different series, and -- most often -- never recomputing the cutoff after the substitution at all, so the actual error is simply unknown rather than small.

Does this calculator replace understanding preferred-value series like E12 and E24?

No, and it isn't trying to. Knowing that components come in E12/E24 steps rather than arbitrary values is still useful background. What this tool removes is redoing the arithmetic by hand every time: it solves for the exact component a target cutoff needs, snaps that to the nearest value in the series you'd actually buy, and reports the cutoff and signed error percent that snapped value really produces -- so the gap between "designed for" and "built with" is a number on the page instead of a guess.

Does this tool store or upload the values I enter?

No. The calculator runs entirely in your browser -- the components and target cutoff you enter, and the result it computes, never leave this page, and there is no account, upload, or server involved.

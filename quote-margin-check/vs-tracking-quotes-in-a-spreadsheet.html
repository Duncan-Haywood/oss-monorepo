This calculator vs. tracking quotes in a spreadsheet

Effective hourly rate, a cost rise, a discount ask, fixed-vs-hourly, a kill fee, and a price ladder are each ordinary arithmetic. This page names no specific product — it compares against the generic method of building that arithmetic in a spreadsheet, and says where a spreadsheet still wins.

What each approach actually does

A spreadsheet can reproduce every formula this calculator runs: total hours to an effective rate, a target price at a chosen hourly rate, what a cost rise or a client's discount ask does to that rate, fixed pay minus what the same hours would earn billed hourly, what finishing beats a kill fee by, and a price ladder across a range of target rates. Building it means one formula per scenario, in its own section, and a new section the next time the quote needs a decision the sheet does not already have.

This calculator keeps all six of those scenarios wired to the same quote, so typing one number — the price, the hours, a requested discount — recomputes every panel at once instead of six formulas one at a time. A shareable link carries the whole quote in the URL itself, so sending someone the scenario is a link instead of a file two people have to keep in sync. And when you do want the numbers in a sheet, the page exports a spreadsheet-safe CSV, a Markdown review, or a JSON report in one click, so building the sheet is optional rather than the only way in.

Common questions

Can a spreadsheet do this math instead?

Yes. Effective hourly rate, a cost rise, a discount ask, fixed-vs-hourly billing, a kill fee, and a target-rate price ladder are all ordinary arithmetic you can put in a spreadsheet. Building it means one formula per scenario, and rebuilding a section the next time the quote needs a decision the sheet does not already have — a client asking for 10% off, a mid-project cancellation, a cost that rose before the work started.

What does a spreadsheet not do that this calculator does?

Keep all six scenarios wired to the same quote so a discount ask, a kill fee, and a cost rise recompute from one edit instead of six separate formula updates. It also produces a shareable link that carries the whole quote in the URL itself, and CSV, Markdown, and JSON exports for the moment you do want the numbers in a sheet, without building the sheet first.

Is a spreadsheet ever the better choice?

Yes, for tracking many quotes over time in one place, or for a fee structure this tool's inputs cannot represent — a retainer with a rolling discount, a multi-currency contract, revenue share instead of a flat price. A spreadsheet you build yourself can represent any rule you can describe. This calculator trades that flexibility for a form that already has the right fields for one quote's effective rate, right now.

Is my data uploaded anywhere?

No. The calculator runs entirely in your browser. A shareable link carries your figures in the URL itself rather than sending them to a server, and the optional local history and usage counters stay on this device.

    Questions people ask

    What is a CD ladder?

    You split your money into equal parts and buy CDs or Treasuries with different maturities, for example 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 years. Each year one rung matures, giving you cash access, and you can reinvest it at the longest term. You earn closer to long-term rates while some money always comes due soon.

    Is a Treasury ladder better than a CD ladder?

    Treasury interest is exempt from state and local income tax, CD interest is not, so in a state with income tax a Treasury can win after tax even at a slightly lower rate. Where there is no state income tax the higher headline rate usually wins. The tool compares both after tax at the rates you type.

    Is a ladder better than putting everything in the longest CD?

    Only when rates rise. If rates fall after you buy, the single longest CD wins because you locked the high rate. If rates rise, rolling short or a ladder reinvests at the better rates. The scenario table shows the result for rate moves of minus 2 to plus 2 points.

    How many rungs and how long should each be?

    More rungs mean smaller chunks come due more often, which helps liquidity, at the cost of managing more positions. Common ladders use 4 to 5 rungs of 12 months, or 12 monthly or quarterly rungs of short T-bills. Match the spacing to when you may need the cash.

    Are the rates in the example real?

    No. They are placeholders, not current quotes. Type in the rates you can actually get from your bank, brokerage or TreasuryDirect, and update them when they change.

    Not decided

    Whether to hold these instruments at all. The model ignores early-withdrawal penalties, callable CDs, FDIC and SIPC limits, brokered-CD fees, Treasury auction mechanics and purchase limits, inflation, and the tax treatment of discount on bills. It treats a rate move as a single parallel shift right after you buy and values accrued interest at the end of the ladder.

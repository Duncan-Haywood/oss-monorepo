# Ground-effect twin: a twin without ground effect says a gravity-feedforward altitude hold sits on target and keeps its stability margin near the ground; the real vehicle hovers 18 mm high at one rotor radius, and a delayed loop with 28% twin headroom goes unstable at 0.54 rotor radii

Pure Python, no dependencies. Real system: a unit-mass rotorcraft holding height `z_r` with `T_c = g + k_p(z_r − z) − k_d ż` (gravity feedforward, no integrator) and thrust `T = G(z) T_c`, where `G(z) = 1/(1 − (R/4z)²)` is the constant-power ground-effect multiplier (Cheeseman & Bennett 1955; rotor radius `R = 0.1 m`; used only for `z ≥ R/2`, where `G ≤ 4/3`). The twin has `G = 1`. Results (all from `experiments/results.txt`): (1) the twin predicts zero offset; the real equilibrium solves `G(z)(g + k_p(z_r − z)) = g` and sits above target by 2.7, 5.8, 9.6, 17.7, 26.4, 30.2 mm at `z_r/R = 3, 2, 1.5, 1, 0.7, 0.6` (0.9% to 50% of the target height) for `k_p = 25, k_d = 7`; a nonlinear simulation lands on the exact root to 1e-6 and the first-order law `(G−1)g/(Gk_p + S)` is within 0.1 mm at `z_r ≥ 2R` but 22% low at `z_r = 0.7R`; (2) sampled loop (`dt = 0.02 s`, one-sample controller delay, `k_p = 400, k_d = 28`): the twin's gain margin is 1.2755 (27.6% headroom) at every height; the real loop's margin, including the undelayed restoring stiffness `S = −G′g/G` the thrust gradient adds, falls to 1.2663, 1.1897, 1.1028, 1.0457, 1.0073 at `z_r/R = 3, 1, 0.7, 0.6, 0.55` and reaches the stability boundary at `z_r = 0.542 R`; (3) the stiffness term is not negligible: dropping it overstates the margin at `0.6 R` (1.0795 vs 1.0457) and, with gain scaling alone, the loop is still stable at `0.505 R` (margin 1.0165), so the instability is missed entirely; (4) a nonlinear sampled simulation confirms the boundary (2 mm perturbation decays at `z_r = 0.55 R` to 0.02 mm after 20 s, diverges at `0.53 R`; the twin simulation is stable at every height); (5) the gain scale the twin-designed loop can tolerate is 0.7%, 6.8%, 13.6%, 18.1%, 21.1% below the twin's at `z_r/R = 3, 1, 0.7, 0.6, 0.55`. Negative/cautionary: the effect is negligible above about two rotor radii (0.7% margin, 6 mm offset), so a twin without ground effect is fine for cruise and only wrong in the final landing phase.

```bash
cd research/groundeffect-twin
PYTHONPATH=src python3 -m unittest discover -s tests -v   # 12 tests, about 15 s
PYTHONPATH=src python3 experiments/run.py                 # about 20 s; output in experiments/results.txt
```

**Builds on.** The UAV simulation-fidelity and sim-to-real direction of RECUV (<https://www.colorado.edu/recuv/>) and the Autonomous Systems IRT (<https://www.colorado.edu/irt/autonomous-systems/>); no specific paper from either group is reproduced and nothing here is affiliated with or endorsed by them. The ground-effect multiplier is the classical Cheeseman–Bennett model (1955); the effect is documented for multirotors by, e.g., Sanchez-Cuevas et al. (2017). Companion to `gust-twin` and `latency-twin`, `windup-twin` (the integrator that would remove the offset) and `uav-energy-twin`.

Stylised: simulated "real" vehicle, no flight data; a point-mass vertical axis, one rotor-radius scale (`R = 0.1 m`), the closed-form constant-power ground-effect model outside its fitted range (it is singular at `z = R/4`, so results stop at `z = R/2`), no rotor dynamics, no body/frame effects, a single one-sample delay and one set of gains. The twin is a deliberately naive baseline (an identified twin with `G(z)` would reproduce these numbers by construction). MIT.

# Twin transfer: regret, a validation blind spot, and the worth of a digital twin in real samples

*Stylised: scalar linear-quadratic plant, Gaussian process noise, certainty-equivalent controller trained in the twin, a known when identifying b. Pure Python; every formula is checked against a literal simulation (`tests/`, `experiments/results.txt`). Plant used throughout: `a=0.9, b=1, q=1, r=0.1, σ²=1`, optimal gain `k*=0.8233`.*

## Question
Simulating and training in a digital clone of a physical system is cheap; the deployed system is what matters. Three questions with exact answers in the smallest model that has them: (1) how much control performance does a wrong twin lose, (2) does a good predictive score for the twin certify that loss is small, and (3) if some real data is available, how many real samples is a twin of a given accuracy worth?

## Model
Plant `x' = a x + b u + w`, `w ~ N(0,σ²)`; average cost `q x² + r u²`; policy `u = −k x`. The twin has parameters `(â, b̂)`; the controller deployed is `k̂ = k*(â, b̂)` from the scalar Riccati equation. This connects to the proper-scoring line of the repo (`decision-regret-transfer`, `audit-weighted-scoring`): the twin is a forecaster, its predictive distribution is scored against real transitions, and the question is what the score certifies.

## Exact results
1. **Deployment cost and regret.** Gain `k` on the plant costs `J(k) = σ²(q+rk²)/(1−c²)` per step, `c=a−bk`, infinite if `|c|≥1` (simulation over 400k steps agrees within 0.4%). The twin-trained gain has regret `J(k̂)−J(k*)`, locally `½ J''(k*) (dk*/db)² (b̂−b)²` (`J''=2.36`, `dk*/db=−0.69`; the local law is within 7% at ±5% error and off by 30–40% at ±25%).
2. **Regret is asymmetric with a cliff.** A twin that underestimates the input gain trains a high gain that overshoots on the real plant: regret is 0.031 at `b̂/b=0.8`, 0.43 at 0.5, 9.3 at 0.25 and **infinite below 0.2138**; overestimating by 2× costs only 0.22 (3× costs 0.51). The local quadratic law understates the low side and overstates the high side. Sim-to-real failures are dominated by the twin being too pessimistic about actuation.
3. **A good predictive score does not certify control.** The twin's expected one-step log-score excess (a KL) on data from behaviour policy `u=−k_B x+e`, `e~N(0,v)` is `[Δa²E x² + 2ΔaΔb E xu + Δb²E u²]/(2σ²)` (matches a 400k-step simulation within 3%). For a twin with `b̂=1.4b`, whose deployment regret is 0.0567: on a passive log (`u=0`) the gap is **exactly zero**; with probing variance 0.05 it is 0.004 (7% of the regret), with 0.5 it is 0.040. Validating on the deployed policy itself with no probing (`v=0`) reveals the error (0.055) but is blind along `Δa = kΔb`: twins `(â, b̂)` on that line have closed-loop gap exactly 0 while their gains differ from `k*` (regret 0.0009 at `â=0.7`; a probe with `v=0.2` exposes them, gap 0.006). So a twin must be scored on state–action data that excite the directions the controller will use; the score is a certificate only for the distribution it was measured on, the covariate-shift structure that inverse-propensity scoring addresses in `audit-weighted-scoring`.
4. **A twin is worth `n_eff = σ²/(vδ²)` real samples, at every accuracy target.** Estimating `b` from `n` real probe steps of variance `v` (`S=nv`), with the twin (bias `δ=b̂−b`) as a ridge prior of weight `κ`: MSE `= (σ²S + κ²δ²)/(S+κ)²`, minimised at `κ*=σ²/δ²` where it equals `σ²/(S+κ*)`. It beats both real data alone (`σ²/S`) and the twin alone (`δ²`), and misweighting by 3× either way loses (`δ=0.3, n=60`: 0.0331 no twin, 0.0243 optimal, 0.0325 at 3κ*, 0.0274 at κ*/3, 0.09 twin only; Monte Carlo matches within 1%). The real-sample saving to reach any MSE target is *exactly* `κ*/v` — 22 steps for `δ=0.3, v=0.5` whether the regret target is `10⁻²` or `10⁻⁴` (113 → 91 versus 11311 → 11289 steps). A twin buys a head start, not a slope: valuable early and irrelevant at scale, `n_eff = 800, 200, 50, 12, 3` for `δ = 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8`.
5. **The head start is a safety margin.** At small `n` the unregularised estimate of `b` sometimes lands near zero, the trained gain blows up and the deployment diverges: 3.1% of runs at `n=10` and 0.5% at `n=20`, mean regret 0.98 and 0.19; with the twin prior (`δ=0.3`) no run diverges and mean regret is 0.024 and 0.019. Regret tracks `½J''(dk*/db)²·MSE` with ratio 0.86, 0.91, 0.97, 0.99 at `n=100…6400`.

## Limitations
Scalar plant; one uncertain parameter (`a` known); a twin trained by certainty equivalence rather than robustly (see `randomized-twin`); iid Gaussian noise; probes are open-loop iid (feedback probes change the information formula); the score-gap results use the stationary distribution. Real robotics has state dimension, nonlinearity, contact and sensor models, none of which are here; the value is in isolating which effects are structural rather than incidental.

## Relevance
For sim-to-real programs: (i) treat *underestimated actuation* as the dangerous twin error and budget for the cliff; (ii) never validate a twin only on passive or closed-loop logs — add designed probing, or the twin can score perfectly and still mistrain the controller; (iii) value the twin in real-sample units (`σ²/(vδ²)`): it substitutes for tens to hundreds of real trials, not thousands, so spend real trials on identifying the twin's bias along control-relevant directions.

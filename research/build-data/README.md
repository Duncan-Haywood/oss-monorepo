# Do open permit records predict how long a permit and the build behind it take, better than each city's own averages?

Research programme (proposal stage), AI-assisted: do open permit feeds predict permit and build duration across cities? See [`PROPOSAL.md`](PROPOSAL.md) and [`LOG.md`](LOG.md). Relates to the construction data warehouse and `ventures/wall-framing-takeoff`.

Milestone 1 is done: `src/permits.py` loads Chicago, San Francisco and Austin onto one schema (`datasheets/`, `results/manifest.json`, `results/lags.json`). Reproduce: `python3 src/permits.py pull --out DIR` then `python3 src/describe.py DIR`. Finding so far: the median permit issue lag is 0 days in all three cities, so the target is zero-inflated.

## Results by milestone

Milestone 2's harness (`src/harness.py`) and milestone 3's LightGBM quantile models (`src/boosted.py`) have been run on the real feeds, test year 2024 (forward split: train to 2022, early-stopping year 2023). Headline (H1 holds): the boosted model beats the per-city, per-permit-type empirical-quantile baseline on pinball loss in 13 of 15 city/target/quantile cells, by 2 to 36% (San Francisco and Austin, both targets, every quantile); in Chicago application-to-issue it wins at the median (-9.7%) and loses at the 0.1 and 0.9 quantiles (+0.3%, +6.5%). The same-day (zero-lag) classifier beats the baseline's Brier score only in Austin and San Francisco application-to-issue, not in Chicago. Full table: [`results/m3_table.md`](results/m3_table.md). Limits: one test year, 3 seeds, no tuning beyond a fixed fast configuration, boosted intervals are not yet conformalised (milestone 4).

Milestone 4 (conformal, `src/conformal_run.py`, `results/conformal_*.json`): H2 is refuted. In test year 2024 conformal coverage of the nominal 80% interval is 0.816 and 0.813 in Austin but 0.860 Chicago a2i, 0.891 SF a2i and 0.834 SF i2c; in Chicago and SF a2i the conformal step changes nothing because same-day permits tie the scores at 0 and the quantile cannot go negative. Where the raw interval under-covers it widens by 3.5 to 22 days and gains 4 to 11 points. Details: [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 8.

Milestone 5 (`src/importance.py`, [`results/m5_table.md`](results/m5_table.md)): H3 holds as worded (k80 = 2 to 4 of 6 to 7 fields; work class leads in Austin and Chicago, permit type in SF a2i, log cost in SF i2c; month and weekday carry nothing), but permutation importance is not a decomposition of the gain over the baseline (sums are 1.7 to 20 times the gain), so the claim is about reliance.

Milestone 6 (`src/transfer.py`, `results/transfer_*_2024.json`): H4 holds. A model trained on the other cities is worse than the held-out city's own per-type baseline in 13 of 15 city/target/quantile cells (+2% to +137%), better only at SF i2c q0.1 (-2.8%), and Austin's transfer interval covers 61 to 63% for a nominal 80%; an own-city model on the same harmonised features does beat the baseline in Austin, so the loss comes from moving between cities. Limits: i2c has one source city. Seattle, added later as a fourth city, is the exception at the lower quantiles: over test years 2022 to 2024 a model trained on SF and Austin beats Seattle's own baseline at q0.1 and q0.5 of issue-to-completion in every year, while application-to-issue transfer is 27 to 36% worse at q0.9 and every transfer interval covers only 55 to 72% for a nominal 80% (paper 4.8; [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), sessions 64, 65, 69). The harmonised permit class cannot carry what kind of work a permit is between cities: Chicago's permit types are review tracks and Seattle's is "building" for 90% of rows ([`results/other_vocab.md`](results/other_vocab.md)). The work type is in the description text, so keyword rules read it; checked against hand labels on held-out 2022 permits, the first rules agree on 60% (Chicago) and 78% (Seattle) of rows and the revised rules on 85% and 95% (`src/desc_eval.py`, [`results/desc_rules_eval.json`](results/desc_rules_eval.json); paper 4.4b; one labeller). A paired bootstrap on the same Seattle 2024 test rows shows what that class buys: it cuts the application-to-issue q0.9 transfer loss by 3.9 days (95% interval 2.8 to 4.9) but adds 2.2 days (0.9 to 3.5) at q0.9 of issue-to-completion ([`results/paired_transfer_seattle_a2i_2024_4city_cascade2_vs_type.json`](results/paired_transfer_seattle_a2i_2024_4city_cascade2_vs_type.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 74). In test year 2023 the issue-time gain repeats (5.6 days, 4.6 to 6.5) and the build-time cost does not (interval spans zero). Where the cost appears, demolition permits carry most of it, the one class whose completion tail is longer in the source cities than in Seattle ([`src/tail_check.py`](src/tail_check.py); session 75). Folding demolition into alteration (`--classes cascade2_nodemo`) cuts that 2024 build-time cost from 3.0 to 1.0 days (paired difference -2.0 days, 95% interval -2.7 to -1.2; demolition rows carry 88% of it) and keeps the issue-time gain ([`results/paired_transfer_seattle_i2c_2024_4city_cascade2_nodemo_vs_cascade2.json`](results/paired_transfer_seattle_i2c_2024_4city_cascade2_nodemo_vs_cascade2.json); session 76).

### Milestone 6 follow-ups: the transfer gap, session by session

Each later session tested one explanation of the build-time transfer gap; one bullet per session (full record: [`LOG.md`](LOG.md)). A new session adds its bullet at the end rather than extending a paragraph.

- Session 77 qualifies that: the gap is right-censoring. A 2024 build-time test row exists only if the permit finished before the pull; censoring Seattle's own history the same way brings its demolition q0.9 from 952 to 580 days (test year 552), and against the uncensored history the sources' demolition tail is the shorter one. A fold rule fixed in advance from history (`--classes cascade2_tailfold`) cuts issue-time transfer q0.9 loss by 13% with Chicago held out and raises it by 8% with Austin held out, so a per-class tail ratio is not a safe merge rule ([`src/truncation_check.py`](src/truncation_check.py); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 77).
- Session 78 checks that on build-time labels the pull sees in full. In Seattle test years 2020 to 2022, folding demolition *costs* 1.7 to 2.5 days at q0.9 (every 95% interval above zero), while scoring only the permits that a pull two years later would have seen reverses the sign in two of the three years. Weighting by the inverse chance of being seen (IPCW) recovers only 10 to 40% of the gap, because 5 to 8% of each cohort takes longer than the longest window. So session 76's fold gain is withdrawn as a censoring artefact, and build-time q0.9 is now scored on mature cohorts only ([`src/censored_eval.py`](src/censored_eval.py); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 78).
- Session 79 reran the work-type class against permit type alone on those mature cohorts. In Seattle the class lowers build-time q0.9 transfer loss by 3.2 to 6.3 days in 2020, 2021 and 2022 (every interval above zero), so session 74's 2.2-day cost was the same censoring artefact; a complete-case score of a two-year window reports a cost in 2020 (-2.3 days). With San Francisco held out the class gains 15.7 days at q0.9; with Austin held out it costs 15.3, and there folding demolition helps (-2.0 days) where in Seattle it hurt. So whether a borrowed model's build-time tail gains from a class feature depends on the city, and needs checking per city on complete labels ([`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 79).
- Session 80 shows the sign holds across years (SF gains 14.4 and 22.7 days at q0.9 in 2020 and 2022; Austin loses 17.2 and 9.8) and finds why. Under permit type alone, the source cities have no rows in the held-out city's main classes, so those classes are unseen. The work-type class fills SF's alteration class (98% of its permits) with 271,672 source rows, and fills Austin's electrical, mechanical and plumbing classes (76% of its permits, 77 to 86% of the cost) with only 42 to 466 Seattle rows whose q0.9 build times are 1.6 to 2.7 times Austin's own ([`src/class_shift.py`](src/class_shift.py); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 80). Session 79's Austin demolition-fold gain does not repeat in 2020 (+1.8 days).
- Session 81 tests a minimum-support rule fixed in advance: a class with fewer than k source training rows is folded into "other" (`--classes cascade2_minsup1000`; k = 1000 chosen on test year 2020 from 100, 1000 and 10,000). In 2021 and 2022 it wins back 9.3 and 4.6 days of Austin's build-time q0.9 transfer cost (61% and 47%, both intervals below zero) and keeps all of San Francisco's and Seattle's gain. But it costs Austin 8.5 and 4.4 days at the median, because a class folded into "other" now shares the sources' catch-all durations instead of staying unseen. So the threshold carries across years while the fold target still needs work ([`results/minsup_summary.json`](results/minsup_summary.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 81).
- Session 82 asked whether the fold target is the problem: folding the same thin classes to a *missing* class (`--classes cascade2_minsupna1000`) instead of "other" removes Austin's median cost (+1.0 and -1.8 days) but gives back the tail gain (-1.2 and +1.1 days at q0.9), because the sources' 721 to 752 thin-class rows, now NaN, still train the branch LightGBM shares between missing and never-seen categories. Dropping those source rows (`cascade2_minsupdrop1000`, post hoc) recovers the tail (-13.0 and -5.4 days, 11.8 and 6.5 better than the missing fold) and brings the median cost back (+4.6, +5.6). Across all four treatments the plain permit-type class still beats every fold rule in Austin at both quantiles in both years, so the class configuration has to be chosen per target city, not from source counts ([`results/minsupna_summary.json`](results/minsupna_summary.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 82).
- Session 83 made that choice from the target city's own previous year: score each candidate class (cascade2, permit type, the drop fold) with the transfer model on the held-out city's year Y-1, which that model never reads, and keep the lowest (`src/select_class.py`). Over 12 cells (Austin, SF, Seattle; 2021 and 2022; q0.5 and q0.9) the pick is within 0.65 days of the best candidate every time, mean regret 0.07 days against 1.7 to 6.6 for any fixed class; it picks permit type for Austin and never for SF, as the test years reward ([`results/select_class_summary.json`](results/select_class_summary.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 83).
- Session 84 scored the same choice on the labels a deployment has on 1 January of the test year, when only 28 to 61% of the target's previous-year permits have completed: mean regret rises to 1.1 to 1.6 days, and the best deployable variant (the target's year Y-2, inverse-probability-of-censoring weights and a significance gate) reaches 0.58 days, within a day of the best in 11 of 12 cells. Its miss is Seattle's 0.9 quantile, where the permits already completed reverse the class contrast with an interval below zero (`src/select_deploy.py`, [`results/select_deploy_summary.json`](results/select_deploy_summary.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 84).
- Session 85 chose from the target's year Y-3 instead (93 to 99% complete on 1 January) and early-stopped the borrowed model on only the source permits completed by then: the selector's mean regret falls to 0.31 days and Seattle's miss goes, but the early-stopping cut raises the best candidate's loss by 1.3 days on average (up to 6.9 in Austin's 0.9 quantile), so the remaining gap is the model, not the choice ([`results/select_deploy_es_summary.json`](results/select_deploy_es_summary.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 85).
- Session 86 built the borrowed model only from what was known on 1 January: source training rows completed by then (98.7 to 99.5% kept) and boosting rounds per quantile fixed from older years (trained on years up to Y-4, early-stopped on Y-3). Austin's 0.9 quantile recovers (+0.79 days against the complete-label model, session 85 +6.87), but the 0.9-quantile models stop after 7 to 28 rounds and the best candidate's loss rises by 1.86 days on average (+10.95 in Seattle 2021), more than session 85's 1.29; the selector stays within 0.36 days of the best ([`results/select_deploy_dep_summary.json`](results/select_deploy_dep_summary.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 86).
- Session 87 early-stopped that model on the sources' year Y-3 instead of carrying a count: the 0.9-quantile models still stop after 8 to 17 rounds in 5 of 6 city-years (34 to 121 with complete labels), yet across 18 model-cells the round count does not track the tail loss (Spearman 0.02). What does is the training cut: dropping only the 0.45 to 0.56% of source permits still open on 1 January, the longest builds, costs San Francisco and Seattle 1.0 to 5.1 days at q0.9 with session 84's early stopping, 44 to 92% of the deployable model's cost there, while Austin gains from it ([`results/rounds_summary.json`](results/rounds_summary.json), [`results/rounds_score_i2c.json`](results/rounds_score_i2c.json), [`results/select_deploy_es3_summary.json`](results/select_deploy_es3_summary.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 87).
- Session 88 weighted the source permits completed by 1 January by the inverse chance of having been seen by then (IPCW, per source city-year from its issue days). In the deployable model (early-stopped on year Y-3) the weights halve the 0.9-quantile cost in San Francisco and Seattle, +2.53 days against the complete-label model where the unweighted version costs +4.74, and win in all 4 city-years. But with session 84's early stopping they give back only 18 to 33% of what the training cut takes, because 55 to 65% of the dropped permits take longer than the longest window any permit of their city-year has on 1 January, so no completed peer can stand for them; that part needs a censored-tail model, not weights ([`results/rounds_score_ipcw_i2c_summary.json`](results/rounds_score_ipcw_i2c_summary.json), [`results/ipcw_reach_i2c.json`](results/ipcw_reach_i2c.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 88).
- Session 89 kept those open permits as lower bounds instead, with Portnoy's (2003) censored-quantile pseudo-rows: each open permit's quantile level comes from a Kaplan-Meier fit (Kaplan and Meier 1958) over its source city and class, and above that level its mass is split between its window and "+infinity". That made the borrowed tail model far worse, not better: +36.4 days at q0.9 in San Francisco and Seattle with complete-label early stopping and +84.9 in the deployable model, against IPCW's +2.53. LightGBM's quantile objective resets each leaf to a weighted percentile of its residuals, so a leaf where open permits hold more than a tenth of the weight lands on their far-right label. Capping that label at the longest completed build halves the damage (+21.8 and +14.5) but still leaves q0.9 predictions up to 25,600 days, because the feeds record builds of 12 to 60 years. All ten predictions were refuted; a boosted model can only use censored rows through a loss that never sets a leaf from their position ([`results/rounds_score_cens_i2c_summary.json`](results/rounds_score_cens_i2c_summary.json), [`results/cens_reach_i2c.json`](results/cens_reach_i2c.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 89).
- Session 90 wrote that loss as a LightGBM custom objective ([`src/cpin.py`](src/cpin.py)): an open permit is charged only while the prediction is below its window, and each leaf is set to the censored loss's own minimiser, a weighted percentile in which an open permit sits at its window. On completed rows alone it reproduces LightGBM's quantile objective (73.27 against 73.17 days). With the open permits kept, the deployable model's q0.9 cost in San Francisco and Seattle falls to +1.63 days (IPCW +2.53, unweighted +4.74; better than IPCW in 4 of 4), and with complete-label early stopping it recovers 47 to 89% of what dropping them costs. Six of seven predictions held; q0.9 predictions still pass ten years in 3 of 24 runs, on at most three permits ([`results/rounds_score_cpin_i2c_summary.json`](results/rounds_score_cpin_i2c_summary.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 90).
- Session 91 put back the permits the feeds never mark complete, which a deployment on 1 January also sees as open: 39% of San Francisco's issued permits, 23% of Seattle's and 8% of Austin's, flat by issue year back to 2010, and in San Francisco mostly still reading "issued" or "expired" years later (permits nobody closed out, not decade-long builds). Read as "at least this long", they wreck the borrowed tail model: +181 days at q0.9 against the complete-label model in San Francisco and Seattle (Seattle +311 and +344). Dropping any permit open more than five years on the decision date, a rule fixed before the run, turns them into a gain: the deployable model's q0.9 change falls to -0.41 days on average (session 90 +1.63), the first deployable variant to match the hindsight model, better in Seattle by 5.7 to 6.9 days and worse in San Francisco by 1.6 to 2.9, and the best on a censored score over all test permits in 4 of 4 city-years ([`results/rounds_score_nc_i2c_summary.json`](results/rounds_score_nc_i2c_summary.json), [`results/sf_never_completed_status.json`](results/sf_never_completed_status.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 91).
- Session 92 swept that threshold (2, 3, 5 and 8 years) and tried to choose it the way a deployment could, from the censored loss on the source cities' permits issued three years before the decision date, as seen on that date. The threshold matters by city: 3 years is best in San Francisco (q0.9 +0.90 and +0.05 days against the complete-label model), 5 years in Seattle (-3.95 and -3.22), and 8 years costs San Francisco 19 to 22 days; over all six city-years with Austin held out, 3 years is the most robust fixed choice (mean -0.80, worst +1.50). But the censored selection score falls with every never-closed record a model keeps, in all six city-years, so it picks 8 years every time: mean regret 11.3 days in San Francisco and Seattle and 60 to 68 in Austin. A censored score cannot select where "open" often means "never recorded" ([`results/rounds_score_sweep_i2c_summary.json`](results/rounds_score_sweep_i2c_summary.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 92).
- Session 93 cut each source at its own 99th-percentile build time instead (Austin and San Francisco about 3.2 years, Seattle 6.4): +0.58 days at q0.9 on average, between the best fixed cuts and no better than five years overall, because a cut set from the sources cannot know what the target city needs. An independent rerun reproduced session 92's models to the digit. The feeds' status fields say what the never-completed permits are: Seattle's and Austin's mostly read expired or void, and 31% of Seattle's read "completed" with no date, while San Francisco's still read "issued" ([`results/rounds_score_ncq_i2c_summary.json`](results/rounds_score_ncq_i2c_summary.json), [`results/closeout_status.json`](results/closeout_status.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 93).
- Session 94 audited the labels themselves: completion dates are builds, not closures (0.04% of dated completions in San Francisco, 0.4% in Seattle and 1.5% in Austin belong to permits the status calls expired, void or withdrawn), but 71% of Seattle's undated "Completed" permits issued 2010 to 2022 say "Closed as Incomplete" in their description, which corrects session 93: they never finished ([`results/label_audit.json`](results/label_audit.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 94).
- Session 95 dropped the dead permits' completion dates from the borrowed model's training. Over the whole training history they are 6.5 to 8.6% of rows, not 1.5%, because 96% of Austin's void and expired permits issued before 2000 carry a completion date exactly 180 days after issue: an expiry stamp, and the most common build-time label in Austin's feed (143,212 rows). Dropping them lengthens the borrowed 0.9-quantile tail and costs Seattle 1.8 to 3.5 days (worse in all 6 seeds) while San Francisco does not move (+0.14, -0.18), so the closures were not what held the deployable model back ([`results/rounds_score_clean_i2c_summary.json`](results/rounds_score_clean_i2c_summary.json), [`results/stamp_check.json`](results/stamp_check.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 95).
- Session 96 split that drop. The 180-day stamps are 82 to 83% of the dropped rows, yet dropping only them moves the 0.9-quantile loss by +0.64 days on average, inside seed noise in 3 of 4 city-years. Seattle's cost comes from the other dead close-outs, mostly Austin's withdrawn permits with long durations: without them Seattle is worse in 6 of 6 seeds (+1.75 and +2.52 days) and San Francisco better in 5 of 6. So the practical rule is to drop the stamps and keep the closures. The staleness cut keeps its city pattern on clean sources (3 years best in San Francisco, 5 in Seattle), and on the 3-year cut cleaning costs 0.68 days ([`results/rounds_score_stamp_i2c_summary.json`](results/rounds_score_stamp_i2c_summary.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 96).
- Session 97 asked whether those closures matter for their length or their class, with a placebo: drop as many live permits with the same build-time distribution (per source city, per decile bin) and keep the closures. The placebo reproduces most of San Francisco's gain (-0.48 and -0.64 days at q0.9, better in 6 of 6 seeds; the closures gave -0.28 and -1.25) and about half of Seattle's cost (+1.47 and +0.73 against +2.52 and +1.75). So the effect is mostly how much long-tail label mass the borrowed model sees, not the close-out class; about one day of Seattle's cost remains specific to the closures, inside one cell's seed spread. The closures over a year long are 40 to 44% of them and carry 64 to 75% of Seattle's cost ([`results/rounds_score_len_i2c_summary.json`](results/rounds_score_len_i2c_summary.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 97).
- Session 98 tried to let the target city pick its own rule, scoring the 3-year cut, the 5-year cut, the stamp drop and the length-thinned tail on the target's own permits issued three years before the decision date. It cannot yet: scored on those completed by the decision date (cut at three years of follow-up) it picks the 3-year cut in all four city-years, right for San Francisco but 5.46 and 3.95 days worse in Seattle (mean regret 2.35); scored by the censored loss it picks the 5-year stamp rule in all four (mean regret 1.79). Neither beats the fixed 5-year cut (mean 72.27 days against 73.47 and 72.91), which stays the safer default ([`results/rounds_score_tsel_i2c_summary.json`](results/rounds_score_tsel_i2c_summary.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 98).
- Session 99 found the score that works: the target city's own permits issued six years before the decision date and completed by it. Scored on those, the selector picks the 3-year cut in San Francisco and the 5-year cut in Seattle in all four city-years, the best of the four rules each time (mean regret 0, mean q0.9 71.12 days against the best fixed rule's 72.27). Five-year-old permits get three of four cells right, and reweighting the three-year-old ones (IPCW) narrows the gap without crossing it: a score needs rows whose follow-up reaches past the shorter cut. The Seattle margins are under a day and the test covers four cells, so this is a rule to check further, not yet a result to bank ([`results/rounds_score_tfol_i2c_summary.json`](results/rounds_score_tfol_i2c_summary.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 99).
- Session 100 tested that selector on six cells it had never seen, and it did not replicate. In test years 2019 and 2020 a 5-year rule beats the 3-year cut in San Francisco and Seattle alike (all four cells), yet every target-cohort score (permits 3, 5, 6 or 8 years old, plain or IPCW-weighted) still picks the 3-year cut: mean regret 2.15 days, against 0.12 for always taking the 5-year cut. With Austin as the target the 3-year cut wins by about 16 days and every selector finds it. Session 99's 4 of 4 was the selector agreeing with a city split that holds only in 2021 and 2022, the years the rules were tuned on. Details: [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 100; `results/rounds_score_rep_i2c_summary.json`.
- Session 101 ran the paired bootstrap session 100 asked for, refitting all eight San Francisco and Seattle city-years with every permit's prediction saved. The target-cohort selector is stable and wrong, not noisy. In all eight, at least 96% of 2,000 resamples of the target's permits keep its pick on the same side. The test-year preference behind the result is just as firm: every 95% interval of the 5-year minus 3-year loss excludes zero, below zero in all of 2019 and 2020 and in Seattle 2021 and 2022, above it in SF 2021 and 2022. So more data would not rescue the selector: its old permits answer a different question from the test year. An independent rerun reproduced session 100's 2019 and 2020 fits to the digit ([`results/rounds_score_oos_i2c_boot.json`](results/rounds_score_oos_i2c_boot.json), [`src/tfol_boot.py`](src/tfol_boot.py); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 101).
- Session 102 added test years 2017 and 2018, and they side with 2021 and 2022, not 2019 and 2020. Dropping source permits open more than three years beats the five-year cut in San Francisco by 8.86 and 2.94 days at q0.9 (95% row intervals well above zero) and in Seattle 2017, and the six-year-old-permit selector picks it in all four cells (regret 0.29 days, against 3.32 for the fixed 5-year cut). Pooled over all twelve San Francisco and Seattle city-years from 2017 to 2022, the selector leaves 0.81 days of regret against 1.53 for the best fixed rule: sessions 100 and 101's failures were concentrated in 2019 and 2020. A finer grid shows the 5-year cut is not a plateau: a 4-year cut is the best fixed rule in these years (84.25 days) and a 6-year cut costs 7.5 days ([`results/rounds_score_early_i2c_boot.json`](results/rounds_score_early_i2c_boot.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 102).
- Session 103 fit the 4-year cut in the other eight city-years (2019 to 2022) and scored the 3-, 4- and 5-year cuts over all twelve. **A 4-year staleness cut is the robust default:** it leaves 0.78 days of mean q0.9 regret against the best of the three cuts in each city-year, against 1.67 for the 5-year cut, 1.73 for the 3-year cut and 0.91 for choosing the cut from the target's six-year-old permits; it is never the worst of the three in any city-year, and its worst miss is 2.31 days (5-year cut 8.86, 3-year cut 5.46). It does not win outright: in 2019 to 2022 alone the 5-year cut is 0.32 days better on average, and Seattle 2020 to 2022 drift toward the longer cut ([`results/rounds_score_grid_i2c_grid.json`](results/rounds_score_grid_i2c_grid.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 103).
- Session 104 set the cut from each source city's own recent records (the 0.99 quantile of build days among its permits completed in the 3 years before the forecast date) and fitted it in the same 12 city-years. **A data-driven cut does worse than the fixed 4-year cut:** 6.65 days of mean q0.9 regret against 0.78, within a day of the best grid cut in only 5 of 12 city-years. The cause is bookkeeping, not construction: in 2017 Seattle closed out 467 permits issued more than 8 years earlier, which pushed its 0.99 quantile from 4.4 to 10.3 years for three forecast years and cost San Francisco 19 to 22 days in 2018 to 2020. Outside those cells it behaves like the 5-year cut. Tail quantiles of open permit feeds track batch close-outs, so keep the fixed 4-year cut ([`results/rounds_score_recent_i2c_recent.json`](results/rounds_score_recent_i2c_recent.json), [`results/open_share_i2c.json`](results/open_share_i2c.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 104).
- Session 105 tried two robust versions of that per-source cut, both fixed before any fit: the 0.95 quantile of recent completions, and the 0.99 quantile after dropping every completion on a day that closed more than 10 permits issued over 8 years earlier. **Neither beats the fixed 4-year cut:** 2.51 and 2.00 days of mean q0.9 regret against 0.78 (session 104's plain quantile: 6.65). Dropping batch close-out days cuts Seattle's 2018 to 2020 cut from 10.2 years to 4.8 to 6.2, but not all of the 2017 sweep fell on batch days. After the fact the two rules split by target city: the short one suits San Francisco, the batch-dropped one suits Seattle (0.91 days against the 4-year cut's 1.08), which a statistic of the source cities cannot know. Keep the fixed 4-year cut ([`results/rounds_score_robust_i2c_robust.json`](results/rounds_score_robust_i2c_robust.json), [`results/robust_cut_i2c.json`](results/robust_cut_i2c.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 105).
- Session 106 tested that default on a target it was not chosen on: Austin, whose builds are shorter than its two sources' (San Francisco and Seattle), in all six test years 2017 to 2022. **The 4-year cut does not carry over:** the 3-year cut wins every Austin year, and the 4-year cut gives up 12.74 days of mean q0.9 loss (5-year 18.12, the batch-dropped per-source cut 21.62). Pooled over all 18 city-years, the 3-year cut leaves 1.16 days of regret against 4.77 for the 4-year cut, and choosing among the 3-, 4- and 5-year cuts by the target's own six-year-old permits leaves 0.61 (worst city-year 4.44 days, against 5.46 for the 3-year cut and 15.53 for the 4-year). New advice: score the cuts on the target's six-year-old permits; without that history, use the 3-year cut ([`results/rounds_score_austin_i2c_austin.json`](results/rounds_score_austin_i2c_austin.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 106).
- Session 107 extended the staleness grid down. With Austin as target, **shorter keeps winning**: the 2-year cut beats the 3-year cut in all six Austin years (mean q0.9 62.67 against 66.00 days), and a 1-year cut, which keeps none of the sources' open permits, beats the 2-year cut in five of six (59.99). For San Francisco and Seattle the 2-year cut loses in all 12 city-years. Putting the 2-year cut on the chooser's menu helps it: pooled over 18 city-years, choosing among the 2- to 5-year cuts by the target's six-year-old permits leaves 1.38 days of regret against 1.72 for the 3- to 5-year chooser and 2.27 for the best fixed cut (3 years), though its worst city-year (SF 2019, 6.96 days) is worse ([`results/rounds_score_floor_i2c_floor.json`](results/rounds_score_floor_i2c_floor.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 107).

Milestone 7 (`src/pooled.py`, [`results/m7_table.md`](results/m7_table.md)): H5 holds on its main claim and its secondary claim is refuted. A model pooled with the other cities plus n rows of the new city reaches that city's full-history per-type baseline only after more than 1000 local rows in 3 of 5 city/target pairs (never within 10,000 in Chicago and SF a2i); and a per-type baseline from just 300 local rows is usually as good as the pooled model, which beats it in 3 of 15 cells. One seed, source rows capped at 200,000 per city.

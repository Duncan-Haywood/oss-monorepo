# Do open permit records predict how long a permit and the build behind it take, better than each city's own averages?

Research programme (proposal stage), AI-assisted: do open permit feeds predict permit and build duration across cities? See [`PROPOSAL.md`](PROPOSAL.md) and [`LOG.md`](LOG.md). Relates to the construction data warehouse and `ventures/wall-framing-takeoff`. Milestone 1 is done: `src/permits.py` loads Chicago, San Francisco and Austin onto one schema (`datasheets/`, `results/manifest.json`, `results/lags.json`). Reproduce: `python3 src/permits.py pull --out DIR` then `python3 src/describe.py DIR`. Finding so far: the median permit issue lag is 0 days in all three cities, so the target is zero-inflated. Milestone 2's harness (`src/harness.py`) and milestone 3's LightGBM quantile models (`src/boosted.py`) have been run on the real feeds, test year 2024 (forward split: train to 2022, early-stopping year 2023). Headline (H1 holds): the boosted model beats the per-city, per-permit-type empirical-quantile baseline on pinball loss in 13 of 15 city/target/quantile cells, by 2 to 36% (San Francisco and Austin, both targets, every quantile); in Chicago application-to-issue it wins at the median (-9.7%) and loses at the 0.1 and 0.9 quantiles (+0.3%, +6.5%). The same-day (zero-lag) classifier beats the baseline's Brier score only in Austin and San Francisco application-to-issue, not in Chicago. Full table: [`results/m3_table.md`](results/m3_table.md). Limits: one test year, 3 seeds, no tuning beyond a fixed fast configuration, boosted intervals are not yet conformalised (milestone 4). Milestone 4 (conformal, `src/conformal_run.py`, `results/conformal_*.json`): H2 is refuted. In test year 2024 conformal coverage of the nominal 80% interval is 0.816 and 0.813 in Austin but 0.860 Chicago a2i, 0.891 SF a2i and 0.834 SF i2c; in Chicago and SF a2i the conformal step changes nothing because same-day permits tie the scores at 0 and the quantile cannot go negative. Where the raw interval under-covers it widens by 3.5 to 22 days and gains 4 to 11 points. Details: [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 8. Milestone 5 (`src/importance.py`, [`results/m5_table.md`](results/m5_table.md)): H3 holds as worded (k80 = 2 to 4 of 6 to 7 fields; work class leads in Austin and Chicago, permit type in SF a2i, log cost in SF i2c; month and weekday carry nothing), but permutation importance is not a decomposition of the gain over the baseline (sums are 1.7 to 20 times the gain), so the claim is about reliance. Milestone 6 (`src/transfer.py`, `results/transfer_*_2024.json`): H4 holds. A model trained on the other cities is worse than the held-out city's own per-type baseline in 13 of 15 city/target/quantile cells (+2% to +137%), better only at SF i2c q0.1 (-2.8%), and Austin's transfer interval covers 61 to 63% for a nominal 80%; an own-city model on the same harmonised features does beat the baseline in Austin, so the loss comes from moving between cities. Limits: i2c has one source city. Seattle, added later as a fourth city, is the exception at the lower quantiles: over test years 2022 to 2024 a model trained on SF and Austin beats Seattle's own baseline at q0.1 and q0.5 of issue-to-completion in every year, while application-to-issue transfer is 27 to 36% worse at q0.9 and every transfer interval covers only 55 to 72% for a nominal 80% (paper 4.8; [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), sessions 64, 65, 69). The harmonised permit class cannot carry what kind of work a permit is between cities: Chicago's permit types are review tracks and Seattle's is "building" for 90% of rows ([`results/other_vocab.md`](results/other_vocab.md)). The work type is in the description text, so keyword rules read it; checked against hand labels on held-out 2022 permits, the first rules agree on 60% (Chicago) and 78% (Seattle) of rows and the revised rules on 85% and 95% (`src/desc_eval.py`, [`results/desc_rules_eval.json`](results/desc_rules_eval.json); paper 4.4b; one labeller). A paired bootstrap on the same Seattle 2024 test rows shows what that class buys: it cuts the application-to-issue q0.9 transfer loss by 3.9 days (95% interval 2.8 to 4.9) but adds 2.2 days (0.9 to 3.5) at q0.9 of issue-to-completion ([`results/paired_transfer_seattle_a2i_2024_4city_cascade2_vs_type.json`](results/paired_transfer_seattle_a2i_2024_4city_cascade2_vs_type.json); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 74). In test year 2023 the issue-time gain repeats (5.6 days, 4.6 to 6.5) and the build-time cost does not (interval spans zero). Where the cost appears, demolition permits carry most of it, the one class whose completion tail is longer in the source cities than in Seattle ([`src/tail_check.py`](src/tail_check.py); session 75). Folding demolition into alteration (`--classes cascade2_nodemo`) cuts that 2024 build-time cost from 3.0 to 1.0 days (paired difference -2.0 days, 95% interval -2.7 to -1.2; demolition rows carry 88% of it) and keeps the issue-time gain ([`results/paired_transfer_seattle_i2c_2024_4city_cascade2_nodemo_vs_cascade2.json`](results/paired_transfer_seattle_i2c_2024_4city_cascade2_nodemo_vs_cascade2.json); session 76). Session 77 qualifies that: the gap is right-censoring. A 2024 build-time test row exists only if the permit finished before the pull; censoring Seattle's own history the same way brings its demolition q0.9 from 952 to 580 days (test year 552), and against the uncensored history the sources' demolition tail is the shorter one. A fold rule fixed in advance from history (`--classes cascade2_tailfold`) cuts issue-time transfer q0.9 loss by 13% with Chicago held out and raises it by 8% with Austin held out, so a per-class tail ratio is not a safe merge rule ([`src/truncation_check.py`](src/truncation_check.py); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 77). Session 78 checks that on build-time labels the pull sees in full. In Seattle test years 2020 to 2022, folding demolition *costs* 1.7 to 2.5 days at q0.9 (every 95% interval above zero), while scoring only the permits that a pull two years later would have seen reverses the sign in two of the three years. Weighting by the inverse chance of being seen (IPCW) recovers only 10 to 40% of the gap, because 5 to 8% of each cohort takes longer than the longest window. So session 76's fold gain is withdrawn as a censoring artefact, and build-time q0.9 is now scored on mature cohorts only ([`src/censored_eval.py`](src/censored_eval.py); [`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 78). Session 79 reran the work-type class against permit type alone on those mature cohorts. In Seattle the class lowers build-time q0.9 transfer loss by 3.2 to 6.3 days in 2020, 2021 and 2022 (every interval above zero), so session 74's 2.2-day cost was the same censoring artefact; a complete-case score of a two-year window reports a cost in 2020 (-2.3 days). With San Francisco held out the class gains 15.7 days at q0.9; with Austin held out it costs 15.3, and there folding demolition helps (-2.0 days) where in Seattle it hurt. So whether a borrowed model's build-time tail gains from a class feature depends on the city, and needs checking per city on complete labels ([`LOG.md`](LOG.md), session 79). Milestone 7 (`src/pooled.py`, [`results/m7_table.md`](results/m7_table.md)): H5 holds on its main claim and its secondary claim is refuted. A model pooled with the other cities plus n rows of the new city reaches that city's full-history per-type baseline only after more than 1000 local rows in 3 of 5 city/target pairs (never within 10,000 in Chicago and SF a2i); and a per-type baseline from just 300 local rows is usually as good as the pooled model, which beats it in 3 of 15 cells. One seed, source rows capped at 200,000 per city. Reproduce M3: `python3 src/permits.py pull --out DIR`, then `python3 src/boosted.py DIR --city sf --target a2i --test-year 2024 --seeds 3` per pair and `python3 src/summarise_m3.py`.

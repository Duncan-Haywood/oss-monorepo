\title{What a Verification Tolerance Costs: Pricing Cheating Margins in Outsourced ML Training}
\author{Anonymous}
\begin{abstract}
A requester pays untrusted workers to train a model; the weights cannot be checked without redoing the work.
We ask for the cheapest payment-and-audit contract that makes full-effort training an equilibrium when the
checks are a refereed re-execution of one step with tolerance $\tau$, a noisy held-out score, and agreement
between redundant workers. On real training (MLPs on scikit-learn digits and breast-cancer data, 20 seeds, and 20 resampled train/test splits) a
tolerance-$\tau$ referee passes a skip-steps worker on 30--67\% of steps at $\tau=10^{-2}$ (about the worst float16 drift),
while the held-out loss it gives up is below one seed standard deviation on digits and negative on breast cancer; loss
is lost only at 2--6$\times$ the worst benign drift, a task-dependent threshold. We solve the contract as a
linear programme, prove a one-sided resolution bound $\max(C,g/p-S)$, and show that against a majority coalition on
one shared cheap trajectory the deterring stake $\max(0,g/p-1)$ does not fall with the number of workers. Two
conjectures fail: a first-order predictor of the degradation misses by 3--100$\times$, and the joint contract beats the
better single check in only 4 of 45 cells.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A requester who outsources training to untrusted workers cannot check the returned weights without redoing the work.
Refereed re-execution of single steps (Verde \cite{arun2025verde}) narrows the dispute to one operator, but it needs a
tolerance $\tau$ because honest hardware disagrees in the last bits; proof-of-learning \cite{jia2021pol} was shown to be
forgeable \cite{zhang2021advpol,fang2022pol}. We ask what the tolerance costs the requester, and what payment contract
makes honest work an equilibrium given a tolerance-$\tau$ audit, a noisy held-out score and redundant workers.
Contributions, each tested on small real training runs or by exhaustive search: (i) a measured \emph{hidden margin}: a
worker that skips every step the referee cannot distinguish from honest work saves a large share of steps at a loss
cost that switches on at a task-dependent threshold (Section~\ref{sec:margin}); (ii) the cheapest contract as a linear
programme, with a proved one-sided bound $\max(C,g/p-S)$ (Section~\ref{sec:contracts}); (iii) against a majority
coalition on one cheap trajectory the deterring stake does not fall with the number of workers
(Section~\ref{sec:coll}); (iv) two refuted conjectures (Section~\ref{sec:neg}).
\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Verifying that training happened.}
Verde \cite{arun2025verde} resolves a dispute by bisection over the training trace with reproducible operators, so a referee
re-executes one step; it does not say what makes anyone dispute, or how much stake a tolerance requires.
Proof-of-learning \cite{jia2021pol} replays logged checkpoints and accepts statistically; spoofing attacks
\cite{zhang2021advpol,fang2022pol} forge cheaper traces that pass. These are the empirical form of our hidden margin, measured as
a break, not priced as an incentive. Zero-knowledge proofs of training \cite{abbaszadeh2024zkpot} remove the tolerance at a
large prover cost and mark the other end of the cost frontier; TOPLOC \cite{ong2025toploc} faces the same tolerance-versus-forgery
trade-off for inference. Refereed delegation to several servers \cite{canetti2011} is secure if one is honest, has no payments,
and is defeated by a unanimous wrong answer, which is the case our collusion section prices.

\paragraph{Eliciting effort and truth with scores.}
Proper scoring rules \cite{gneiting2007} elicit beliefs about a held-out outcome; whether the score of a model identifies the work
behind it is not their question. Peer prediction \cite{miller2005,prelec2004} pays for agreement with peers and needs a common prior;
multi-task and effort-aware variants \cite{dasgupta2013,shnayder2016,kong2019} make informed reporting beat uninformative play, but
their report is a label and a training run has no label per task. The closest prior art is \citet{gao2016peer}: mixing a few
ground-truth checks with peer prediction can hurt. Our agreement result is the training analogue, in a narrower form.

\paragraph{Hidden action and costly verification.}
Costly state verification \cite{townsend1979} is the skeleton of an audit-plus-stake design, with exact verification; ours has a
tolerance. Payments for effort in estimation \cite{cai2015} concern data quality for a statistic, not compute for a trajectory.
Linear contracts are near optimal under hidden action \cite{duetting2019simple}; we compare our frontier with the best linear
contract on the final-model score and find it dearer by orders of magnitude where the score barely moves.
\paragraph{Incentives in federated learning.}
Contract-theoretic incentive design for federated learning \cite{kang2019contract} prices a participant's contributed data and
compute under hidden type, and free-rider attacks and defences \cite{lin2019freeriders} show that a worker can submit updates
that pass aggregation without doing the work. Both treat the submitted update as the object checked; we ask what a referee's
numerical tolerance leaves a cheater, and price the stake that tolerance needs.
\section{Model}\label{sec:model}
A worker runs $T$ steps; honest cost is $C$. An action $a$ skips the steps whose honest update has norm at most
$\tau'$, so its effort is one minus the skipped share and its cost is effort${}\times C$. An audit re-executes $m$
random steps and flags a step iff the worker's checkpoint is farther than the referee tolerance $\tau$ from the
referee's recomputation, so a skipped step is caught iff its update norm exceeds $\tau$. A held-out score is Gaussian
with the measured mean, seed variance and per-example variance, fixed by test-set size $n_{\text{test}}$. A contract
pays $w(\text{signal})\ge -S$ (limited liability). The cheapest contract implementing a target action is
$\min E_{\text{target}}[w]$ subject to incentive compatibility against each other action and individual rationality,
a linear programme in $w$ (\texttt{src/vtm/contract.py}); it equals the closed form $\max(C,C/p-S)$ on a grid and a
$2500\times2500$ brute-force search within 1\%.
\paragraph{A one-sided resolution bound.}
Let
\[ r=\min_{s:P_h(s)>0}\frac{P_a(s)}{P_h(s)},\qquad p=1-r. \]

\emph{Proposition.} Any contract implementing honest against $a$ with $w\ge -S$ costs $\mathbb{E}_h[w]\ge\max(C,\,g/p-S)$.
\emph{Proof.} Incentive compatibility gives $\mathbb{E}_h[w]-\mathbb{E}_a[w]\ge g$. With $f=w+S\ge0$ we have $\mathbb{E}_a[f]\ge r\,\mathbb{E}_h[f]$
(mass of $P_a$ off the support of $P_h$ only adds), so $g\le\mathbb{E}_h[f]-\mathbb{E}_a[f]\le p\,\mathbb{E}_h[f]$, i.e.\ $\mathbb{E}_h[w]\ge g/p-S$.
Individual rationality gives $\mathbb{E}_h[w]\ge C$. $\square$ (Over several deviations take the largest bound.)
\section{The hidden margin on real training (H1)}\label{sec:margin}

\paragraph{Cheaper cheats.} Skipping is not the only shortcut. Two others save real compute and submit a non-zero update:
\emph{subsample} (the gradient of a quarter of the batch) and \emph{stale} (resubmit the previous step's gradient, no backward pass).
The same referee accepts each on $12$ to $14\%$ of steps at $\tau=10^{-2}$, close to skip's $16\%$, so the referee mostly accepts
small-update steps whatever the cheat. The cost differs (Table~\ref{tab:cheats}). Subsample costs at most $0.3$ seed std up to
$\tau=3\times10^{-2}$. Stale costs $+0.0177$ at $\tau=10^{-2}$ ($2.1$ seed std, CI excluding zero), ten times skip, and $+1.55$ at
$10^{-1}$. A tolerance harmless against skipping therefore already admits a harmful stale cheat, which refutes the narrow form of H1
for that cheat. The cause is chaining: an accepted stale gradient becomes the next step's reference, so reuse runs on for $43$ steps on
average (longest $83$) at $10^{-2}$. Allowing reuse only after an honest step (\emph{stale1}) cuts the cost to $+0.00016$, $1\%$ of
stale's. This shows the chain is what costs, not that a no-chain rule can be enforced; the cheater here obeys it by construction.

\section{Single-check and joint contracts (H2, H5)}\label{sec:contracts}
\begin{figure}[t]\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{frontier.png}
\caption{Requester cost per unit of honest compute against $\tau$ for the joint contract and the two single-check alternatives (\texttt{experiments/frontier}).}\label{fig:frontier}\end{figure}
For the honest target, audit-only costs $1.001\,C$ at $\tau=0$ (one audited step) and is infeasible at every
$\tau\ge10^{-4}$ at any stake: skipping the steps below $\tau$ passes every audit and pay cannot tell it from honest work.
Score-only then costs $5.76\,C$ at $n_{\text{test}}=540$, $12.4\,C$ at 100 and $1.23\,C$ at 5,000 (stake $S=C$), and the best
contract linear in the score costs $1.2\times10^{4}\,C$. The joint contract is strictly cheaper than the better single
check in 4 of 45 cells (ratio 0.72--0.81, $n_{\text{test}}=5000$), always where audit-only is infinite, so the audit
helps only where the score is almost precise enough; H2 holds in that narrow form. A requester who accepts the
sanctioned margin sets the target to the skip-below-$\tau'$ action and the referee to $\tau'$; any further skipping is
caught with probability equal to its gain per audited step, and one audited step at $S=C$ already deters (total cost
$0.698\,C$ at $\tau'=10^{-2}$, $0.468\,C$ at $3\times10^{-2}$). \emph{Bound.} Any contract implementing honest effort
against a deviation of gain $g$, with pay floor $-S$ and one-sided resolution $p=1-\min_s P_a(s)/P_h(s)$, costs at least
$\max(C,g/p-S)$ (proof in Section~\ref{sec:model}). The LP never violates it on 2,755 random feasible
instances and equals it on 2,410; it is exact for two actions with a binary audit, but H5 as first worded (a function of
$g,\tau,S$ met within a constant factor) is false: the largest LP/bound ratio is 2,093. For a Gaussian score the bound is
vacuous (1.0 against an LP cost of 5.76).
\section{Redundancy and collusion (H3, H4)}\label{sec:coll}
\emph{Agreement.} With two workers and one shared cheap trajectory, adding an agreement term to pay never raises the
cheapest contract (a refinement) and lowers it in 1,191 of 4,000 random instances; once copying equilibria are excluded,
it lowers it in 451 instances but in 0 of 1,349 with perfect copying ($\kappa=1$), where it is worth nothing beyond the
audit. \emph{Measured agreement on a transformer.} Along the honest trajectory of the transformer run (10 seeds), two
honest workers never disagree at any tolerance ($\delta=0.000$ at $\tau=10^{-3}$ to $10^{-1}$). A cheat built from an
independent quarter batch agrees with the honest gradient with probability $\alpha=0.038/0.122/0.216/0.429$ at
$\tau=10^{-3}/10^{-2}/3\cdot10^{-2}/10^{-1}$, and two independent quarter-batch cheats agree with each other with
$\kappa=0.032/0.102/0.185/0.350$; the seed ranges of $\alpha$ and $\kappa$ do not overlap. A peer contract can use only
the gap $\alpha-\kappa$ (at most $0.079$ here), because an honest worker facing a cheating peer also disagrees with
probability $1-\alpha$. Priced with the measured triple, it is cheaper than audit-only in 2 of the 16 cells ($\tau$, $p\in\{0.1,0.3\}$, $S\in\{0,1\}$) for the independent
quarter-batch cheat, both at $\tau=10^{-1}$ with $p=0.1$ (a saving of 0.12 to 0.14 on a cost of 6.5 to 7.5), and in
none of the 16 cells for a shared-quarter cheat or for the stale cheat. The contract parameters $g$, $p$
and $S$ are chosen, not measured. \emph{Collusion.} With $m$ workers, majority-output acceptance and one independent audit per job (catch
probability $p$, gain $g$), the least stake making all-honest a strong equilibrium is $S^{*}=\max(0,g/p-1)$ for every
$m\ge1$: a coalition of $\lfloor m/2\rfloor+1$ escapes replication with probability one and faces only the audit.
Exhaustive search over 140 cells ($m=1$ to 7) matches the formula to $7\times10^{-10}$. The proposal's $1/m$ law is
therefore wrong here and belongs to a setting where a briber pays each verifier separately. The flat-in-$m$ result
follows from the payoff structure (a theorem, confirmed by the search), and the pay bill grows with $m$, so against a
coordinating coalition replication is dominated by $m=1$ plus the audit. \emph{Side payments.} With heterogeneous savings $g_i$ the least stake is $\max(0,\bar g/p-1)$ with $\bar g$ the $k$-th largest saving ($k=\lfloor m/2\rfloor+1$) when every member must gain, and the mean of the $k$ largest savings when members can split gains. Exhaustive search over 1,080 instances ($g_i\sim U(0,1)$, $p=0.1$, $m\le7$) matches both forms to $9\times10^{-10}$; the transferable-utility stake is never lower and is strictly higher in 816 of 816 instances where the top $k$ savings differ. Means at $m=3/5/7$ are $3.7/4.0/4.0$ (no transfers) and $5.1/5.6/5.8$ (transfers), against $1.3/0.8/0.6$ for a $1/m$ law; neither falls with $m$ (closed forms to $m=51$: about $4.0$ and $6.4$). Savings are drawn, not measured, and transfers are costless. \emph{A bribable auditor.} An auditor paid only for catching cheats earns nothing in the all-honest profile whatever it does, so no catch reward deters collusion; a bond $B$ forfeited when a meta-audit (probability $q<p$) exposes it does. With the stake at the transferable-utility level, exhaustive search over workers plus auditor (440 instances, $m\le6$, $p=0.1$, $q=0.02$) matches $B^\ast=\max(0,\sum_{i\in C}(g_i-q(1+S)))/q$ to $5\times10^{-10}$, where $C$ is the $k$ largest savings plus every further worker with $g_i>q(1+S)$. Below the worker-only stake no bond suffices (432 of 432). The mean bond grows with $m$ (closed form: 17 at $m=1$, 134 at $m=7$, 923 at $m=51$), and is zero when $q=p$. Replication therefore raises the price of an honest auditor; $q$ and $B$ are parameters, not measured. \emph{Imperfect coordination.} If the cheaters' outputs coincide only with probability $\kappa$ (all-or-nothing; a lone cheater always coincides), the least stake is $\max(0,g/(1-\kappa(1-p))-1)$; exhaustive search over 270 cells ($m\le6$, $\kappa\in\{1,.75,.5,.25,0\}$) matches it to $8\times10^{-10}$. At $g=0.5$, $p=0.1$ it is $4.00$, $0.54$ and $0$ for $\kappa=1$, $0.75$ and $\le0.5$ ($m\ge2$). Since a shared-quarter cheat has $\kappa=1$ on a transformer, the conservative design assumes $\kappa=1$.
 \emph{Shared-mode copying.} If each cheater lands on the shared trajectory only with probability $\kappa$ (otherwise its output agrees with nobody), the least stake is $\max(0,g/(1-a(1-p))-1)$ with $a=\kappa\Pr[1+\mathrm{Bin}(m-1,\kappa)>m/2]$; exhaustive search over 84 cells matches it to $9\times10^{-10}$. For $\kappa<1/2$ it falls to zero as $m$ grows ($g=0.9$, $\kappa=0.35$: $0.31$ at $m=1$, $0$ from $m=21$); for $\kappa>1/2$ it settles at a positive floor ($1.17$ at $\kappa=0.65$, $2.21$ at $0.8$, against $8.0$ at $\kappa=1$) and alternates with the parity of $m$. Replication therefore deters a coordinating majority exactly when copying is imperfect and rarer than one half, in a model where the copy is a single shared mode. \emph{Pricing replication.} Charging each replica the job's compute and each unit of stake a capital cost $\lambda$ per job, replication beats one worker only if $\lambda(S^\ast(1)-mS^\ast(m))>m-1$; over $g\in\{0.5,0.9\}$, $\kappa\in\{0.2,\dots,1\}$, odd $m\le201$ the smallest break-even $\lambda$ is $16$ (finite in 3 of 12 cells, never at $\kappa=1$), so as a deterrent alone replication does not pay at any realistic lock cost; its value is robustness to a single faulty worker, which stake pricing does not capture. Pricing the audit too (cost $c\in\{0.1,0.5,2\}$ compute units per unit probability, 72 cells, $m\le51$, $p$ on a 0.01 grid), the cost-minimising design uses one worker in every cell: deterrence is bought with audit probability, not replicas. \emph{Faulty workers.} Let each worker independently return a unique faulty output with probability $f$, a lone faulty output being caught by the audit with probability $p$ and otherwise accepted at loss $L$ compute units; with $m>1$ a fault never forms a majority and the job is re-run when no majority is correct. Over 648 cells ($f\in\{.01,.05,.2\}$, $L\in\{10,100,1000\}$, audit cost $c\in\{.5,2,5,20\}$, odd $m\le51$, $p$ on a 0.01 grid) the optimum is $m=1$ (422 cells) or $m=3$ (226 cells) and nothing larger; no cell with $c=0.5$ picks replication, and at $c=2$ only some with $L\ge100$ do. Replication is thus a reliability tool bought as a triple against faults, not a deterrent. $f$, $L$, $c$ are parameters, not measured, and faults are independent (a shared bug defeats majority voting). To place $f$, we flipped one bit of one weight at a random step of a 600-step run of a 32,801-parameter transformer (500 flipped runs, 8 seeds): a flip changes the final held-out loss by more than $0.01$ (or makes it non-finite) with probability $0.045$ for a uniform bit, almost all from the four high exponent bits ($0.35$, of which $0.24$ non-finite), against $0$ of 100 for mantissa bits 0 to 15; 4 threads against 1 differ by a relative $7\times10^{-8}$ in the weights. With $q$ the probability a job takes a flip, $f=0.045\,q$, and the milestone 39 optimiser picks $m>1$ in 0 of 48 cells at $q=10^{-3}$ and 28 of 48 at $q=0.1$. One tiny model, weights only; $q$ is not measured. \emph{Silent bit flips.} In a 32,801-parameter transformer a single flipped bit of one fp32 weight, gradient element or activation harms the held-out loss (change above $0.01$ or non-finite) in 1\% to 3\% of flips, almost all of them high-exponent bits; fp32, bf16 and fp16 storage differ little (harm $0.22$ to $0.25$ among high-exponent flips). A bound $|w|\le K\max|w_0|$ on the submitted weights, with $\max|w_0|$ taken from the public initialisation, catches 15 of 15 harmful flips at every $K\le100$, but an adversary planting a value of $a\max|w_0|$ harms $0.05$ of jobs at $a=2$ and $0.30$ to $0.60$ at $a\ge4$ ($n=20$ per $a$): the check filters faults and does not deter a strategic cheater (milestones 40 to 45; one model, weights-only).
\section{Negative results (H6)}\label{sec:neg}
Two predictors that see only the honest runs: (A) the first-order descent forgone by skipped steps, and (B) the loss of an
honest worker trained for the shortened number of steps. Both miss: only 2 of 9 tolerances have degradation above one seed
standard deviation, A is out by $123\times$ and $12\times$ there, B by $3.0\times$ and $1.00\times$ (trivial: every step skipped),
and the ratio is in $[0.5,2]$ in 1 of 9 settings. Both overshoot because skipped steps are exactly the small-update steps,
whose loss the remaining training recovers. A model of recovery is the open constructive claim.
A third predictor (C) trains the honest worker for the share of \emph{path length} that skipping leaves, with no fitted parameter.
On the four non-trivial rows above one seed standard deviation (digits $\tau=0.1$; transformer $\tau=0.03$ and $0.1$) it lies at $0.62\times$,
$0.61\times$ and $0.69\times$ the measured degradation (B: $2.4\times$ to $6.7\times$), but at the small tolerances where the cost is
detectable yet below one standard deviation it undershoots ($0.00\times$ to $0.49\times$), so it is inside $[0.5,2]$ on half of the detectable rows only.
C does not carry to other cheats: on the transformer's quarter-batch and reused-gradient cheats it is inside $[0.5,2]$ on none of 8 rows
(subsample $7\times$ over at $\tau=0.1$; stale $0.01\times$ to $0.03\times$ of the measured $0.018$ to $1.55$), because it prices an accepted
step by the distance forgone and ignores what the cheat does instead. A fitted scalar $kC$ does not repair this either: the digits undershoot falls from $9.5\times$
to $1.6\times$ with $\tau$, and $k=3.6$ puts the transformer's scored rows $2.2\times$ and $2.5\times$ over.
Three further runs (10 seeds each) say why no norm-based predictor can work for these cheats. (i) Replacing the accepted error by a random direction of the same norm removes the damage: at $\tau=0.1$ subsample costs $+0.0003$ against $+0.0024$ (about $9\times$ less) and stale $+0.0007$ against $+1.55$, although the accepted error is 16\% and 24\% of the path length, so the referee's norm test certifies a path share and not a loss bound. (ii) For subsample the error component orthogonal to the honest gradient reproduces the real damage (ratio $0.97$ to $1.04$ at $\tau=10^{-2}$ to $10^{-1}$), the parallel component none ($\le 0.07$); the orthogonal-arm intervals contain zero at all three tolerances, so this locates the mean effect, not a detectable one. (iii) That orthogonal error has a Rayleigh quotient about $320\times$ a random direction's (95\% range over seeds $297$ to $361$), about 18\% of the estimated $\lambda_{\max}$, but the second-order sum $\tfrac12\eta^2 e^{\top}He$ overshoots the measured damage $44\times$ to $110\times$, because later honest steps damp most of the injected error. The mechanism is identified (a structured error in sharp directions); a closed-form bound is not, and we offer none.
\section{Limitations}
Three small scikit-learn tasks and plain SGD, plus one 30k-parameter character-level transformer (10 seeds, 600 and 3,000 steps, loss not converged; the cost at $\tau=10^{-2}$ is unchanged when the held-out block moves per seed, $+0.0016$ against $+0.0017$; see Table~\ref{tab:tf}), no realistic scale; the skip shortcut only for the dense results (on the transformer a reused-gradient cheat costs 2.1 seed std at $\tau=10^{-2}$, so the margin depends on the cheat; the cost comes from chains of consecutive reuses, 43 steps on average, and disappears, $+0.00016$, when reuse after a reused step is disallowed); one reorder
referee; two workers for agreement and pure strategies for collusion; contract parameters ($\delta,\alpha,\kappa$, stakes)
are swept rather than measured on real trajectories; the related-work section covers verification, scoring rules, hidden-action contracts and federated-learning incentives but not trusted-hardware attestation or Byzantine-robust aggregation, and the draft is in the
two-column \texttt{sigconf} style rather than the single-column EC'26 style files, whose page limit is not yet checked.

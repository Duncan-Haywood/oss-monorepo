# Particle twin: a Gaussian-sensor twin sizes a particle filter on its average ESS, which outliers barely move, while the tail collapses and the estimate gets 45–420× worse

Pure Python, no dependencies. Real system: a bootstrap particle filter that weights prior particles by the Gaussian likelihood `exp(−(y−x)²/(2R))` of a sensor that occasionally returns an outlier (a Gaussian scale mixture: `N(0, R)` w.p. 0.95, `N(0, 100R)` w.p. 0.05). The twin's sensor is the datasheet Gaussian `N(0, R)`. Results (all from `experiments/results.txt`; for `P = R = 1`, the large-`N` effective-sample-size fraction is exactly `ρ(y) = c₀ exp(−a y²)` with `c₀ = √(R(2P+R))/(P+R) = 0.866` and `a = P/((P+R)(2P+R)) = 1/6`, checked against numerical integration in the tests and against simulation): (1) the *mean* ESS fraction hides the problem: 0.671 in the twin against 0.645 with outliers (4% lower, and the same 5% lower at every dimension `d` up to 16, where both are ≈ 0.0016 and a twin-sized `N` for a target mean ESS is off by only that factor); (2) the *tail* does not: `P(ESS < 1% of N)` is 2.5e-4 in the twin and 3.1e-2 with outliers (120×; 750× at 0.3%, 4000× at 0.1%), and it saturates at the outlier probability `ε` once `κ ≳ 30`; (3) sizing `N` from the twin for `ESS ≥ 50` with failure probability `δ = 0.01` gives 528 particles, and on the real sensor that fails 4.4% of steps instead of 1.1% (13.3% vs 9.4% at `δ = 0.1`); the large-`N` formula says the real sensor would need about 6e13 particles for `δ = 0.01`, but it only applies while `N·ρ(y)` is not small (at `y = 8` it predicts ESS 0.04 and simulation gives 4.2: one or two particles carry all the weight), so the honest reading is "no feasible N", not a number; (4) the estimator damage: mean squared deviation of the particle posterior mean from the exact Kalman update is 0.019 (twin) vs 0.848 (real) at `N = 50`, 0.0046 vs 0.679 at `N = 200`, 0.00097 vs 0.408 at `N = 1000`: ×45, ×146, ×421. The twin's error falls like `1/N`; the real error barely moves with `N` because the outliers lie outside the prior particle cloud.

```bash
cd research/particle-twin
PYTHONPATH=src python3 -m unittest discover -s tests -v   # 11 tests, about 1 s
PYTHONPATH=src python3 experiments/run.py                 # about 20 s; output in experiments/results.txt
```

**Builds on.** The sensor-simulation and sim-to-real perception direction of ARPG (<https://arpg.colorado.edu/>) and the field-robot state-estimation setting of RECUV (<https://www.colorado.edu/recuv/>); no specific paper from either group is reproduced and nothing here is affiliated with or endorsed by them. The bootstrap filter is Gordon, Salmond & Smith (1993); ESS as a degeneracy diagnostic is Kong, Liu & Wong (1994); weight collapse with dimension is Snyder, Bengtsson, Bickel & Anderson (2008). Companion to `gate-twin` (the same outlier model against an innovation gate) and `filter-twin`.

Stylised: simulated "real" sensor, no recorded data; one scalar measurement update with prior particles drawn from the exact prior and a correct `R` in the filter's weights; independent state dimensions; the large-`N` formulas are asymptotic and are not valid when `N·ρ` is small (Item 4 in the whitepaper quantifies where); no resampling dynamics, proposal improvement (auxiliary or Gaussian-mixture proposals) or likelihood tempering, all of which change the tail results. The twin is a deliberately naive baseline: a twin with outliers reproduces the real numbers by construction. MIT.

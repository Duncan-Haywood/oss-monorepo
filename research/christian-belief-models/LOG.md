# Research log: christian-belief-models

## 2026-11-12 · Milestone 6 (part): why does a transferred model miss a subgroup's level, and can the items the subgroup was asked correct it? (predictions written before the run; outcome below)

- Why: milestone 1 found that models trained on one group rank another group's respondents almost as well but miss its *level* (third-generation to foreign-born Catholics: up to 11 points). Milestone 6 asks for a bound on a transferred model's error. The level error splits exactly: b<sub>j</sub> = E<sub>T</sub>[f<sub>j</sub>] &minus; E<sub>T</sub>[Y<sub>j</sub>] = A<sub>j</sub> + B<sub>j</sub>, where A<sub>j</sub> = E<sub>T</sub>[f<sub>j</sub> &minus; p<sub>S,j</sub>] is source misfit where the target lives (covariate shift; estimable with no target label as the importance-weighted source residual E<sub>S</sub>[r(X)(f<sub>j</sub> &minus; Y<sub>j</sub>)], r = dT/dS from a domain classifier) and B<sub>j</sub> = E<sub>T</sub>[p<sub>S,j</sub> &minus; p<sub>T,j</sub>] is conditional (concept) shift, which needs the target's answers to j. So |b<sub>j</sub>| &le; |A<sub>j</sub>| + |B<sub>j</sub>|, with |A<sub>j</sub>| &le; &radic;(1 + &chi;<sup>2</sup>(T&Vert;S)) &Vert;f<sub>j</sub> &minus; p<sub>S,j</sub>&Vert;<sub>S</sub>. The useful question is whether B<sub>j</sub> for the item a subgroup was *not* asked can be read off the items it *was* asked. `experiments/m6_shift_bound/run.py` fits gradient boosting (the milestone-1 model) on a source group for each of the 12 items (13 for Catholic-to-Catholic pairs) and imputes each item for a target group, over 20 source&rarr;target pairs: Catholic generation 3&rarr;1, 3&rarr;2, 1&rarr;3, non-Hispanic&rarr;Hispanic Catholics, all 12 ordered pairs of Catholic, Evangelical, Mainline and Black Protestant, and each tradition &le;2014&rarr;2016&ndash;24. For each held-out item j it compares estimators of the target's share that use only the target's covariates and its answers to the *other* items: E0 the transferred model as is; E1 the source share; IW, E0 minus the label-free A&#770;<sub>j</sub>; E2, E0 shifted on the logit scale by the median of the other items' recalibration intercepts &delta;<sub>k</sub>; E3, shifted by &Sigma;&rho;<sub>jk</sub>&delta;<sub>k</sub>/&Sigma;|&rho;<sub>jk</sub>|, &rho;<sub>jk</sub> the weighted correlation of the two items' predicted logits over the target's rows (label-free, so a negatively related item such as ever-divorced lends its shift with the sign flipped). Truth is the target's observed weighted share. A two-pair `--quick` run checked the code; these predictions were written before its output was read.
- Predictions: (1) covariate shift explains little: median |A&#770;<sub>j</sub>| under 1 point, and |A&#770;<sub>j</sub>| &lt; |B&#770;<sub>j</sub>| in at least 75% of cells, so IW is within 0.3 points of E0 on mean absolute error; (2) the naive transfer E0 misses by at least 3 points on average over cells and beats the source share E1; (3) E3 cuts E0's mean absolute error by at least 25%, with a pair-cluster bootstrap 95% interval for the paired difference excluding 0; (4) E2 helps less than E3, because shifts differ in sign across items (milestone 1: foreign-born Catholics are more certain of God but less likely to believe in an afterlife than the model expects); (5) the label-free correction size |E3 &minus; E0| ranks cells by actual |b<sub>j</sub>| with Spearman &ge; 0.4; (6) the period pairs gain least, since the items that moved fastest (same-sex relations and marriage) moved apart from the rest. Refuted, with the reading that a subgroup's asked items carry its unasked item's level shift, if E3 is not better than E0 on mean absolute error (interval includes 0 or E3 worse).
- **Outcome** (`experiments/m6_shift_bound/results/cells.csv`, `summary.md`; 241 cells over 20 pairs, about 9 minutes on 4 cores; intervals resample pairs, 2000 draws). Mean absolute error of the target's share in points: E0 5.59, E1 10.90, IW 5.25, E2 5.18, E3 5.24. Paired with E0: IW &minus;0.33 (95% &minus;0.69 to +0.04), E2 &minus;0.41 (&minus;0.96 to +0.16), E3 &minus;0.35 (&minus;0.74 to +0.01); E3 closer than E0 in 59% of cells. Median |b| 4.08 points, median |A&#770;| 0.83, median |B&#770;| 3.45. (1) **Held in its first two parts, missed narrowly in the third**: median |A&#770;| 0.83 points (&lt; 1) and |A&#770;| &lt; |B&#770;| in 77% of cells (&ge; 75%), but IW is 0.33 points better than E0, just past the 0.3 predicted; the gain is all in tradition pairs (5.73 &rarr; 5.18), where |A&#770;| runs 1 to 4 points, and none in generation or period pairs (|A&#770;| about 0.2). (2) **Held**: E0 misses by 5.59 points and beats the source share by 5.3 (3.9 to 6.8). (3) **Refuted**: E3 cuts E0's error by 6%, not 25%, and its interval reaches +0.01. (4) **Refuted**: E2 is no worse than E3 (E3 &minus; E2 +0.06, &minus;0.31 to +0.35); the signed weighting buys nothing. (5) **Refuted**: Spearman(|E3 &minus; E0|, |b|) = 0.11; the correction's size does not say which cells are badly off, though its sign is right in 71% of cells. (6) **Held**: period pairs gain nothing (E3 6.93 vs E0 6.83). **The refutation rule was met**: a subgroup's answers to the items it was asked do not carry a usable estimate of the unasked item's level shift.
- Reading: a transferred model's level error is mostly concept shift, item by item. Covariate shift, the part importance weighting can fix without any target label, is under a point for immigrant generations, Hispanic Catholics and later decades, and one to four points between traditions. The rest (median 3.45 points) is the target group answering *this* item differently from source respondents with the same profile, and that difference does not travel between items: the items whose shift E2/E3 fixed best were the sexual-morality cluster (premarital sex 5.5 &rarr; 3.3, same-sex relations 5.9 &rarr; 3.4, cohabitation 5.9 &rarr; 3.0), which move together, while abortion (4.6 &rarr; 6.2), divorce law (7.0 &rarr; 9.2) and adultery (2.3 &rarr; 3.5) got worse because their shifts run against the rest. For the bound this means |b<sub>j</sub>| &le; |A<sub>j</sub>| + |B<sub>j</sub>| has a label-free first term and a second term that only target answers to j itself identify. For practice: before rotating an item off a subgroup's ballot, keep a calibration subsample that is asked it; the asked items' shifts are not a substitute. Caveats: one boosted model and one seed; the domain classifier's weights have a median effective sample of a third of the source; tradition pairs share respondents' years, so period and tradition shifts mix; cells within a pair are not independent (hence the pair bootstrap). Next: how large a target calibration subsample makes a difference estimator (E0 plus the subsample's mean residual; prediction-powered inference, Angelopoulos et al. 2023, Science, doi:10.1126/science.adi6000) beat the subsample's own mean, by item, which is the number a survey designer needs.

## 2026-11-12 · Milestone 3 (part): does giving the mixture the survey period let it recover a held-out ballot? (predictions written before the run; outcome below)

- Why: the mixture entry (below) read the near-tie of MICE and the year-free DPMPM (4.66 and 4.81 points, both worse than interpolation's 3.43) as evidence that the barrier is missing period information, not the imputer's class. That reading is testable: give the same sampler the period. `experiments/m3_period_mixture/run.py` repeats `m3_mixture` exactly (Catholics, 13 items, 307 item-years, K = 80, 400 burn-in sweeps, m = 10 imputations 30 sweeps apart, the same seeds) with one added categorical variable observed for every respondent, the survey decade (`--period decade`), and as a second arm five-year bins (`--period half`). The exact year is still not a variable, so the held-out year's respondents can only borrow the item from same-period respondents in other years whose ballot asked it, through period-specific classes. Where an item was asked in no other year of the held-out year's period (same-sex marriage 1988 and 2004) the period carries nothing and the two models should agree. A one-item smoke run (same-sex marriage, 2022, 24 sweeps) was done to check the code; these predictions were written after it and before the full runs.
- Predictions: (1) the decade mixture's mean absolute year-level error is at least 0.5 points below the year-free mixture's 4.81; (2) the gain is concentrated on the two items whose meaning moved: same-sex marriage below 12 points (year-free 20.3) and same-sex relations below 7 (10.5); (3) it still does not beat interpolation (3.43) on mean error, because a decade pools years that differ by up to 9 years of drift; (4) Rubin 95% coverage rises from 81.8% to at least 88% of item-years; (5) the five-year arm beats the decade arm on same-sex relations but not on mean error over all items (fewer same-period years per bin, so more noise). Refuted, and with it the reading that missing period information is what holds the joint model back, if the decade mixture is no better than the year-free mixture on mean error.
- **Outcome** (`experiments/m3_period_mixture/results/summary.md`, `period_mixture_decade.csv`, `period_mixture_half.csv`; 307 item-years each, 258 and 235 CPU-minutes on 4 cores; occupied classes median 56 and 55 of K = 80). Mean absolute year-level error in points: decade mixture 3.55 (median 2.64; Rao-Blackwellised 3.54), five-year mixture 3.59 (3.53), against the year-free mixture 4.81, MICE 4.66, plain hgb 3.87, hgb_shift 3.38, decade mean 3.54, interpolation 3.43. Paired with the year-free mixture on the same item-years: decade &minus;1.26 points (item-year bootstrap 95% interval &minus;1.58 to &minus;0.94), five-year &minus;1.22 (&minus;1.53 to &minus;0.93); closer on 66% and 67% of item-years. (1) **Held**: 1.26 points better, well past the 0.5 predicted. (2) **Held**: same-sex marriage 11.5 points (year-free 20.3, under 12 predicted), same-sex relations 5.7 (10.5, under 7 predicted). (3) **Held**: it does not beat interpolation (3.55 vs 3.43; closer on 50% of item-years). (4) **Held**: Rubin 95% coverage 91.5% (year-free 81.8%), same-sex marriage 3 of 5 years (0 of 5), same-sex relations 73% (40%), at about the same half-width (7.5 vs 7.6 points); Winkler score 25.0 against 44.9. (5) **Refuted in its first half**: the five-year arm is not better on same-sex relations (5.9 vs 5.7); it is, as predicted, not better on mean error (3.59 vs 3.55). The refutation rule was not met. Averaged with interpolation, the decade mixture gives 3.15 points, the lowest point error in the programme (earlier best `combo` 3.23); with the drift interval it covers 95.1% (worst item 79%) at a Winkler score of 21.5, level with `combo` (21.1).
- Reading: one variable, the survey period, takes a quarter off the mixture's error and nearly closes the gap to the neighbour baselines, which confirms the mixture entry's reading that missing period information, not the imputer's class, held both principled imputers back. But the period-aware mixture lands on the decade mean (3.55 vs 3.54), not below it: as an estimator of a wholly unseen year's share, it has learned about what the period's other ballots already say. The other answers rank respondents well (milestone 1) but add nothing to the level beyond the period. The classes do not split by period: a median 2% of the held-out year's respondents sit in classes mostly drawn from their own decade, so the period works by tilting assignment among mixed classes. Finer bins buy nothing (five-year and decade within 0.04 points), and the six decade item-years with no other asked year in their period (same-sex marriage 1988 and 2004 among them) stay bad (10.2 points). For practice: a joint imputer for a rotated GSS item should carry the period; the honest product is still neighbour-anchored (now `combo_pmix`, 3.15) with the drift interval. Caveats: one chain per item-year; the mixture is unweighted; demographics binned; Kish effective n, not the GSS design; `same_period_n` counts only held-out-eligible years (asked of at least 150). Next: milestone 5's local-model baseline, or a mixture with a smooth period effect (neighbouring decades sharing strength) for the end-of-run years.

## 2026-11-11 · Milestone 3 (part): does Bayesian mixture imputation (Si and Reiter's DPMPM) recover a held-out ballot? (predictions written before the run; outcome below)

- Why: the proposal names two principled baselines for milestone 3, MICE (2026-11-10) and the Dirichlet-process mixture of product multinomials of Si and Reiter (2013), which earlier entries deferred because the GSS host refused the proxy. It answered this session (checksums match `data/SHA256SUMS`). `src/dpmpm.py` is a numpy blocked Gibbs sampler for the truncated mixture (Dunson and Xing 2009; Ishwaran and James 2001): all variables categorical, missing cells ignored under MAR, so no separate feature imputation. `experiments/m3_mixture/run.py` repeats the held-out-year design (Catholics, 13 items, item-years asked of at least 150, `year` not a variable) with the raw item codes and binned demographics, the target as one more variable missing for the held-out year, K = 80 classes, 400 burn-in sweeps, m = 10 imputations 30 sweeps apart, and Rubin's rules as in the MICE entry. A timing run (weekly attendance, 2010) found K = 30 fully occupied (30 of 30, alpha about 10), so K was raised to 80 (about 64 occupied); the 2010 share moved by under 1.2 points across K = 30, 50, 80. These predictions were written after that timing run and before the full run.
- Predictions: (1) the mixture's mean absolute year-level error is between 3.8 and 5.0 points: better than MICE (4.66) because the classes carry interactions a main-effects logistic model lacks, but not better than plain hgb (3.87); (2) it does not beat interpolation (3.43); (3) its Rubin 95% intervals cover the observed share in under 85% of item-years and in at most 40% of same-sex-marriage years, the item still worst with error above 10 points; (4) the drift-aware conformal interval of the last entry, put around the mixture, covers at least 90% overall with every item at least 70%. Refuted, and with it the reading that no year-free model beats carrying the neighbours, if the mixture beats interpolation on mean error.
- **Outcome** (`experiments/m3_mixture/results/mixture.csv`, `summary.md`; 307 item-years, 13 items, 211 CPU-minutes on 4 cores; occupied classes median 53, max 69 of K = 80, so the truncation did not bind). Mean absolute year-level error in points: `dpmpm` 4.81 (median 3.49), its Rao-Blackwellised share 4.76, against MICE logreg 4.66, plain logit 4.06, plain hgb 3.87, hgb_shift 3.38, decade mean 3.54, interpolation 3.43. (1) **Refuted in part**: the error is inside the predicted 3.8 to 5.0 band, but the mixture is not better than MICE (4.81 vs 4.66; closer on 48% of item-years) and is worse than plain hgb (closer on 37%). (2) **Held**: closer than interpolation on 43% of item-years, 1.4 points worse on average. (3) **Held**: Rubin 95% intervals cover 81.8% of item-years and 0 of 5 same-sex-marriage years, which is the worst item at 20.3 points (interpolation 5.8); same-sex relations 10.5 (interpolation 2.9), covered in 40% of years. (4) **Half held**: the drift-aware conformal interval around the mixture covers 93.8% overall, but its worst item is same-sex relations at 63%, under the 70% predicted. - Reading: the two textbook principled imputers, chained equations and a Bayesian latent-class mixture, are built on opposite assumptions (linear conditionals vs a joint model with every interaction carried by classes), yet they land within 0.15 points of each other on a held-out ballot (4.66 and 4.81), both about 1.3 points worse than carrying the neighbouring years' shares and with the same over-confident intervals (82 to 83% coverage at a nominal 95%, near zero on same-sex marriage). That agreement is the result: the barrier is not the imputation model's class but the missing period information. Milestone 3 now has its full baseline set: on this test no year-free imputer, simple or principled, beats the neighbours, and the honest product remains the neighbour-anchored `combo` with the drift interval (2026-11-11, entry below). Caveats: one chain per item-year with no convergence diagnostic beyond the occupied-class count; the mixture is unweighted (as in Si and Reiter) while the shares are weighted; demographics were binned into 5 or 6 levels; Kish effective n, not the GSS design. Next: the step in milestone 3 these baselines leave open is a period-aware joint model (for example the mixture with decade as a variable, so classes can be period-specific), then milestone 5's local-model baseline.

## 2026-11-11 · Milestone 3 (part): can an interval for an unseen ballot's share be made honest by pricing in period drift? (predictions written before the run; outcome below)

- Why: the MICE entry (below) found Rubin 95% intervals cover the held-out year's observed share in 83% of item-years overall but 20 to 40% on the two items whose trend broke, because they carry within-model uncertainty only. The GSS host (gss.norc.org) refused this session's proxy (403), so this step uses only the committed per-item-year CSVs (`m3_period_shift`, `m3_mice`): no microdata is needed to recalibrate an interval around an imputed share already computed. `experiments/m3_drift_intervals/run.py` builds leave-one-item-out split-conformal intervals (Vovk, Gammerman and Shafer 2005; Lei et al. 2018) around three point imputations (`interp`, `hgb_shift`, `mice_logreg`) and their average `combo` = (interp + hgb_shift) / 2. For the item being scored, the 95% quantile of the scores of the other 12 items' item-years sets the width. Three scores: `const` (|error|, a fixed width), `samp` (|error| / s, s the binomial sampling sd of the year's share at its n), and `drift` (|error| / sqrt(s<sup>2</sup> + d<sup>2</sup>)), where d is a drift scale from the item's *other* asked years only: the per-year change between the two neighbouring asked years (both sides if the year is interior, the two nearest on one side if not) times the distance to the nearest asked year.
- Predictions: (1) `drift` intervals around `hgb_shift` cover 90 to 97% of item-years overall; (2) their worst single-item coverage is at least 70% (Rubin MICE: 20% on same-sex marriage); (3) at matched overall coverage `drift` has a smaller median half-width than `const`; (4) `samp` alone under-covers the fast-moving items (below 60% on same-sex marriage or same-sex relations), as Rubin did; (5) `combo` has a lower mean absolute error than both its parts (interp 3.43, hgb_shift 3.38). Refuted, as a way to make intervals honest where opinion moved, if `drift` worst-item coverage is below 60%.
- **Outcome** (`experiments/m3_drift_intervals/results/summary.md`, `intervals.csv`; 307 item-years, 13 items, under 5 s, no microdata). Around `hgb_shift`, leave-one-item-out 95% intervals cover: `const` 94.1% (worst item 60%, same-sex marriage), `samp` 95.1% (worst 60%), `drift` 95.1% (worst 83%, divorce law; same-sex marriage 5 of 5 years, same-sex relations 87%). Median half-widths 9.4, 8.2 and 7.6 points; mean interval (Winkler) scores 30.1, 28.5 and 22.2. For comparison, Rubin's MICE intervals cover 82.7% (worst 20%) and the hgb_shift bootstrap 76.2% (worst 40%). (1) **Held**: 95.1%. (2) **Held**: worst item 83%. (3) **Held**: `drift` is narrower than `const` (7.6 vs 9.4 points) at slightly higher coverage, and has the best interval score. (4) **Refuted narrowly for hgb_shift**: `samp` covers same-sex marriage in exactly 60% of years (3 of 5) and same-sex relations in 87%, not below 60%; around `mice_logreg` it is 20% and 50%, as predicted. (5) **Held**: `combo` mean error is 3.23 points, below interp (3.43) and hgb_shift (3.38), and `combo` + `drift` has the lowest interval score of all (21.1). The refutation rule was not met. The pattern holds at 80% and 90% nominal levels (drift worst-item 66% and 72%, against 40% and 59% for `const`).
- Reading: the fix for MICE's over-confident intervals is not more imputations but a width that grows where the item's neighbours were moving. A conformal interval calibrated on the other items, with the score scaled by sampling noise and a drift scale read off the neighbouring asked years, reaches nominal coverage overall and on the fast-moving items while being narrower in the typical year. So for a survey analyst the usable product is a point imputation (the neighbour average blended with a shifted model, `combo`) plus this interval, about ±7 points for a typical Catholic item-year: honest, and wide. The drift scale itself is a weak predictor of the error (Spearman 0.13); it earns its keep in the tail, the few years between asked years that straddle a break. Caveats: conformal validity here assumes the 13 items are exchangeable, which they are not (same-sex marriage has 5 years, so its 100% is 5 of 5); scores within an item are correlated across years; s ignores weights and the GSS design effect; hgb_shift's neighbour offsets are in-sample (2026-11-08); and none of this helps an item never asked, which has no neighbours. Next: the mixture baseline (Si and Reiter 2013) when the GSS host is reachable again, and the same intervals around it.

## 2026-11-10 · Milestone 3 (part): does chained-equation multiple imputation (MICE) recover a held-out ballot, and are its intervals honest? (predictions written before the run; outcome below)

- Why: the proposal names MICE and mixture imputation as milestone 3's principled baselines, and the three period-aware single models (2026-11-08, 2026-11-09) all stopped near interpolation. MICE adds two things the earlier variants lack: missing *features* are imputed jointly with the target instead of median-filled or split around, and Rubin's rules give an interval for the imputed share. `experiments/m3_mice/run.py` repeats the held-out-year design (Catholics, 13 items, item-years asked of at least 150 respondents, `year` not a feature) with m = 5 chained-equation imputations (sklearn IterativeImputer, BayesianRidge, posterior draws, 5 rounds) and two target models from the same completed data: `mice_norm` (the chain's own linear draw, clipped) and `mice_logreg` (logistic regression on a bootstrap resample, Bernoulli draws; mice's "logreg.boot"). A one-item timing run (weekly attendance, 2010, m = 2) was done to size the job; these predictions were written after it and before the full run.
- Predictions: (1) `mice_logreg` mean absolute year-level error is within 0.3 points of plain logit (4.06) and does not beat plain hgb (3.87): imputing features jointly does not tell the model anything about the unseen year; (2) `mice_norm` is worse than `mice_logreg` (a linear-probability draw, clipped, mis-states shares near 0 or 1); (3) the Rubin 95% interval covers the year's observed share in under 80% of item-years for both, since it carries within-model uncertainty only, not period drift; (4) same-sex marriage is still the worst item, with error above 10 points. Refuted, and the reading of the last three entries with it, if `mice_logreg` beats interpolation (3.43) on mean error.
- **Outcome** (`experiments/m3_mice/results/mice.csv`, `summary.md`; 13 items, 307 item-years, m = 5, about 25 min on 4 cores). Mean absolute year-level error in points: `mice_logreg` 4.66 (median 3.46), `mice_norm` 7.18, against plain logit 4.06, plain hgb 3.87, hgb_shift 3.38, hgb_w5 3.44, decade mean 3.54, interpolation 3.43. (1) **Refuted in the worse direction**: `mice_logreg` is 0.6 points *worse* than plain logit, not within 0.3, and does not beat hgb (closer than hgb on 39% of item-years, than interpolation on 42%). (2) **Held**: the clipped linear draw is far worse (7.2). (3) **Half held**: the Rubin 95% interval covers the observed share in 39.7% of item-years for `mice_norm` but 82.7% for `mice_logreg` (80% predicted as the ceiling), with a median half-width of 7.4 points. Both are below the nominal 95%, and coverage collapses where opinion moved: same-sex relations 40%, same-sex marriage 20% (1 of 5 years). (4) **Held**: same-sex marriage is the worst item (15.8 points; interpolation 5.8). The refutation rule was not met: MICE does not beat interpolation, so the reading of the last three entries stands.
- Reading: the textbook multiple-imputation workflow is the worst of the model-based ways tried so far to recover a wholly unseen ballot's share. Imputing the missing features jointly did not help, and on same-sex relations (10.0 vs 6.8 for plain logit) and adultery (5.6 vs 3.9) it hurt; one possible cause, not tested, is that features imputed from the same pooled linear chain shrink the held-out year's profiles toward the all-years average. The useful finding is about the intervals: MICE's Rubin intervals look reasonable on average (83% coverage at a nominal 95%) only because the Bernoulli draws make them wide (median ±7.4 points); they cover 20 to 40% of the years on the two items whose trend broke, so an analyst trusting them would be told a 1990s same-sex-relations share was known to within a few points when it was off by ten. An interval that includes period drift (for example, the spread of the neighbouring years' shares) is the natural next step, together with the mixture baseline (Si and Reiter 2013). Caveats: one seed per imputation; m = 5; the chain is linear (BayesianRidge) for every feature including one-hot categoricals; the within-imputation variance uses a Kish effective n, not the GSS design; the mixture baseline is still not run.

## 2026-11-09 · Milestone 3 (part): does fitting on the nearest years only fix the held-out-year bias? (predictions written before the run; outcome below)

- Why: the period-shift check (entry below) left the best variant, `hgb_shift` (3.38 points), at parity with interpolation (3.43), and the level shift did not close the gap on fast-moving items, which suggests the relation between other answers and the item changed, not only its level. `experiments/m3_local_window/run.py` fits the year-free logit and hgb only on training years within 5 or 10 years of the held-out year (fallback to all other years when the window has under 600 respondents or one class). Same 13 items, 307 item-years, 200 bootstrap draws and baselines. The predictions were written after the job started but before I opened any output.
- Predictions: (1) the best window variant has a lower mean absolute year-level error than plain hgb (3.87) by at least 10%; (2) it does not beat interpolation (3.43) by more than 0.3 points; (3) the 5-year window is better than the 10-year one on the fast-moving items (same-sex marriage, cohabitation) and worse on the slow ones (attendance, premarital sex), where less data costs more than drift; (4) at least 78% of year intervals contain zero for the best variant. Refuted if the best window variant is no better than plain hgb on mean error.
- **Outcome** (`experiments/m3_local_window/results/local_window.csv`; 13 items, 307 item-years, 200 bootstrap draws, about 15 min; the window fell back to all other years in 11 item-years at W = 5 and 5 at W = 10). Mean absolute year-level error in points: logit_w5 4.84, logit_w10 4.38, hgb_w5 3.44, hgb_w10 3.48, against plain hgb 3.87, hgb_shift 3.38, decade mean 3.54, interpolation 3.43. (1) **Held**: hgb_w5 cuts plain hgb error by 11%. (2) **Held**: it matches interpolation (3.44 vs 3.43; closer on 56% of item-years) and does not beat it by 0.3. (3) **Refuted**: the 5-year window is not systematically worse on slow items (attendance 2.5 vs 2.8 for W = 10; premarital sex 2.9 vs 2.6 is the one case that is) and ties on same-sex marriage (16.7 both). (4) **Refuted narrowly**: 77.5% of hgb_w5 year intervals contain zero (78% predicted); 76% for hgb_w10. Under the stated rule the window helps (3.44 < 3.87), so the hypothesis that period-local fitting helps is not refuted, but it only reaches parity with interpolation and the shift, as the level shift did.
- Reading: three different ways to make a model period-aware (a linear year term, a neighbour-fitted level shift, a local window) all stop at roughly 3.4 to 3.7 points, the same as carrying the neighbouring ballots' observed shares. A boosted model on other answers adds nothing about a wholly unseen ballot beyond what its neighbours already say. The exception is same-sex marriage, 16.7 points for both windows against 5.8 for interpolation: the item moved so fast that even a 5-year window straddles the break, and the logit windows are worse than hgb everywhere (4.4 to 4.8). So milestone 3's remaining question is no longer 'which period fix' but whether MICE or mixture imputation does better on items that were never asked; the held-out-year test cannot separate them from interpolation. Caveats: one seed; the bootstrap ignores GSS clustering; windows that are small fall back to all years (11 item-years), which blurs the W = 5 result at the ends of runs; same-sex marriage dominates the spread.

## 2026-11-08 · Milestone 3 (part): does a period term fix the held-out-year bias? (predictions written before the run; outcome below)

Same items, years, bootstrap (200 draws) and baselines. The hgb neighbour fit is in-sample, so its offsets are optimistic (disclosed).
- Predictions: (1) the shift variants cut mean absolute year-level error of the plain models (hgb 3.9 points) by at least a quarter; (2) `logit_shift` or `hgb_shift` matches or beats interpolation (3.4) on mean error, but does not beat it by more than 0.5 points, since both lean on the same two neighbours; (3) `logit_year` helps the fast-moving items (same-sex marriage, cohabitation) but is worse than the shift on end-of-run years; (4) the share of year intervals containing zero rises from 69% to at least 80% for the best variant. Refuted if the best shift variant is no better than plain hgb on mean error.
- **Outcome** (`experiments/m3_period_shift/results/period_shift.csv`; same 13 items and 307 item-years, 200 bootstrap draws, about 20 min). Mean absolute year-level error in points: plain logit 4.06, plain hgb 3.87, `logit_year` 3.70, `logit_shift` 3.84, `hgb_shift` 3.38, decade mean 3.54, interpolation 3.43. (1) **Refuted**: the best shift cuts hgb error by 13%, not a quarter. (2) **Held, barely**: `hgb_shift` matches interpolation (3.38 vs 3.43; lower on 56% of item-years) and does not beat it by 0.5. (3) **Refuted**: `logit_year` is no better on the fast items (same-sex marriage 17.9 vs 17.3 for plain hgb, cohabitation 7.8 vs 6.9) and about equal to `hgb_shift` at the ends of runs (6.25 vs 6.34 points). (4) **Refuted**: 76% of `hgb_shift` year intervals contain zero (logit_year 74%, plain hgb 69%). Under the stated rule the shift is better than plain hgb on mean error, so the programme's hypothesis that a level shift helps is not refuted, but the gain is small.

The next step is a period-by-feature interaction (or a model fitted on the nearest years only), and the MICE and mixture baselines. Caveats: one seed; the hgb neighbour offsets are in-sample and so optimistic, yet hgb_shift is still only at parity; the bootstrap ignores GSS clustering; interpolation needs asked neighbours that an item never asked does not have.

# Research log: christian-belief-models

## 2026-11-19 · Milestone 5 (part): does the language model's sense of the gap between groups grow with scale? A 3B point (predictions written before the run; outcome below)

- Why: the 2026-11-17 entry left one narrow, testable question: a local model knows *which way* groups differ but predicts a tenth of the gap (slope of predicted on true gap 0.09 for Qwen2.5-1.5B, &minus;0.10 for 0.5B). If the slope grows quickly with scale, the paper's conclusion ("each group needs its own calibration subsample") has an expiry date set by model size; if it grows slowly, the conclusion holds for any model a survey office can run locally. A third point on the same family's scale curve is the cheapest real test. A 7B model in bfloat16 does not fit this session's 15 GB of memory; Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct (6 GB) does.
- Design: unchanged from 2026-11-17 except the model. Same code (`llm.py`, `score.py`), same seed-0 sample of 3,471 respondents, same profiles and questions, same 74 cells; scored label-blind before any outcome is computed. `scale.py` (new) computes the 3B model's per-cell AUC, level error, predicted gap and LLM-shift estimate exactly as `analyse.py` does and joins them to the committed `cells.csv` rows of the other estimators (the 0.5B and 1.5B per-respondent scores are not in git; their per-cell values are). It adds a cell-bootstrap 95% interval (2,000 draws) for each model's slope of predicted on true gap. Licence note: Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct is released under the Qwen Research Licence (research and non-commercial use; the 0.5B and 1.5B are Apache-2.0). It is used here for research only and no weights are redistributed.
- Predictions: (1) Gap slope of the 3B model between 0.10 and 0.35 (a log-linear extrapolation of the 0.5B and 1.5B points gives about 0.21), with its interval excluding 1; Spearman with the true gap at least 0.30. (2) Zero-shot median AUC between 0.56 and 0.64, and at least 0.06 below GBM-demog (0.699). (3) Zero-shot median absolute level error at least 15 points. (4) 3B-shift beats carrying the source share (median paired abs-error difference below 0) and stays at least 2 points behind GBM-full. (5) By kind, 3B-shift does better than 1.5B-shift on tradition cells (median abs error below 14.78). **The milestone's reading is refuted** ("a model a survey office can run locally does not carry the concept shift") if the 3B slope is at least 0.5 with a bootstrap interval whose lower end is above 0.3, or if 3B-shift beats GBM-full's median absolute level error.

## 2026-11-17 · Milestone 5: can a local open-weights language model stand in for the transferred model or the calibration subsample? (predictions written before the analysis; outcome below)

- Why: milestone 5 ("a local-LLM imputation baseline, an open-weights model, no paid API, against the tabular models") is the one milestone never started, and the paper's introduction cites LLM opinion imputation (Argyle et al. 2023; Kim and Lee 2023) without a number of its own. The paper's central finding is that a transferred model misses a subgroup's *level* through concept shift, which the subgroup's other answers cannot recover. A language model is the one predictor here that knows something about the target group without any of its GSS answers: it is told "Religion: Evangelical Protestant" or "Interviewed in 2021" and may know from pre-training how such people answer. So the question is sharper than "is the LLM a good imputer": does it know the *shift* the tabular model cannot see?
- Design (`experiments/m5_local_llm/`): two Apache-2.0 models, Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, on CPU in bfloat16 (`llm.py`). Each respondent gets a plain-English profile built only from the 15 demographic variables of `src/gss.py` (`DEMOG`: year, age, sex, schooling, race, Hispanic origin, generation, region, political views, party, marital status, children, income, raised Catholic, strength of affiliation) plus the tradition; no item answers, as in Argyle et al.'s "silicon samples". Each of 12 items (the 13 of the paper less `ever_divorced`, whose profile would reveal current marital status) is a yes/no question phrased so that "Yes" is the coded answer; P(yes) is the softmax of the next-token logits of "Yes" and "No". The profile is run once and its key/value cache reused for the 12 questions (checked to agree exactly with an unbatched pass in float32). Sample (`sample.py`, seed 0): a simple random sample of 300 respondents from each of 12 groups (3,471 respondents in all), scored label-blind by `score.py` before these predictions were written (no outcome was computed). Eight transfer pairs (generation 3&rarr;1, non-Hispanic&rarr;Hispanic Catholics, Catholic&rarr;Evangelical, Catholic&rarr;Black Protestant, Evangelical&rarr;Catholic, Mainline&rarr;Evangelical, Catholic and Evangelical &le;2014&rarr;2016&ndash;24); cells with at least 60 sampled target and source respondents asked the item. Compared on the same target rows: the raw LLMs (zero-shot, no GSS answers); the 1.5B model recalibrated on the source sample (weighted logistic on its logit, "Platt"); the milestone-1 boosted model on the 15 demographics only (GBM-demog) and on demographics plus the other items (GBM-full, as in m6), both fitted on the whole source group; the source group's share (SRC); and a level-only estimator, the source share plus the LLM's predicted gap between the groups (LLM-shift = &theta;<sub>S</sub> + f&#772;<sub>T</sub> &minus; f&#772;<sub>S</sub>). Metrics per cell: weighted AUC, level error (points), and var(y &minus; f)/var(y), the fraction of a plain calibration subsample the difference estimator needs (Section 5). Intervals: cell bootstrap, 2,000 draws.
- Predictions: (1) Ranking: median AUC of the zero-shot 1.5B model between 0.55 and 0.68; GBM-demog at least 0.03 higher and GBM-full at least 0.12 higher (median paired differences); 1.5B above 0.5B by a median of at least 0.02. (2) Level: the zero-shot 1.5B model's median absolute level error is at least twice GBM-full's and at least 8 points. (3) Recalibrated on the source, the 1.5B model's median absolute level error is within 1.5 points of SRC's and no more than 1 point below GBM-full's. (4) As the difference estimator's f: median var(y &minus; f)/var(y) at least 0.95 for the raw 1.5B model and at least 0.92 recalibrated; GBM-demog between 0.85 and 0.95; GBM-full at most 0.88. (5) The shift: across cells the LLM's predicted gap f&#772;<sub>T</sub> &minus; f&#772;<sub>S</sub> has Spearman correlation at least 0.3 with the true gap &theta;<sub>T</sub> &minus; &theta;<sub>S</sub>, but LLM-shift does not beat GBM-full's median absolute level error by more than 0.5 points overall; on tradition cells LLM-shift beats SRC (smaller median absolute error), on generation and period cells it does not. *The milestone's headline reading is refuted* (a local model does carry the concept shift the tabular model misses) if LLM-shift beats GBM-full by more than 1 point in median absolute error with a bootstrap interval excluding zero.
- **Outcome** (`experiments/m5_local_llm/results/summary.md`, `cells.csv`, `gap_scatter.svg`; scoring 3,478 respondents took 27 min for 0.5B and 66 min for 1.5B on 4 CPU cores, the analysis about 3 min). 74 cells over 8 pairs and 10 items: same-sex marriage and cohabitation never reached 60 sampled respondents asked in both groups, and papal infallibility did in only 2 cells. (1) **Held**: median AUC of the zero-shot 1.5B model 0.569 (cell bootstrap 0.537&ndash;0.589); GBM-demog higher by a median 0.130 (0.110&ndash;0.140) and GBM-full by 0.193 (0.162&ndash;0.209); 1.5B above 0.5B by 0.051 (0.021&ndash;0.058); the 0.5B model is at chance (0.531). (2) **Held**: the zero-shot 1.5B model misses the group's share by a median 32.8 points (GBM-full 5.1), worst on certainty about God (73 points) and daily prayer (54). (3) **Partly held**: recalibrated on the source, its median error is 14.05 points against SRC's 12.41, 1.64 apart (predicted within 1.5; paired median +2.17, 0.78 to 2.76), and 7.6 points worse than GBM-full. Recalibration imports the source's level and then does slightly worse than carrying the source share, because the model's ranking is too weak to move it the right way. (4) **Held**: var(y &minus; f)/var(y) 0.990 raw and 1.000 recalibrated (no calibration saving), GBM-demog 0.927, GBM-full 0.796. (5) **Partly held**: the 1.5B model's predicted gap between the groups has Spearman 0.345 (p = 0.003) with the true gap, but its slope is 0.09: it predicts a median gap of 2.6 points where the true one is 12.4. The 0.5B model's predicted gap runs the wrong way (Spearman &minus;0.51). The refutation rule was not met. Of 5 predictions, 3 held and 2 held in part.
- Reading: a small open-weights model given only a respondent's background is no stand-in for either piece of the paper's design. As an imputer it ranks respondents barely above chance (AUC 0.57, where a boosted model on the same 15 variables reaches 0.70) and its raw probabilities are not calibrated to the survey at all, so as the difference estimator's f it saves nothing. Its one real asset is the one the transferred model lacks: it knows, weakly, which way groups differ (more conservative Evangelicals, less devout recent cohorts). But it knows about a tenth of the size of those gaps, so adding its gap to the source share helps a little over the source share and stays far behind a model fitted to the source group's other answers. The paper's conclusion that each group needs its own calibration subsample stands against this baseline. What it does not settle: these are 0.5B and 1.5B models on CPU. Argyle et al. used a 175B model, and the slope of 0.09 may grow with scale. The question to put to a larger model is narrow and testable with this code: does its predicted gap have a slope near 1?
- Checks (`results/check_Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct.txt`, first 20 sampled respondents): the cached, batched scoring path matches an unbatched pass of the same tokens in float32 to 1.3e-6. The bfloat16 scores used here agree with float32 at Pearson r 0.995 (median absolute difference 0.009, largest 0.053), and their mean is 1.0 point higher (15.3% against 14.4%). That is small next to the 33-point level error and cannot change any reading above. Per-respondent scores stay out of git, like the GSS microdata (`.gitignore`); `score.py` regenerates them.
- Deviations from the plan: none in the analysis. One limit was fixed before scoring: `ever_divorced` is left out, because the profile states current marital status.

## 2026-11-16 · Milestone 6 (part): the calibration section under the GSS's own design, strata and PSUs (predictions written before the run; outcome below)

- Why: the paper draft (`paper/draft.md`, sections 5 and 10, "Before submission") says the calibration numbers ignore the GSS's clustered design: subsamples were simple random draws scored against the target's *sample* share, with weights-only (Kish) variances. Two consequences are untested. (a) Clustering: the GSS draws paired PSUs within strata (`vstrat`, `vpsu`; 2 PSUs per stratum, strata unique to a year), so true variances are larger; the transferred model may remove some of the between-PSU variation, which would make D's saving larger under the design than under Kish. (b) The survey's own error: against the *population* share, M and D share the phase-1 variance V1 of the full target sample's share, which no calibration subsample reduces, so D's saving on total error must shrink as n grows.
- Design (`experiments/m6_design_variance/run.py`): the 241 cells of `m6_calibration` refitted identically (pipeline check: biases and var(y&minus;f)/var(y) must match its `cells.csv`). Per n &isin; {50, 100, 200, 400} (n at most half the rows), 500 simple random subsamples: phase-2 variances V2_M, V2_D around the sample share; total-error ratio (V1 + V2_D)/(V1 + V2_M); V1's share of M's total error; the inflation sqrt((V1 + V2_D)/mean Kish interval variance) by which `m6_calibration`'s D intervals are too narrow for the population share; and a design variance estimate for D a designer could compute (f on every target row, y on the subsample only: ultimate-cluster variance of w(f &minus; f&#772;)/W<sub>T</sub> + w(r &minus; r&#772;<sub>s</sub>)/W<sub>s</sub>), scored as its mean over draws against V1 + V2_D. - Reading: the paper's section-5 numbers survive the design. For subgroup estimates pooled over many GSS years, clustering adds only about 6% to the variance of a share (2% for the residual), because each PSU holds about two respondents of the group; the model removes a little of that, so the design-based saving is slightly larger than Kish's (19% against 17%). ## 2026-11-14 · Milestone 6 (part): can the shrinkage estimator's interval be trusted in each subgroup? (predictions written before the run; outcome below)

- Why: the entry below found that shrinking each group's residual correction toward the transferred model (FH_prior, &tau;<sup>2</sup> from the same kind of group's biases on other items) halves the group-average MSE at 50 respondents per group, and flagged that its naive interval was not checked. A designer who reports a subgroup share needs an interval, and a shrunk estimate is biased by (1 &minus; &gamma;)b in a group whose transferred-model bias is b, so the textbook interval &plusmn;1.96&radic;(&gamma;v) (the posterior sd when &tau;<sup>2</sup> is known; Prasad and Rao 1990, doi:10.1080/01621459.1990.10476213, add terms only for an estimated &tau;<sup>2</sup>) can cover far below 95% in exactly the groups that matter. The robust empirical Bayes interval of Armstrong, Kolesár and Plagborg-Møller (2022, doi:10.3982/ECTA18597) widens the critical value so that coverage *averaged over groups* is at least 95% for any bias distribution with the assumed second moment, which here comes from other items rather than the groups themselves.
- Design (`experiments/m6_intervals/run.py`): no microdata (gss.norc.org refused the proxy this session); it reuses the per-group bias b, residual sd &sigma; (with the Kish design effect) and budgets of `m6_allocation/results/groups.csv` and the per-cell biases of `cells.csv` for &tau;<sup>2</sup> exactly as that run set it. Under the normal approximation the FH_prior error in group g is (1 &minus; &gamma;)b + &gamma;&radic;v Z, v = &sigma;<sup>2</sup>/n, so each interval's coverage given b is computed exactly rather than simulated. Intervals: D (difference estimator &plusmn; 1.96&radic;v, the 95% reference), FH_naive (&plusmn; 1.96&radic;(&gamma;v)), FH_robust (AKP critical value for normalised-bias second moment v/&tau;<sup>2</sup>, times &gamma;&radic;v) and FH_cmse (&plusmn; 1.96 times the root of the plug-in conditional MSE &gamma;<sup>2</sup>v + (1 &minus; &gamma;)<sup>2</sup>max(0, r&#772;<sup>2</sup> &minus; v), integrated over r&#772;). Per n&#772; &isin; {50, 100, 200, 400}: mean coverage over groups, share of groups below 80%, coverage by kind, and median half-width over D's. ## 2026-11-13 · Milestone 6 (part): how should a fixed calibration budget be split across subgroups, and does shrinking toward the transferred model beat the difference estimator? (predictions written before the run; outcome below)

- Why: the entry below sized a calibration subsample for one group at a time and found the difference estimator D saves about a sixth of it, with a break-even n* = &sigma;<sub>Y&minus;f</sub><sup>2</sup>/b<sup>2</sup> (median 91) below which the transferred model alone (E0, bias b) is better. A designer rotating item j off several subgroups' ballots has one budget B for all of them. Two levers remain: where to put the respondents (allocation) and how far to trust E0 in each group (a composite E0 + &gamma;<sub>g</sub>(D &minus; E0), the small-area estimator of Fay and Herriot 1979, doi:10.1080/01621459.1979.10482505, with &gamma;<sub>g</sub> = &tau;<sup>2</sup>/(&tau;<sup>2</sup> + v<sub>g</sub>)). With known b<sub>g</sub> and residual sd &sigma;<sub>g</sub> the composite's MSE is b<sup>2</sup>v/(b<sup>2</sup> + v), v = &sigma;<sup>2</sup>/n, and minimising its sum over groups under &Sigma;n<sub>g</sub> = B gives a water-filling rule n<sub>g</sub> = max(0, &sigma;<sub>g</sub>/&radic;&mu; &minus; n*<sub>g</sub>): Neyman allocation (Neyman 1934, doi:10.2307/2342192) minus each group's break-even, so groups the model already fits get nothing.
- Design (`experiments/m6_allocation/run.py`): the milestone-1 boosted model and the same source&rarr;target cells as the entry below, grouped into families that share an item and a kind: tradition (one source, its 3 other traditions as targets), period (the 4 traditions' 2016-24 targets) and generation (Catholic gen1, gen2 and gen3 targets); the single Hispanic cell has no family. Budget B = G &times; n&#772; with n&#772; &isin; {50, 100, 200, 400}, no group above half its asked rows. R = 300 draws of simple random subsamples per family and n&#772;. Loss = mean over the family's groups of MSE against each group's full weighted share. Strategies: M_eq (plain subsample mean, equal split); D_eq (difference estimator, equal split; the reference); D_ney (D, Neyman split on the oracle &sigma;<sub>g</sub>); C_or (oracle composite with known b and &sigma;, water-filling split; a lower bound, not feasible); FH_fam (equal split, composite with &tau;<sup>2</sup> estimated by moments across the family's groups from the subsamples); FH_prior (equal split, &tau;<sup>2</sup> = mean b<sup>2</sup> of the same kind's cells on the other items, as a designer would take from items already fielded); FH2 (half the budget as an equal pilot, the rest water-filled on the pilot's posterior b<sup>2</sup> and &sigma;, then FH_prior). Medians over families, item-cluster bootstrap intervals.
- Predictions: (1) D_ney's loss is within 3% of D_eq's (median ratio 0.97 to 1.00) at every n&#772;: residual sds of binary items vary too little for Neyman to matter. (2) The oracle C_or's median loss ratio to D_eq is at most 0.60 at n&#772; = 50 and between 0.75 and 0.95 at n&#772; = 400. (3) FH_prior captures at least half the oracle's gain at n&#772; = 50 and 100 (ratio &le; (1 + C_or)/2) and is no worse than D_eq by more than 2% (median) at n&#772; = 400. (4) FH_fam is worse than FH_prior at n&#772; = 50 by at least 0.05 in median ratio, because 3 or 4 groups estimate &tau;<sup>2</sup> badly. (5) FH2's loss is within 5% of FH_prior's at every n&#772;: allocation adds little beyond shrinkage. (6) Shrinkage trades the worst group for the average: at n&#772; = 50 the median ratio of FH_prior's worst-group RMSE to D_eq's is above 1. Refuted, with the reading that shrinking toward a transferred model does not help small subgroups, if FH_prior's median loss ratio is 0.97 or more at n&#772; = 50.

- Reading: for a designer rotating an item off several subgroups' ballots, the lever is the estimator, not the allocation. Equal splits are fine: Neyman allocation on the residual sd moves the loss by under 1%, and water-filling on pilot estimates of the bias makes it slightly worse. Shrinking each group's residual correction toward zero by &gamma; = &tau;<sup>2</sup>/(&tau;<sup>2</sup> + v), with &tau;<sup>2</sup> set from the same kind of group's biases on items already fielded in full, halves the group-average MSE at 50 respondents per group, which is roughly the difference estimator's error with twice the respondents, and still saves 11% at 400. &tau;<sup>2</sup> has to come from outside the family: three or four groups cannot estimate it, and the family-level version ends up worse than no shrinkage at moderate budgets. Next: Fay-Herriot intervals with an honest MSE estimate (Prasad and Rao 1990) and their coverage per group, a per-group prior from covariates, and the paper's section on the bound.

## 2026-11-12 · Milestone 6 (part): how big a calibration subsample does a rotated item need in each group, and does the transferred model shrink it? (predictions written before the run; outcome below)

- Why: the entry below found that a transferred model's level error is mostly concept shift (median 3.45 points) that the group's other answers cannot fix, so a rotated item needs a calibration subsample in each group that is asked it. The number a survey designer needs is how big. With a subsample of n target respondents asked item j, two design-consistent estimators of the target's share &theta;<sub>j</sub> exist: M, the subsample's own weighted mean (variance &sigma;<sub>Y</sub><sup>2</sup>/n), and D, the difference (model-assisted, prediction-powered) estimator E<sub>T</sub>[f<sub>j</sub>] over all target rows plus the subsample's mean residual Y &minus; f<sub>j</sub> (S&auml;rndal, Swensson and Wretman 1992; Angelopoulos et al. 2023, doi:10.1126/science.adi6000), variance &sigma;<sub>Y&minus;f</sub><sup>2</sup>/n. So D needs a fraction &sigma;<sub>Y&minus;f</sub><sup>2</sup>/&sigma;<sub>Y</sub><sup>2</sup> of M's sample for the same precision, and it beats the label-free transfer E0 (bias b, no variance) once n &gt; n* = &sigma;<sub>Y&minus;f</sub><sup>2</sup>/b<sup>2</sup>. `experiments/m6_calibration/run.py` refits the milestone-1 boosted model on the source group for the same 20 pairs and items as the entry below, then draws R = 500 simple random subsamples of n &isin; {25, 50, 100, 200, 400} from the target rows asked j (n at most half of them), and scores M, D, PPI++ (D with a power-tuning factor &lambda;&#770; = cov(Y, f)/((1 + n/N) var(f)) estimated on the subsample; Angelopoulos, Duchi and Zrnic 2023, arXiv:2311.01453) and R (f recalibrated by a logit intercept fitted on the subsample, then averaged over all target rows) against the target's full weighted share, with Wald 95% intervals (Kish-linearised weighted variance, finite-population correction) for M and D. A test run on synthetic data checked the code; these predictions were written before any GSS output was read.
- Predictions: (1) D has lower RMSE than M in at least 85% of cell &times; n combinations, and the median variance ratio &sigma;<sub>Y&minus;f</sub><sup>2</sup>/&sigma;<sub>Y</sub><sup>2</sup> over cells is between 0.65 and 0.85 (D saves 15 to 35% of the calibration sample); (2) savings are largest for weekly attendance and the sexual-morality cluster (median saving at least 30% over their cells for premarital sex, same-sex relations, same-sex marriage and weekly attendance) and smallest for ever divorced and divorce law (under 10%); (3) the median break-even n* where D overtakes E0 lies between 50 and 300, and in at least 80% of cells whose simulated crossover falls inside the grid the analytic n* is within a factor of 2 of it; (4) PPI++ and R are within 5% of D's RMSE (median ratio over cells) at every n; (5) D's Wald 95% interval covers &theta; in 93 to 96% of draws pooled over cells at n &ge; 100, and below 93% at n = 25. Refuted, with the reading that a transferred model does not shrink the calibration sample a rotated item needs, if the median variance ratio is 0.95 or more.
- **Outcome** (`experiments/m6_calibration/results/summary.md`, `cells.csv`, `sims.csv`, `rmse_by_n.svg`; 241 cells over 20 pairs, 1,172 cell &times; n combinations, 500 subsamples each, about 4 minutes on 4 cores). ## 2026-11-12 · Milestone 6 (part): why does a transferred model miss a subgroup's level, and can the items the subgroup was asked correct it? (predictions written before the run; outcome below)

- Why: milestone 1 found that models trained on one group rank another group's respondents almost as well but miss its *level* (third-generation to foreign-born Catholics: up to 11 points). Milestone 6 asks for a bound on a transferred model's error. The level error splits exactly: b<sub>j</sub> = E<sub>T</sub>[f<sub>j</sub>] &minus; E<sub>T</sub>[Y<sub>j</sub>] = A<sub>j</sub> + B<sub>j</sub>, where A<sub>j</sub> = E<sub>T</sub>[f<sub>j</sub> &minus; p<sub>S,j</sub>] is source misfit where the target lives (covariate shift; estimable with no target label as the importance-weighted source residual E<sub>S</sub>[r(X)(f<sub>j</sub> &minus; Y<sub>j</sub>)], r = dT/dS from a domain classifier) and B<sub>j</sub> = E<sub>T</sub>[p<sub>S,j</sub> &minus; p<sub>T,j</sub>] is conditional (concept) shift, which needs the target's answers to j. So |b<sub>j</sub>| &le; |A<sub>j</sub>| + |B<sub>j</sub>|, with |A<sub>j</sub>| &le; &radic;(1 + &chi;<sup>2</sup>(T&Vert;S)) &Vert;f<sub>j</sub> &minus; p<sub>S,j</sub>&Vert;<sub>S</sub>. The useful question is whether B<sub>j</sub> for the item a subgroup was *not* asked can be read off the items it *was* asked. `experiments/m6_shift_bound/run.py` fits gradient boosting (the milestone-1 model) on a source group for each of the 12 items (13 for Catholic-to-Catholic pairs) and imputes each item for a target group, over 20 source&rarr;target pairs: Catholic generation 3&rarr;1, 3&rarr;2, 1&rarr;3, non-Hispanic&rarr;Hispanic Catholics, all 12 ordered pairs of Catholic, Evangelical, Mainline and Black Protestant, and each tradition &le;2014&rarr;2016&ndash;24. For each held-out item j it compares estimators of the target's share that use only the target's covariates and its answers to the *other* items: E0 the transferred model as is; E1 the source share; IW, E0 minus the label-free A&#770;<sub>j</sub>; E2, E0 shifted on the logit scale by the median of the other items' recalibration intercepts &delta;<sub>k</sub>; E3, shifted by &Sigma;&rho;<sub>jk</sub>&delta;<sub>k</sub>/&Sigma;|&rho;<sub>jk</sub>|, &rho;<sub>jk</sub> the weighted correlation of the two items' predicted logits over the target's rows (label-free, so a negatively related item such as ever-divorced lends its shift with the sign flipped). Truth is the target's observed weighted share. A two-pair `--quick` run checked the code; these predictions were written before its output was read.
- Predictions: (1) covariate shift explains little: median |A&#770;<sub>j</sub>| under 1 point, and |A&#770;<sub>j</sub>| &lt; |B&#770;<sub>j</sub>| in at least 75% of cells, so IW is within 0.3 points of E0 on mean absolute error; (2) the naive transfer E0 misses by at least 3 points on average over cells and beats the source share E1; (3) E3 cuts E0's mean absolute error by at least 25%, with a pair-cluster bootstrap 95% interval for the paired difference excluding 0; (4) E2 helps less than E3, because shifts differ in sign across items (milestone 1: foreign-born Catholics are more certain of God but less likely to believe in an afterlife than the model expects); (5) the label-free correction size |E3 &minus; E0| ranks cells by actual |b<sub>j</sub>| with Spearman &ge; 0.4; (6) the period pairs gain least, since the items that moved fastest (same-sex relations and marriage) moved apart from the rest. Refuted, with the reading that a subgroup's asked items carry its unasked item's level shift, if E3 is not better than E0 on mean absolute error (interval includes 0 or E3 worse).
- **Outcome** (`experiments/m6_shift_bound/results/cells.csv`, `summary.md`; 241 cells over 20 pairs, about 9 minutes on 4 cores; intervals resample pairs, 2000 draws). Mean absolute error of the target's share in points: E0 5.59, E1 10.90, IW 5.25, E2 5.18, E3 5.24. Paired with E0: IW &minus;0.33 (95% &minus;0.69 to +0.04), E2 &minus;0.41 (&minus;0.96 to +0.16), E3 &minus;0.35 (&minus;0.74 to +0.01); E3 closer than E0 in 59% of cells. Median |b| 4.08 points, median |A&#770;| 0.83, median |B&#770;| 3.45. (1) **Held in its first two parts, missed narrowly in the third**: median |A&#770;| 0.83 points (&lt; 1) and |A&#770;| &lt; |B&#770;| in 77% of cells (&ge; 75%), but IW is 0.33 points better than E0, just past the 0.3 predicted; the gain is all in tradition pairs (5.73 &rarr; 5.18), where |A&#770;| runs 1 to 4 points, and none in generation or period pairs (|A&#770;| about 0.2). (4) **Refuted**: E2 is no worse than E3 (E3 &minus; E2 +0.06, &minus;0.31 to +0.35); the signed weighting buys nothing. (5) **Refuted**: Spearman(|E3 &minus; E0|, |b|) = 0.11; the correction's size does not say which cells are badly off, though its sign is right in 71% of cells. - Reading: a transferred model's level error is mostly concept shift, item by item. Covariate shift, the part importance weighting can fix without any target label, is under a point for immigrant generations, Hispanic Catholics and later decades, and one to four points between traditions. The rest (median 3.45 points) is the target group answering *this* item differently from source respondents with the same profile, and that difference does not travel between items: the items whose shift E2/E3 fixed best were the sexual-morality cluster (premarital sex 5.5 &rarr; 3.3, same-sex relations 5.9 &rarr; 3.4, cohabitation 5.9 &rarr; 3.0), which move together, while abortion (4.6 &rarr; 6.2), divorce law (7.0 &rarr; 9.2) and adultery (2.3 &rarr; 3.5) got worse because their shifts run against the rest. For the bound this means |b<sub>j</sub>| &le; |A<sub>j</sub>| + |B<sub>j</sub>| has a label-free first term and a second term that only target answers to j itself identify. For practice: before rotating an item off a subgroup's ballot, keep a calibration subsample that is asked it; the asked items' shifts are not a substitute. Caveats: one boosted model and one seed; the domain classifier's weights have a median effective sample of a third of the source; tradition pairs share respondents' years, so period and tradition shifts mix; cells within a pair are not independent (hence the pair bootstrap). Next: how large a target calibration subsample makes a difference estimator (E0 plus the subsample's mean residual; prediction-powered inference, Angelopoulos et al. 2023, Science, doi:10.1126/science.adi6000) beat the subsample's own mean, by item, which is the number a survey designer needs.

## 2026-11-11 · Milestone 3 (part): can an interval for an unseen ballot's share be made honest by pricing in period drift? (predictions written before the run; outcome below)

- Why: the MICE entry (below) found Rubin 95% intervals cover the held-out year's observed share in 83% of item-years overall but 20 to 40% on the two items whose trend broke, because they carry within-model uncertainty only. The GSS host (gss.norc.org) refused this session's proxy (403), so this step uses only the committed per-item-year CSVs (`m3_period_shift`, `m3_mice`): no microdata is needed to recalibrate an interval around an imputed share already computed. `experiments/m3_drift_intervals/run.py` builds leave-one-item-out split-conformal intervals (Vovk, Gammerman and Shafer 2005; Lei et al. 2018) around three point imputations (`interp`, `hgb_shift`, `mice_logreg`) and their average `combo` = (interp + hgb_shift) / 2. For the item being scored, the 95% quantile of the scores of the other 12 items' item-years sets the width. Three scores: `const` (|error|, a fixed width), `samp` (|error| / s, s the binomial sampling sd of the year's share at its n), and `drift` (|error| / sqrt(s<sup>2</sup> + d<sup>2</sup>)), where d is a drift scale from the item's *other* asked years only: the per-year change between the two neighbouring asked years (both sides if the year is interior, the two nearest on one side if not) times the distance to the nearest asked year.
- Predictions: (1) `drift` intervals around `hgb_shift` cover 90 to 97% of item-years overall; (2) their worst single-item coverage is at least 70% (Rubin MICE: 20% on same-sex marriage); (3) at matched overall coverage `drift` has a smaller median half-width than `const`; (4) `samp` alone under-covers the fast-moving items (below 60% on same-sex marriage or same-sex relations), as Rubin did; (5) `combo` has a lower mean absolute error than both its parts (interp 3.43, hgb_shift 3.38). Refuted, as a way to make intervals honest where opinion moved, if `drift` worst-item coverage is below 60%.
- **Outcome** (`experiments/m3_drift_intervals/results/summary.md`, `intervals.csv`; 307 item-years, 13 items, under 5 s, no microdata). Around `hgb_shift`, leave-one-item-out 95% intervals cover: `const` 94.1% (worst item 60%, same-sex marriage), `samp` 95.1% (worst 60%), `drift` 95.1% (worst 83%, divorce law; same-sex marriage 5 of 5 years, same-sex relations 87%). Median half-widths 9.4, 8.2 and 7.6 points; mean interval (Winkler) scores 30.1, 28.5 and 22.2. For comparison, Rubin's MICE intervals cover 82.7% (worst 20%) and the hgb_shift bootstrap 76.2% (worst 40%). (1) **Held**: 95.1%. (2) **Held**: worst item 83%. (3) **Held**: `drift` is narrower than `const` (7.6 vs 9.4 points) at slightly higher coverage, and has the best interval score. (4) **Refuted narrowly for hgb_shift**: `samp` covers same-sex marriage in exactly 60% of years (3 of 5) and same-sex relations in 87%, not below 60%; around `mice_logreg` it is 20% and 50%, as predicted. - Reading: the fix for MICE's over-confident intervals is not more imputations but a width that grows where the item's neighbours were moving. A conformal interval calibrated on the other items, with the score scaled by sampling noise and a drift scale read off the neighbouring asked years, reaches nominal coverage overall and on the fast-moving items while being narrower in the typical year. So for a survey analyst the usable product is a point imputation (the neighbour average blended with a shifted model, `combo`) plus this interval, about ±7 points for a typical Catholic item-year: honest, and wide. The drift scale itself is a weak predictor of the error (Spearman 0.13); it earns its keep in the tail, the few years between asked years that straddle a break. Caveats: conformal validity here assumes the 13 items are exchangeable, which they are not (same-sex marriage has 5 years, so its 100% is 5 of 5); scores within an item are correlated across years; s ignores weights and the GSS design effect; hgb_shift's neighbour offsets are in-sample (2026-11-08); and none of this helps an item never asked, which has no neighbours. Next: the mixture baseline (Si and Reiter 2013) when the GSS host is reachable again, and the same intervals around it.

(4) **Refuted for one item**: ECE is under 0.03 for 12 of 13 targets (0.006 to 0.026); cohabitation is 0.041 (n = 1,489, the smallest ballot), and same-sex marriage is 0.026 on 1,869.
- Reading: out-of-fold imputed shares match the observed shares within about 1 point in a typical decade x generation cell, and the cells that miss are as often second-generation as first. The two small-ballot items (cohabitation, same-sex marriage) are the least calibrated. Caveats: one fold seed; the cells' bootstrap ignores GSS clustering and strata; these are out-of-fold checks on asked respondents, not yet a comparison against a ballot that asked the item while the model never saw that ballot (milestone 3).

## 2026-11-03 · Milestone 1 results: other answers predict unasked items well; associations transfer, levels do not

- Ran `experiments/m1_imputation/run.py` on the GSS 1972–2024 R3a (fit and test about 30 min on 2 cores). Tables are in `experiments/m1_imputation/results/summary.md`, and `summarise.py` rebuilds them from the committed CSVs.
- A first full run crashed in the temporal step. Some items were never on the same ballot before 2014, which left an all-missing feature column. Such columns are now dropped per training split, and the whole run was redone.
- **Headline.** Across 49 tradition × item cells, a model given a respondent's other answers beats the decade prior by a median 0.21 AUC. Its log loss is lower in 44 of the 49 (paired 95% interval below zero).
  - Among Catholics, boosting reaches 0.86 for weekly Mass attendance, 0.85 for "sex before marriage always wrong", 0.84 for same-sex relations, 0.88 for opposing same-sex marriage, 0.82 for cohabitation, 0.75 for abortion, 0.69 for divorce law and 0.71 for ever-divorced.
- **H1: mostly supported.** The four sexual-morality and marriage-belief items clear 0.75. Adultery (0.73) does not: almost everyone (77%) says it is always wrong, so there is little to rank. Divorce law stays under 0.70 as predicted. Ever-divorced reaches 0.71 with boosting (logistic 0.67), which breaks the "< 0.70" half of H1 for that model.
- **H2: supported.** The median boosting − logistic gap is +0.003 AUC. Only 4 of 49 cells have an interval wholly above +0.01, and 3 of those are ever-divorced, where age and exposure act nonlinearly. Logistic regression is never worse by more than 0.01.
- **H3: supported.** A model fitted only on third-plus-generation Catholics ranks foreign-born Catholics within 0.03 AUC of the pooled model on 12 of 13 items (ever-divorced −0.038). Its levels are wrong by more than 5 points on 5 of 13:
  - It under-predicts certainty about God by 11 points and opposition to same-sex marriage by 8.
  - It over-predicts belief in an afterlife by 10, support for harder divorce by 7 and ever-divorced by 8.
  - So the association structure largely travels to immigrants, but group levels do not. An imputation model must carry generation (and later country of origin) as a level term.
- **H4: refuted.** Scored on Protestants, a Catholic-fitted model loses a mean 0.011 AUC on Evangelicals, 0.005 on Mainline and 0.007 on Black Protestants. Black Protestants are not the worst. Again levels are the problem: on Evangelicals the Catholic model under-predicts "sex before marriage always wrong" by 17 points and averages 6.7 points low.
- **Descriptive** (Catholics, weighted share, by decade and generation; `shares_catholic_decade_generation.csv`):
  - Weekly Mass attendance fell from 41–48% in the 1970s to 14–19% in the 2020s, in every generation.
  - In the 2020s, foreign-born Catholics are the most likely to oppose abortion for any reason (62% vs 47% third-plus generation) and to hold papal infallibility (56% vs 37%).
- **Orthodox Christians:** only 27–205 GSS respondents per item, below the 300 floor, so no models were fitted. Global and Orthodox coverage waits on EVS/WVS/ISSP access (milestone 4).

## 2026-11-03 · Session 1, plan for milestone 1 (written before the run)

- Data: GSS 1972–2024 cumulative cross-section, release 3a (NORC; `data/download.sh`, checksums in `data/SHA256SUMS` match the copy `research/marriage-divorce-evidence` records). 75,699 respondents; Catholic (relig 2), Orthodox (relig 10) and the three Protestant traditions of Steensland et al. (`reltrad` 1–3).
- Targets: 12 binary items coded as the answer consistent with Catholic teaching (Catechism paragraph in `src/gss.py`) or a practice level, plus ever-divorced among the ever-married and, for Catholics, papal infallibility.
- The GSS asks many items on only some ballots, so most "missing" answers are missing by design (at random). That makes it a fair test of imputation: predict an item a respondent was not asked from the ones they were.
- Planned analysis: 5-fold weighted cross-validation within each tradition. Models are the decade prior, L2 logistic regression and gradient boosting. Metrics are weighted AUC, log loss and Brier, with paired respondent bootstrap intervals (300 draws). Then a temporal hold-out (train ≤2014, test 2016–2024), an immigrant-generation transfer, a cross-tradition transfer and permutation importance.
- **H1.** Other answers predict the sexual-morality items well beyond the prior (AUC ≥ 0.75 among Catholics). Divorce law and ever-divorced are much harder (AUC < 0.70). *Refuted if* sexual-morality AUCs are below 0.75, or divorce-law AUC reaches them.
- **H2.** Gradient boosting beats logistic regression by less than 0.01 AUC on most items, as flexibility did not help in `marriage-divorce-evidence`. *Refuted if* the bootstrap interval for the boosting minus logistic AUC gap lies above 0.01 on most items.
- **H3.** A model fitted on third-plus-generation Catholics ranks first-generation (foreign-born) Catholics almost as well as the pooled model does (AUC loss ≤ 0.03), but its level is off: the mean prediction misses the first-generation share by more than 5 points on at least a third of items. *Refuted if* the AUC loss exceeds 0.03 on most items, or levels transfer within 5 points everywhere.
- **H4.** A Catholic-fitted model ranks Evangelical and Mainline Protestants within 0.03 AUC of their own models. It ranks Black Protestants worse, because party and ideology carry less information about sexual morality there. *Refuted if* the Black Protestant loss is no larger than the other two.

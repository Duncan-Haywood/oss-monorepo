A stereo twin that adds Gaussian noise to depth gets the median right: what does it get wrong about over-range errors, safety margins and averaging compared with real disparity-space noise?

research/stereo-twin · 14 tests

Gaussian-depth-noise stereo twin vs real disparity-space noise: median exact, gated mean biased by ≈s², over-range errors up to 191× more frequent at 40 m, a twin 3σ margin exceeded 10× as often, mean-depth averaging floors while averaging disparity converges (negative result at the 60 m range floor). Stylised.

Abstract

Real sensor: disparity noise σ = 0.25 px on a 700 px, 0.12 m rig (matches only above 1 px, so 84 m), depth = reciprocal of disparity; twin: Gaussian depth noise with the linearised sd. The median is exact, but the (gated) mean is biased by about s2 (+1.5% at 40 m) and over-estimates of distance are far more common than the twin says: at 40 m P(depth > 1.5Z) is 0.0026 vs 10−5 (191×), and a twin mean + 3σ margin is exceeded 10× as often as designed. Matching the variance leaves 41× on that tail. Averaging 1024 frames of depth floors at 1.5% error where the twin predicts 0.4%, whereas averaging disparity converges; at 60 m the range floor loses 5.5% of looks and flips or removes these effects (reported as a negative result). Stylised: Gaussian i.i.d. disparity noise, no outliers or calibration error.

A rigid-body twin of a cart carrying liquid predicts no slosh: how wrong is that, what does a slosh-safe move cost, and what does a stale frequency calibration do to it?

Abstract

Real cart: 80 kg body with a damped spring-mass slosh mode (20 kg liquid, 0.5 Hz at full fill); twin: one rigid mass. The twin gets the centre of mass exactly (RK4 error ~10−14 m) but predicts zero slosh; for a bang-bang rest-to-rest move the real residual is 4μa0sin2(ωT/4)/ω2, matched by exact propagation and RK4. The twin’s minimum-time move (3 m, 4.90 s) leaves 10.7 cm of slosh, 10× a 1 cm tolerance; the fastest move meeting it takes 7.60 s (55% slower) because zero-residual times are whole multiples of two slosh periods. Calibrating ω at a full tank is harmless down to half fill (−5% frequency, 9.5 mm) but at 25% fill the smallest safe null-tuned move takes 28 s vs 8.7 s recalibrated, worse than the tuning-free bound 24.7 s; at fixed acceleration the first unsafe null order is exactly ⌊asin(√ρ)/(π|ε|)⌋+1. Stylised: one linear slosh mode, bang-bang force, no shaper optimisation, no nonlinear slosh.

Abstract

Follow-up to slosh-twin. What is a digital twin worth for training a controller?

research/twin-transfer · 12 tests

What a digital twin is worth for training a controller: exact LQ sim-to-real regret with a stability cliff, a validation blind spot (zero score gap yet mistrained control without probing), and a twin worth exactly σ²/(vδ²) real samples at any accuracy.

Abstract

In a scalar linear-quadratic plant the twin-trained gain has exact deployment regret, asymmetric with a stability cliff: underestimating actuation to 0.21× is infinitely costly while 2× over costs 0.22. A twin can score perfectly on passive logs (log-score gap exactly 0) yet mistrain the controller, and closed-loop logs are blind along Δa = kΔb, so validation needs designed probing. Used as a ridge prior, a twin with bias δ is worth exactly σ2/(vδ2) real probe steps at every accuracy target (22 steps at δ=0.3), and removes small-sample divergence (3.1% to 0 at n=10). Matched to simulation within 1–4%. Stylised.

Randomizing the twin's input gain over ±ε trains a more conservative gain (monotone in ε; small-width law kε−k* = −(εb)2Jkbb/(6Jkk)) at a quartic price when the twin was right, and moves the deployment stability cliff out by about 15%. Abstract

For a twin refitted from dedicated experiments rather than filtered from operating data, the second-order-optimal schedule is closed-form: interval T* = (4cσ2/(vρqd2))1/3, with experiment downtime, estimation noise and drift regret exactly equal at the optimum, so excess cost scales as qd1/3 (fitted slope 0.333) and 10× cheaper experiments lower it only 2.2×. A provider paid Brier score on the twin’s parameter reports its posterior mean; one paid the deployed control cost reports the Bayes gain, which is more conservative (0.778 vs 0.823 at posterior sd 0.3, mean 1) and equals domain randomization under the provider’s own uncertainty. What does a light-tailed simulated human do to a collaborative robot's timeout?

research/handover-twin · 6 tests

When a collaborative robot should give up waiting for a human: J'(τ)=S(τ)(w−c·h(τ)) makes exponential/Weibull simulated humans corner-only (never abort), 3.7% regret in the base case rising to 37–81% for heavy tails and cheap aborts, plug-in lognormal fit regret 1/n (delta method within 11%).

Abstract

A robot that waits for a human and aborts after τ has cost J(τ) = wE[min(T,τ)] + cP(T>τ), with slope S(τ)(w − c·h(τ)). Simulated humans with constant or increasing hazard (exponential, Weibull) therefore have only corner-optimal timeouts and, when aborting costs more than the mean wait, never give up. Against a lognormal human (σ=1.2, an assumed model) the interior optimum is 9.4 s and the light-tailed twin’s regret is 3.7% of the optimum, rising to 37% (σ=1.6) and 81% (σ=2) for heavier tails and cheap aborts. A lognormal twin with the right mean and σ off by 0.2 recovers all but 0.5%. Fitting the correct family from n real trials costs regret ∝ 1/n, matched by the delta method to within 11% (0.0323 at n=10, 0.00034 at n=1000). Stylised: scalar i.i.d. response time, one fixed timeout, no real human data.

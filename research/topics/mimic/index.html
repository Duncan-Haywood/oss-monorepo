How well do prompt-free detectors tell people from a plainly prompted LLM, and do they carry over to unseen kinds of text?

research/mimic-contexts · 10 tests

How well do prompt-free detectors tell people from a plainly prompted LLM, and do they carry over to unseen kinds of text? Eight RAID families split by family: n-gram detector AUROC 0.93 and Binoculars-style 0.80 on held-out families, length at chance. Preliminary first step of track 11.

Abstract

Eight text families from RAID (300 human and 300 plain-prompt chat-LLM texts each), split by family: train on five, hold out poetry, reviews and wiki. Prompt-free detectors on the held-out families: character/word n-gram logistic regression AUROC 0.93 (95% interval 0.91–0.94), a Binoculars-style zero-shot score from Qwen2.5-0.5B 0.80 (0.77–0.83), perplexity alone 0.76; a length-only detector is at chance (0.52). Zero-shot scores are weak on mpt-chat text (0.65) while n-grams are not (0.95). Limits: the ‘imitator’ is RAID’s own chat generations, not a prompted or optimised one; three chat models; no author-group labels for fairness checks. Preliminary: first step of track 11, no game yet.

Do the file sizes and line counts of this repository follow Benford’s law?

research/benford-repo · 5 tests

Do this repository's file sizes follow Benford's law? Not as a whole (byte MAD 0.018, nonconforming) because templated files repeat the same size thousands of times; counting each distinct size once gives acceptable conformity (MAD 0.0068).

Abstract

First-digit frequencies of the byte sizes and line counts of the 35,648 git-tracked files at one commit, scored against Benford’s law by chi-square and Nigrini’s mean absolute deviation (MAD). All files together are nonconforming (byte MAD 0.0183, line MAD 0.0278), because a few sizes repeat thousands of times (1,071 bytes 1,297 times, 47 bytes 993 times, 4 lines 1,822 times). Counting each distinct size once, byte sizes conform acceptably (MAD 0.0068, n = 12,429). By extension, .json and .html stay nonconforming even deduplicated, and distinct Python line counts are acceptable (MAD 0.0103, n = 476). Descriptive: one commit of one generated, templated repository; an illustration of when the law fails, not a test of the law.

Can prompt search alone move a frozen 0.5B imitator past a prompted 0.5B detector, and which proposer does it best at equal budget?

research/mimic-loop · 17 tests

Can prompt search alone move a frozen 0.5B imitator past a prompted 0.5B detector? A stylised first run of the imitation game, four proposers at equal budget on two families: the starting detector is below chance, proposers cannot be ranked at this size, and length is a major confound.

Abstract

A first, stylised run of the imitation game: Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct as imitator and detector, only prompts change, alternating rounds on two families (reddit, reviews) with four proposers (random, GA, GrIPS-style, local-LLM) at 4 evaluations per side per round. The plain detector starts below chance (paired accuracy 0.12), the final detectors reach 0.12–0.58 on 24 test pairs with intervals of about ±0.2, and the proposers cannot be ranked. Length is a major confound: imitators that wrote longer texts fooled the detector more. One seed; a harness and a first look, not a detection result.

Does the prompt-only imitation game settle or cycle, and does beating the latest detector mean beating the others?

research/mimic-crossplay · 12 tests

Does the prompt-only imitation game settle or cycle? Every imitator prompt from mimic-loop against every detector prompt (0.5B on both sides): pure equilibria below chance (reddit 0.396, reviews 0.292), no cycles at a noise margin, and text length tracks detector accuracy. Stylised, one loop seed; model-size question open.

Abstract

Follow-up to mimic-loop. Every imitator prompt any of its eight runs produced is scored against every detector prompt (Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct on both sides, 48 titles per cell, detector paired accuracy, 0.50 = chance), giving a 10×7 matrix on reddit and 9×10 on reviews. Both zero-sum games have a pure equilibrium with value below chance (reddit 0.396, 95% title-bootstrap 0.28 to 0.47; reviews 0.292, 0.17 to 0.34): no detector prompt guarantees chance, and no cell exceeds 0.56. On reviews the unadapted plain imitator prompt is the equilibrium imitator. Mean text length correlates with detector accuracy (Spearman 0.76 on reddit, 0.30 on reviews). No cycles at a 0.15 noise margin; overfitting to the latest opponent is small and not established. Stylised: one loop seed, a 0.5B detector that starts below chance. The model-size question is left open.

Negative: the prompted Qwen2.5-0.5B detector is inverted (mean AUROC 0.278 plain, 0.293 universal on held-out families, none above 0.5 on any family) while the n-gram detector gets 0.93. Abstract

Prompted-detector half of mimic-anycontext. Does MAUVE, a distribution-level score, rank human-vs-machine gaps the way a text detector does?

research/mimic-mauve · 4 tests

Does MAUVE rank human-vs-machine gaps the way a text detector does? Across 20 RAID family × model cells MAUVE (lexical embedding) and a cross-fit n-gram detector agree loosely (Spearman −0.60) and disagree on recipes and news; every cell is far below the human-vs-human null (0.92 to 0.99). Preliminary, no GPT-2 features.

Abstract

MAUVE between human and RAID chat-model text in 20 family × model cells (TF-IDF/SVD embedding, 5 draws of 48–100 texts), against a cross-fit n-gram detector on the same texts. Human-vs-human MAUVE is 0.92–0.99; every human-vs-model cell is at most 0.83. MAUVE and AUROC rank cells loosely alike (Spearman −0.60, p = 0.005; wiki/mistral-chat is hardest for both), but disagree on recipes (AUROC 0.999, MAUVE 0.32) and news (0.97, 0.75), and MAUVE does not track the distinct-2 ratio (ρ = 0.24, n.s.). Preliminary: the embedding is lexical, not MAUVE’s usual GPT-2 features, so values are not comparable with the literature; plain-prompt text only, no LLM run.

Can an imitator fool a prompt-free human-vs-machine text detector with cheap surface edits, such as typos, contractions or dropping the chat preamble?

research/mimic-evade · 5 tests

Can cheap surface edits (typos, contractions, no chat preamble) fool the n-gram human-vs-machine detector? Most move held-out AUROC under 0.005; 2% typos cost 0.012 and keeping half the text 0.051 (from 0.927), and a refit detector wins it back.

Abstract

The mimic-contexts n-gram detector (held-out AUROC 0.927) is scored on poetry, reviews and wiki after scripted edits to the machine texts only. Markdown stripping, preamble removal and contractions move it by at most 0.005; 2% typos cost 0.012 (interval −0.014 to −0.010); keeping the first half costs 0.051, the largest drop, and no edit approaches chance. A detector refit on edited training text recovers and exceeds the baseline (0.956 to 0.969), partly by learning text length. Lowercasing and whitespace edits are null by construction. Preliminary: scripted rules, not LLM-chosen edits; one seed.

How many words must an imitator change to evade a word n-gram human-vs-machine detector that it can read?

research/mimic-whitebox · 5 tests

How many words must an imitator change to evade a word n-gram human-vs-machine detector it can read? Deleting the 20 most machine-ward word types per text takes held-out AUROC from 0.948 to chance (0.487); a refit detector recovers to 0.94-1.00.

Abstract

A word 1-2 gram logistic-regression detector (held-out AUROC 0.948 on poetry, reviews and wiki) is attacked by an imitator that knows its weights and deletes or swaps the k most machine-ward word types in each of its own texts. Deleting 10 word types drops AUROC to 0.738, 20 to 0.487 (chance) and 40 to 0.150; swapping 20 gives 0.317. A detector refit on text attacked the same way recovers to 0.94–1.00. Stylised: scripted edits with no fluency check, one seed, one detector family, no LLM run.

How many detector queries does an imitator need to evade a human-vs-machine text detector it cannot read?

research/mimic-blackbox · 5 tests

How many detector queries does an imitator need to evade a word n-gram human-vs-machine detector it cannot read? About 100 score queries per text, deleting the 20 best word types, take held-out AUROC from 0.948 to 0.520 (white-box 0.487); blind deletion does nothing. Stylised, no fluency check.

Abstract

The word 1-2 gram detector of mimic-whitebox (held-out AUROC 0.948) is attacked by an imitator that sees only the score of each text it submits. It deletes each of Q candidate word types in turn, reads the score drop and removes the k largest. With k=20, 25 queries per text give AUROC 0.873, 50 give 0.722 and about 95 give 0.520, against 0.487 for the white-box attack that reads the weights; blind deletion (0 queries) changes nothing. Stylised and preliminary: a linear detector, one seed, no fluency check on the edited text, and no retraining of the detector against the attack.

Does a prompt-free human-vs-machine detector carry over to a generator or decoding it never saw?

research/mimic-genshift · 5 tests

Does a prompt-free detector carry over to a generator or decoding it never saw? The mimic-contexts n-gram detector loses 0.01 to 0.02 AUROC on a chat model left out of the fit (0.96 to 0.98 left out) and 0.025 to 0.05 on a left-out decoding (sampling 0.936); averaging over four same-source texts lifts AUROC to 0.999. Preliminary, plain-prompt RAID text only.

Abstract

The mimic-contexts n-gram detector fit on all eight RAID families, scored on text from a chat model or decoding left out of the fit (items split in two halves by source_id, halves swapped). AUROC falls from 0.991 to 0.978 (llama-chat), 0.983 to 0.971 (mistral-chat) and 0.981 to 0.959 (mpt-chat); leaving out sampling decoding costs 0.984 → 0.936 and greedy 0.989 → 0.964, with held-out wiki the weak spot (0.83 on sampling). Averaging the score over k same-source texts lifts AUROC from 0.971 (k=1) to 0.999 (k=4). Human batches are more varied than model batches (recipes distinct-2 0.589 against 0.373 for llama and 0.399 for mistral), but mpt-chat matches or exceeds human variety on poetry and reddit. Preliminary: plain-prompt RAID text, three chat models each present in only some families (generator and family partly confounded), one detector type, no LLM run.

The two scores are uncorrelated within class, but averaging them lifts held-out AUROC only from 0.926 to 0.935 (paired gain +0.009, interval −0.010 to +0.026); fitted stacks do no better. Abstract

The mimic-contexts n-gram detector and its Binoculars-style score (Qwen2.5-0.5B) are uncorrelated within each class (−0.02 human, 0.02 machine), yet combining them barely helps on the held-out families poetry, reviews and wiki: n-gram AUROC 0.926, mean of z-scores 0.935 (paired gain +0.009, 95% interval −0.010 to +0.026), fitted logistic stacks −0.003 and −0.019. How much text does a prompt-free human-vs-machine detector need, and what does it key on?

How much text does a prompt-free detector need, and what does it key on? The mimic-contexts n-gram detector on held-out families: AUROC 0.65 at 50 characters, 0.84 at 400, 0.93 at 1500; stripping punctuation or shuffling words costs at most 0.014. Preliminary, plain-prompt RAID text only.

Abstract

The mimic-contexts n-gram detector (TF-IDF character and word n-grams, fitted on five families) is scored on poetry, reviews and wiki after cutting each text to its first N characters: AUROC 0.65 at 50, 0.75 at 100, 0.84 at 400 (the length the imitation-game loop shows its detector) and 0.93 at 1,500; refitting on the short text recovers only 0.02–0.07. If a human-vs-machine detector's threshold is set for a 5% false-positive rate, does that rate hold on a kind of text it never saw?

If a detector's threshold is set for a 5% false-positive rate, does it hold on an unseen text type? Leave-one-family-out with the mimic-contexts n-gram detector: realised rate 0.00 to 0.54 (abstracts 0.54, books 0.18), and recipes catch 1.7% of machine text. Preliminary, plain-prompt RAID text only.

Abstract

The mimic-contexts n-gram detector is fit on seven RAID families and its threshold set so 5% of human text is flagged (out-of-fold), then applied to the eighth. Realised false-positive rates run from 0.00 to 0.54: abstracts 0.54, books 0.18, while news, poetry, recipes, reddit, reviews and wiki sit at 0.00–0.06. On recipes the cut-off is too strict instead: it catches 1.7% of machine text where a threshold set on recipes would catch 46%. How many human texts does it take to re-set a detector's threshold for a 5% false-positive rate on a new kind of text?

research/mimic-recalib · 5 tests

How many human texts does it take to re-set a detector's threshold for a 5% false-positive rate on a new kind of text? About 80: with the plain 95th percentile k = 20 gives a mean false-positive rate of 9% (36% chance above 10%), k = 80 gives 6.1%; a conformal rule holds 4.9% but costs recall and needs 19+ texts. Stylised, RAID text, one n-gram detector.

Abstract

Follow-up to mimic-threshold. The n-gram detector is fit on seven RAID families; k human texts of the eighth set its cut-off, 500 random draws per cell. Taking the empirical 95th percentile, k = 20 gives a mean false-positive rate of 9% and a 36% chance of exceeding 10%; k = 80 gives 6.1% and 10%; k = 160 gives 5.5%. A conformal rank rule holds the mean at 4.7–4.9% from k = 20 but gives up recall (0.66 against 0.79 at k = 20) and cannot flag anything below 19 calibration texts. Even at k = 80 one draw lands anywhere from 1.7% to 8.8% (10th–90th percentile). Recalibration fixes the false-accusation rate, not the detector: recipes still catches under half of machine text. Preliminary: plain-prompt RAID text, one detector, calibration from the same population as the test, no LLM run.

How many texts from one source does a weak human-vs-machine text detector need before a batch verdict is reliable?

research/mimic-batch · 7 tests

How many texts from one source does a weak human-vs-machine detector need before a batch verdict is reliable? Four all-machine texts give AUROC 0.997 (one text: 0.935); a quarter-machine source needs about 32; but a threshold set on 150 human texts drifts from 7% to 15% false flags at k = 32. Preliminary, RAID text, one n-gram detector.

Abstract

The mimic-contexts n-gram detector scores a held-out RAID family; a batch is k independent texts and its statistic is their mean score. One text separates people from a plainly prompted LLM at AUROC 0.935 (mean of 8 families); four all-machine texts reach 0.997 and eight reach 1.00. If only half the batch is machine, k = 8 gives 0.98; a quarter needs about 32 (0.985); at 10% machine 32 texts give only 0.82. The catch is calibration: with a threshold set on 150 human texts, the share of human batches flagged drifts from 7.4% at k = 1 to 15% on average at k = 32 (books 60%), because the batch mean's spread shrinks while the calibration error does not. Preliminary: plain-prompt text, i.i.d. batches, one detector, no LLM run.

Does a classifier two-sample test catch digital-twin errors that a paired trajectory error misses?

research/mimic-twinc2st · 8 tests

Does a classifier two-sample test catch digital-twin errors that a paired trajectory error misses? Stylised pendulum: boosted-tree AUROC 0.72 for a friction error whose RMSE is a quarter of the sensor noise, 0.92 for Gaussian-vs-Student-t noise at zero paired error. Preliminary, simulated only.

Abstract

A stylised pendulum with Coulomb friction and heavy-tailed (Student-t) sensor noise is the ‘real’ system; twins differ in friction, noise shape or noise level. A classifier two-sample test (5-fold CV AUROC, 800 vs 800 trajectories, 3 seeds) separates real from twin trajectories with boosted trees: a friction error of 0.03 (10%) that moves the noise-free trajectory by only 0.006 rad RMSE — a quarter of the 0.02 rad noise sd — gives AUROC 0.72, and 0.91 at 20%. A twin with the right dynamics but Gaussian instead of Student-t noise of the same sd has paired error exactly 0 yet AUROC 0.92 once second-difference features are added (0.59 on raw angles). A logistic regression on raw angles stays at chance (0.51) throughout, because the start angles are symmetric. Limits: one toy system, two noise models, three seeds, a handful of classifier settings; simulated only, nothing about real hardware. Preliminary, no LLM involved: a side piece of track 11.

Abstract

A kernel two-sample test (unbiased RBF MMD, median-heuristic bandwidth) is compared with a gradient-boosted classifier two-sample test on the stylised pendulum of mimic-twinc2st. Rejection uses the 95th percentile of the exact twin’s own statistic. Raw-trajectory MMD has essentially no power against a Coulomb-friction error at n = 100–400 per class (0.05–0.06 at the median bandwidth, 0.37 at 0.03× for −20% friction), where the classifier reaches 1.00 at −20% from n = 200. MMD on second differences detects a +25% noise-level error at 0.97 from n = 100, where the raw-feature classifier is at 0.08–0.25. Stylised; 24 classifier replicates per cell, so those intervals are wide.

Does a digital twin that a classifier can tell apart from the real system also make a worse controller?

research/mimic-twinregret · 9 tests

Does a digital twin that a classifier can tell apart from the real system also make a worse controller? Stylised pendulum: 6 of 15 wrong twins have zero controller regret at C2ST AUROC 0.58 to 0.999; the worst regret (3.8%) comes from a twin with no sensor noise and zero paired error. Preliminary, simulated only.

Abstract

In the stylised pendulum of mimic-twinc2st with a torque input, a PD controller on noisy feedback is tuned on each wrong twin (48-point gain grid, 200 twin start angles) and scored on 2,000 held-out start angles on the ‘real’ system. Regret is its real cost minus the best grid point’s (0.259). Six of 15 wrong twins have regret exactly 0 in all 12 tuning seeds although the classifier two-sample test separates them from the real system with AUROC 0.58–0.999, among them g/L −20%, whose paired error is the largest (0.25 rad). The largest regret, 0.0099 (3.8%), comes from a twin with no sensor noise at all, whose paired error is 0. Across the 15 twins AUROC and regret correlate only loosely (Spearman 0.46, p = 0.08) and paired RMSE not at all (−0.28). Detecting a twin is not the same as the twin mattering for the task. Limits: one system, one task, a coarse grid that quantises regret, 12 tuning seeds. Stylised, simulated, no LLM: a side piece of track 11.

Follow-up to mimic-twinswing on the same stylised swing-up pendulum, removing two confounds: the best pump gain sat at the edge of the grid, and the controller's gravity constant was fixed. With ke up to 32 (126 controllers, 14 twins, 8 seeds) gravity errors remain the costly twins and sensor-noise errors stay under 0.0010. Letting the controller use the twin's own g/L made gravity errors worse: g/L −20% regret rises from 0.013 to 0.177 and g/L +20% falls from 0.036 to 0.017. Spearman AUROC vs regret is ρ = 0.63 (p = 0.021) but is driven by four gravity twins, and is not comparable with the earlier twin set. Preliminary: one toy system, simulated only.

Does domain randomization over a wrong digital twin cut the controller regret of tuning on one wrong twin?

Does tuning a controller on a spread of randomized twins cut the regret of tuning on one wrong twin? Stylised pendulum: it brings the no-noise twin's regret from 0.0098 to 0.00006, but a very wide range raises a doubled-noise twin's worst-seed regret 7.5-fold. Preliminary, no LLM.

Abstract

Follow-up to mimic-twinregret on the same stylised pendulum and noisy-feedback PD task. Gains are tuned on 200 start angles spread over 8 randomized twins (noise level, Coulomb and viscous friction, g/L each uniform on nominal ± w× the real value) and scored on 2,000 held-out real start angles, over 24 tuning seeds. For the no-noise twin, whose single-twin regret is 0.0098, randomization with w = 0.5 brings it to 0.00006, the same as the exact twin. No-Coulomb and g/L +20% twins fall from 0.0014 to 0.00012 at w = 1–2. It does not always help: for a doubled-noise twin regret stays about 0.003 for w ≤ 1 and at w = 2 the mean rises to 0.0057 with a worst seed 7.5× the single-twin worst. Limits: one system and task, a 48-point grid that quantises regret, one randomization family with a shared width. Stylised, simulated, no LLM.

How many real trajectories does a classifier test need to flag a wrong digital twin, and are the twins it flags the ones that cost a controller?

research/mimic-twinpower · 5 tests

How many real trajectories does a classifier test need to flag a wrong digital twin, and are the twins it flags the ones that cost a controller? Stylised pendulum: all six twins with controller regret of at least 0.001 are flagged by 80 real trajectories, and so are three that cost nothing. Preliminary, simulated only.

Abstract

On the stylised pendulum of mimic-twinregret, a boosted-tree C2ST with n real and n twin trajectories flags a twin when its AUROC beats the 95th percentile of the exact-twin null at the same n (20 replicates per cell, n = 10 to 160). The six twins whose controller regret is at least 0.001 are all flagged in at least 80% of replicates by n = 80 (by 40 for the four noise-level and noise-shape twins), so the test does not miss costly twins at that size. It also flags three twins that cost nothing (g/L −20%, noise ×0.5, noise ×0.75) by n = 40–80, while Coulomb −10% and −20% stay unflagged at 160. Spearman correlation of power at 80 with regret is 0.52 (p = 0.048, 15 twins). A flag is a cheap alarm, not a verdict on usefulness. Limits: one system, one task, 20 replicates, a null threshold that is degenerate at n ≤ 20. Stylised, simulated, no LLM.

Can an imitator that changes its own code fool a human-vs-bot cursor detector, and does shared feedback help either side?

research/mimic-cursor · 9 tests

Can an imitator that changes its own code fool a human-vs-bot cursor detector, and does shared feedback help? On DUX cursor strokes of 46 people, a held-out reference detector catches every final imitator at AUROC 0.99 to 1.00 in all four feedback conditions; two-way sharing gave the strongest detectors in cross-play (0.81 vs 0.70 blind, interval includes 0), and an 8x larger search stops at 0.88, the level of replaying real strokes. Preliminary.

Abstract

Browser use, the first subject of the adversarial-imitation track beyond writing. Cursor strokes of 46 people from DUX (CC-BY-4.0) against an imitator built from code components and a detector that picks 3 of 11 feature functions, 4 feedback conditions × 8 seeds × 30 rounds at equal budget. A reference detector the loop never saw catches every final imitator at AUROC 0.99 to 1.00; detector explanations and shared imitator code did not make a better imitator. Detectors trained with two-way sharing did best against other runs’ imitators (cross-play AUROC 0.81 vs 0.70 blind), but the paired interval includes zero. Can a generator imitate a person's webcam emotion signals well enough to fool a detector?

Can a generator imitate a person's webcam emotion signals well enough to fool a detector? Abstract

Webcam, the third subject of the adversarial-imitation track, signals first: no pixels or faces. Ten-second windows of 12 facial-coding channels (Affectiva scores logged by DUX, CC-BY-4.0) from 46 people, split by person. Six fixed generators from constant to AR(1) with channel correlation, holds and spikes, and an imitator that tunes its own generator code against a 3-feature-group detector for 30 rounds (8 seeds; 4 seeds at 8× the budget), are all separated from held-out people by a boosted-tree reference detector the loop never saw at AUROC 0.997 or higher. Only replaying whole windows of other people gets near chance (0.61, which is the gap between people). Preliminary and stylised: one context, a fixed component library, and windows biased toward moments when the person was using the mouse or keyboard.

Does giving a webcam-signal generator real heavy tails and bursts fool a detector?

research/mimic-webcam-tails · 5 tests

Does giving a webcam-signal generator real heavy tails and bursts fool a detector? No: copula and quiet/burst generators still separate from held-out people at AUROC 1.000 (8 seeds); restoring per-window level and spread brings level and spread to near chance, but extremes (0.995) still give it away; copying a real window's value set (G6) puts those at chance too, yet all-groups AUROC stays 0.998 on temporal structure.

Abstract

Follow-up to mimic-webcam on DUX facial-coding scores of 46 people. Generators that take each channel's marginal from the training people (Gaussian copula through empirical quantiles; quiet/burst regimes) still separate from 14 held-out people at reference AUROC 1.000 (8 seeds). Matching marginals alone made level and spread easier to catch (0.99–1.00 vs 0.91 and 0.94 for the Gaussian baseline); restoring per-window level and spread brings them to 0.54 and 0.57, but extremes (0.995), holds (0.995) and autocorrelation (0.94) still give it away. A sixth generator that copies a real window’s whole value set puts level, spread, extremes and floor at chance (0.44–0.46), yet all-groups AUROC stays 0.998: holds, autocorrelation, roughness and cross-channel structure carry it. Stylised: hand-built generators, one context, post-hoc final variants.

Why is block-stitched real webcam signal still caught: the joins, or mixing people?

Abstract

Ceiling study on the DUX webcam expression signals: 100-frame windows stitched from real blocks of 5 to 50 frames, scored by a boosted-tree detector on 14 held-out people (8 seeds). With 50-frame blocks random stitching is caught at AUROC 0.887; blocks from one person only give 0.687; join-matched blocks 0.796; both together 0.588, equal to replaying whole windows (0.597).

Open-source purpose-built robots

Robots built for one job and designed from the task outward: when a specialised body beats a general-purpose arm, on cost, speed and reliability.

When does a purpose-built gantry beat a general two-link arm at tray pick-and-place?

research/osrobot-specialise · 10 tests

When does a purpose-built gantry beat a general two-link arm at tray pick-and-place? At 2 mm tolerance the arm wins only between 0.36 and 0.99 m of work area, and the band depends on assumed costs (stylised).

Abstract

A stdlib model compares an XY gantry with an equal-link planar arm over a square work area, on cost per good pick per hour. At 2 mm tolerance the gantry wins up to 0.35 m and above about 1.0 m; the arm wins between 0.36 and 0.99 m. Tolerance below 1 mm removes the arm’s band, and the band depends heavily on the assumed cost coefficients (halving the gantry’s removes it). Stylised: every cost, mass, speed and accuracy figure is assumed, and only one task is modelled.

Does co-designing an arm's link lengths and controller beat tuning the controller on a fixed body?

research/osrobot-morph · 8 tests

Does co-designing an arm's link split and controller beat tuning the controller on a fixed body? Co-design gained 1.9% in held-out move time (0.283 against 0.289 s), within three-seed noise; tuning the controller mattered about 37%. Stylised, preliminary, mostly negative.

Abstract

A stdlib planar two-link arm model searches the split of a fixed 0.50 m reach together with PD gains, against an equal-link body with tuned gains, at equal search budget. The tray is reachable only for splits of 0.40 to 0.60. Co-design gained 1.9% in held-out move time (0.283 against 0.289 s), within three-seed noise, while tuning the controller cut hand-set gains' 0.460 s by about 37%. Stylised, preliminary and mostly negative: one body parameter and an assumed torque limit.

Can a small twin of a liquid-handling bench catch protocol errors before they run, and how much pipetting error does a serial dilution tolerate?

research/osrobot-lab · 10 tests

Can a small twin of a liquid-handling bench catch protocol errors before they run, and how much pipetting error does a serial dilution tolerate? It flags 366 of 522 injected faults against 100 for a syntax-only baseline, and an assumed error curve gives a final-well CV of 0.187 at 10 µL and 0.035 at 100 µL transfers (stylised, circular fault set).

Abstract

A stdlib deck-and-pipette twin tracks tips, well volumes, liquid labels and straight-line travel over labware, and checks protocols before they run. On 522 injected faults across three reference protocols it flags 366 (355 with the intended code) where a syntax-only baseline flags 100; the misses are mostly equivalent mutants. Collisions appear only where a move crosses taller labware (a tube rack in one protocol; forced travel heights up to 70 mm collide, 90 mm and above do not). With an assumed pipetting-error curve, a 12-well two-fold serial dilution has a final-well CV of 0.187 at 10 µL transfers, 0.100 at 20 and 0.035 at 100 µL. Stylised and circular by construction: the faults match the checks, and every geometry, timing and error value is assumed.

Does the real Opentrons simulator catch the protocol faults a small stdlib liquid-handling twin catches?

research/osrobot-labreal · 7 tests

Does the real Opentrons simulator catch the faults the stdlib liquid-handling twin catches? On the same 522 seeded faults it raises on 270 and the twin flags 366; everything the simulator catches the twin catches too, while tip reuse, a 5 µL dispense and a half-empty source pass the simulator. Mostly policy differences; the fault set was written for the twin (stylised).

Abstract

The 522 seeded faults of osrobot-lab (three protocols, 11 fault kinds) were translated into Opentrons Python API scripts and run through the real simulator (opentrons==8.0.0, OT-2, p300). It raises on 270 of them against the twin's 366, and every one it catches the twin also catches. It catches scripting errors (unknown labware or well, missing or doubled tip pick-up, 350 µL or 5000 µL aspirate) and accepts tip reuse, a 5 µL dispense, a tip left on and a half-empty source, even with starting liquids declared. Those are mostly policy differences, not simulator misses. Stylised: the fault set was written for the twin.

Does searching an arm’s joint count, link lengths and mount, with its controller tuned in the loop, beat a fixed three-link body at the same cost?

research/osrobot-bodysearch · 9 tests

Does searching a planar arm's joint count, links and mount, with its controller tuned, beat a fixed three-link body at equal cost? On a shelf task with a tool-down constraint, 0.95 held-out success against 0.26; on a long position-only reach, no gain (stylised, kinematic).

Abstract

A stdlib model searches link lengths, joint count and mount position of a planar arm, with its inverse-kinematics controller tuned in the loop, against a fixed three-link body at an equal cost budget (300 evaluations, 10 seeds, held-out targets). On a shelf task that needs the tool pointing down, the search reaches 0.95 success against 0.26 for the fixed body with a tuned controller and 0.50 with the mount also tuned; on a long position-only reach the fixed body already succeeds and the search adds nothing. Random search beat a (1+4) evolution strategy here, weakly. Stylised: kinematics and static torque only (no MuJoCo dynamics), and every cost, mass and limit is assumed.

How heavy must a purpose-built UAV for targeted observation be, and when does a hover-capable airframe stop being worth its weight?

research/osrobot-uav · 8 tests

How heavy must a purpose-built UAV for targeted observation be, and when is a hover-capable airframe worth its weight? At 3 km out and 20 min on station a fixed wing closes at 1.27 kg against 2.14 kg for a quadrotor and 2.08 kg for a VTOL wing, and the quadrotor stops closing at 45 min (stylised, assumed constants, no flight stack run).

Abstract

A stdlib mass-closure sizing model closes a quadrotor, a fixed wing and a fixed wing with a vertical-lift system for the same mission, choosing rotor diameter or wing area for least mass. For 3 km out, 20 min on station and a 0.5 kg payload the plain fixed wing is lightest (1.27 kg), against 2.14 kg for the quadrotor and 2.08 kg for the VTOL wing. The quadrotor does not close at 45 min on station; the VTOL wing is lighter than it from 30 min or beyond about 10 km. Stylised: every technology constant is assumed, no flight stack was run, and a fixed wing cannot hover or launch unaided.

Does a steady wind change which purpose-built observation UAV is lightest, and what is wind worth to a multirotor?

research/osrobot-uavwind · 8 tests

Does a steady wind change which purpose-built observation UAV is lightest? At 3 km and 20 min on station the fixed wing stays lightest from 0 to 18 m/s (1.27 to 1.50 kg) while the quadrotor goes 2.14 to 7.0 kg; the multirotor's still-air win at 1 min on station ends near 10 m/s. Stylised, steady known wind only.

Abstract

A stdlib extension of the osrobot-uav mass-closure sizing adds a steady wind: headwind out, tailwind back, airspeed re-optimised, and station-keeping at airspeed equal to the wind. For 3 km out, 20 min on station and 0.5 kg the plain fixed wing stays lightest at every wind from 0 to 18 m/s (1.27 kg at 0, 1.30 at 10, 1.50 at 18); the quadrotor goes 2.14 → 2.33 → 2.86 → 7.0 kg at 0, 10, 15 and 18 m/s and the VTOL wing 2.08 → 2.54. The multirotor’s one win in still air (1 min on station) lasts to about 8 m/s. Designs sized for still air keep 17.2 (fixed wing) and 18.5 min (multirotor) of 20 at 9 m/s and 8.8 and 13.8 at 15 m/s; building for 15 m/s costs 34% (multirotor) and 9% (fixed wing) in mass. Crediting the multirotor with translational lift makes its mass fall to 1.71 kg at 10 m/s, an optimistic artefact of a steady known wind. Stylised: assumed coefficients, no gusts, crosswind or launch limits, nothing flown.

How much heavier must an observation UAV be if the wind is uncertain, and is designing to a wind quantile cheaper than a bigger battery reserve?

research/osrobot-uavgust · 8 tests

How much heavier must an observation UAV be if the wind is uncertain? Designing to the 95th-percentile of wind ~ N(8, 3) m/s costs the fixed wing +3.7% and the multirotor +11.7% mass, and a bigger battery reserve buys about the same reliability for about the same mass; stylised, one steady wind per mission.

Abstract

A stdlib extension of osrobot-uavwind draws one along-track wind per mission from a normal distribution (exact discretisation, no sampling noise) and sizes the airframe for a quantile of it. For a 3 km, 20 min, 0.5 kg mission with wind ~ N(8, 3) m/s, designing to the 95th-percentile wind (12.9 m/s) costs the fixed wing +3.7% mass (1.333 against 1.286 kg) and the multirotor +11.7% (2.518 against 2.254 kg). The standard 20% reserve, if it may be spent, already lifts a mean-wind design to 0.95 (fixed wing) and 0.99 (multirotor) success, and a larger reserve and a quantile design cost within a few percent of each other. At a 1 min stay the 95th-percentile design flips the lightest airframe from multirotor to fixed wing. Stylised: one steady wind per mission, assumed constants, no turbulence, no launch limit, and no flight.

If an observation UAV only launches when the forecast wind is below a limit, how much does forecast error bring failures back, and what does it cost in scrubbed missions?

research/osrobot-uavnogo · 5 tests

If an observation UAV launches only when the forecast wind is under a limit, how much does forecast error bring failures back? A perfect forecast turns every failure into a scrub (fixed wing, wind ~ N(5, 4) m/s: 4.6% scrubbed at the 95th-percentile limit); a 2 m/s error gives 6.8% failures at the median limit but 1.1% at the 95th. Stylised, one steady wind per mission.

Abstract

A stdlib extension of osrobot-uavgust sizes the airframe for a launch limit wind and launches only if the forecast of the wind is at most that limit, with a normal forecast error (exact discretisation, no sampling noise). With a perfect forecast every failure becomes a scrub (fixed wing, wind ~ N(5, 4) m/s: 48% scrubbed at the median limit, 4.6% at the 95th percentile, 1.274 to 1.313 kg). A 2 m/s forecast error brings the failure rate to 6.8% at the median limit but only 1.1% at the 95th-percentile limit. Stylised: one steady wind per mission, assumed constants, normal forecast error, no cost put on a scrub.

If a scrubbed observation-UAV launch can be retried later the same day, how much does persistent wind limit what retrying buys, and does retrying bring failures back?

research/osrobot-uavretry · 7 tests

If a scrubbed observation-UAV launch can be retried later the same day, how much does persistent wind limit the gain, and do retries bring failures back? Fixed wing, median-wind limit, perfect forecast: eight slots complete 99.7% of days with independent wind but 71.5% with persistence 0.95; with a 2 m/s forecast error eight slots raise failures from 6.8% to 13.6% of days (23.7% at persistence 0.95). Stylised.

Abstract

A stdlib extension of osrobot-uavnogo lets the crew look again at the forecast in up to 8 slots a day, with wind following a Markov chain of persistence ρ (exact probabilities, no sampling noise). Fixed wing, wind ~ |N(5, 4)| m/s, limit at the median wind, perfect forecast: one slot completes 52.0% of days, and eight slots complete 99.7% with independent wind but 97.0%, 87.2% and 71.5% at ρ = 0.5, 0.8 and 0.95. With a 2 m/s forecast error each retry is another draw of the error, so eight slots raise the failure share from 6.8% to 13.6% of days (23.7% at ρ = 0.95). Stylised: assumed constants, one steady wind per slot, forecast errors independent between slots, no abort, no value on a mission. Nothing was flown.

What is a UAV launch wind limit worth in money, and should the launch threshold sit below the design limit when the forecast is wrong?

research/osrobot-uavvalue · 4 tests

What is a UAV launch wind limit worth in money, and should the launch threshold sit below the design limit when the forecast is wrong? With an assumed expensive airframe and a 2 m/s forecast error the best threshold sits 1 to 6 m/s below the design limit, worth up to $284 per attempt, with failures at or below 0.1%; with a cheap airframe the best design covers nearly every wind. Stylised, assumed prices.

Abstract

A stdlib extension of osrobot-uavnogo puts a money value on a completed observation, the loss of an aircraft on a failure and a scrubbed day, then searches the design wind limit L and the launch threshold T together (exact probabilities, no sampling noise). With a cheap airframe ($1,500/kg, assumed) the best design is for nearly every wind (L at 14 m/s or more in 17 of 18 cases, 11 on the grid edge) and the rule barely matters. With an expensive one ($30,000/kg, assumed) the optimum is interior, and with a 2 m/s forecast error the best T sits 1 to 6 m/s below L in all 9 cases, worth up to $284 per attempt over tying T to L, with failures at or below 0.1%. Money figures are assumptions, many optima sit on the grid edge, and a failure is assumed to lose the whole airframe. Stylised, no aircraft designed or flown.

When does one shared robot beat a dedicated robot per job in a pick-and-place shop?

research/osrobot-reconfig · 8 tests

When does one shared robot beat a dedicated robot per job in a pick-and-place shop? Sharing wins while the total load fits one machine and changeover is short (4 jobs, trays within 0.4 m: shared cheapest at demand 0.2 per job, dedicated from 0.3); the shared arm often cannot hold tight tolerances. Stylised, assumed costs.

Abstract

A stdlib extension of osrobot-specialise equips a shop of 2 to 8 pick-and-place trays three ways: a robot per job, one shared gantry spanning all trays, or one shared arm, on capital cost over 200 random job mixes per row. Sharing wins only while the total load fits one machine (4 jobs at scatter 0.4 m: shared cheapest in every mix at demand 0.2 of a gantry per job, a robot per job from 0.3) and only while changeover is short (a robot per job in 99% of mixes at 1,800 s). Trays spread beyond about 0.6 m erode the saving. The shared arm can hold 1 to 2 mm in only 13% of default mixes, but is cheapest in about half of mixes with loose (5 and 10 mm) tolerances. Stylised: every cost, speed, demand and changeover figure is assumed.

What does a shared pick-and-place robot cost once it has to stay up, and do spares change when sharing beats a robot per job?

research/osrobot-uptime · 8 tests

What does a shared pick-and-place robot cost once it must stay up? Spares priced per job or for every job at once: the shared fleet costs the same fraction of a robot per job when its load fits one machine (0.68 at 4 jobs), and falls to 0.34 to 0.51 when all jobs must run at once. Stylised, assumed availabilities.

Abstract

A stdlib extension of osrobot-reconfig adds independent unit failures (availability A) and buys the fewest spares that meet a service target, either per job or for every job at once (200 random job mixes per row, seed 7). While the shared robot's load fits one machine, spares change nothing in the per-job comparison: the shared fleet costs 0.68 of a robot per job at 4 jobs, A = 0.95, target 0.99, with or without spares. Asking every job to be up at once favours sharing (0.34 to 0.51 at 4 jobs, A 0.90 to 0.99; 0.41 at 8 jobs, A = 0.95), because a shared robot fails every job together while four independent jobs each need a spare. At demand 0.3 per job spares close the gap load opened (1.34 bare, 1.01 with spares). Limits: “served in full” is all or nothing, failures are independent with unlimited repair crews, and one assumed availability covers every machine. Stylised; no price or MTBF is sourced.

When a spoon-feeding arm meets a moving mouth, what sets the force on the lips: the sensor, the approach speed or a force cap?

research/osrobot-assist · 6 tests

What sets the force on the lips when a spoon-feeding arm meets a swaying mouth: the sensor, the approach speed or a force cap? Peak force tracks approach speed (1.0 N at 0.05 m/s, 4.0 N at 0.20), a camera cuts how often the lips are touched (0.40 against 0.72 of trials) but not how hard, and latency erodes that gain. Stylised point-mass model, assumed constants.

Abstract

A stdlib planar point-mass twin of a feeding arm and a swaying mouth, with 200 paired trials per row. With a 0.05 s forward lead the push on a blocked tip is 20 N·s/m × speed, so peak lip force tracks approach speed (1.0 N at 0.05 m/s, 4.0 N at 0.20). A camera lowers the share of trials that touch the lips (0.40 against 0.72 at 30 mm sway) without lowering the peak, and its advantage fades with latency (touch share 0.07 at no delay, 0.71 at 0.4 s). A 1 N force cap trims the worst peak but not the share of comfortable feeds. Stylised: all constants are assumed, the peak-force result is largely an identity of the chosen controller, and no human model or Assistive Gym was used.

Does a spoon-feeding arm need more than two joints? Tip mass, lip force and servo cost of 2 to 6 joint arms

research/osrobot-dof · 7 tests

Does a spoon-feeding arm need more than two joints? From 2 to 6 joints the tip mass moves only 0.63 to 0.50 kg and lip force not clearly at all, while servo cost triples (stylised; extends osrobot-assist).

Abstract

A stdlib planar model gives 2 to 6 joint feeding arms an operational-space tip mass (servo, link and spoon masses plus each servo's rotor inertia reflected through a 345:1 gearbox) and feeds it to osrobot-assist's lip-contact trials (200 paired trials per row, 30 mm sway, camera arm). The tip mass falls only from 0.63 to 0.50 kg from 2 to 6 joints; the worst peak lip force is identical (2.0 N at 0.10 m/s, 4.0 N at 0.20) and the safe share at 0.20 m/s is 0.60 to 0.65, within sampling error. Servo cost alone triples, from $52–98 to $156–295 (two retailers' listings, 2026-10-19). Most of the mass is the geared-up rotor. Stylised: planar, one pose, position-only task, assumed constants, so the real reasons for more joints (spoon orientation, 3-D motion) are not tested.

Does the stylised feeding-arm lip-force result survive real contact physics? osrobot-assist re-run in MuJoCo

research/osrobot-mjfeed · 6 tests

Does the stylised feeding-arm lip-force result survive MuJoCo contact physics? The steady push matches KP × LEAD × speed, but the impact peak is 1.84 × higher and the share fed at most 2 N falls from 1.00 to 0.45 at 0.10 m/s (stylised; extends osrobot-assist).

Abstract

The osrobot-assist spoon-feeding trial rebuilt in MuJoCo (a sphere on two slide joints, two box lips that follow the mouth's sway, MuJoCo soft contact), with the same controller and 200 paired seeds per row. With the arm blocked, the steady contact force equals the controller identity KP×LEAD×speed (2.00 N at 0.10 m/s) but the impact peak is 1.84× higher at every speed (3.68 N), and falls with more contact damping. Feeding time agrees within 4 % in all but one row. Stylised and preliminary: rigid lips that cannot yield, assumed contact damping, no human model.

Does a better tour rescue scout-then-treat weeding? 2-opt cuts the tour from 539 m to 468 m but a field still takes 56.1 min against 51.7 min treating inline; scouting loses at every weed density tried (stylised, simulation only).

Abstract

A stdlib follow-up to osrobot-field that swaps nearest neighbour for 2-opt as the second stage of scout-then-treat. 2-opt cuts the straight-line tour over about 232 detections from 539 m to 468 m (random order: 5,403 m), but a field still takes 56.1 min against 51.7 min treating inline (nearest neighbour: 57.0), and scouting is slower at every weed density from 0.05 to 1.2 per m². Stylised: assumed constants, a mud-blind planner, 30 fields per row; no robot was built.

For a weeding robot with a cheap camera, is it better to treat weeds as it drives or to scout first and come back, and what does a weak sensor cost?

research/osrobot-field · 7 tests

For a weeding robot with a cheap camera, is it better to treat weeds as it drives or to scout first? Scouting then touring killed the same share (0.399 against 0.401 at 1 m/s) and was slower in all 23 paired rows; lane overlap repays a weak sensor (0.40 to 0.86 killed) at 3.5 times the time. Stylised, constants assumed.

Abstract

A stdlib simulation of a lane-following weeding robot on a 60×30 m field with clustered weeds, a motion-blurred noisy detector and muddy squares. Scouting then touring the targets kills the same share of weeds as treating inline (0.399 against 0.401 at 1 m/s) and is slower in all 23 paired rows, by 2.8 to 7.8 min. Lane overlap repays a weak sensor at a steep price: 0 to 0.75 overlap raises the kill share from 0.40 to 0.86 and the time from 52 to 178 min; no grid configuration reaches a 0.9 kill share at a 0.5 hit rate. Stylised: every constant is assumed, the speed results move with the blur assumption, and the planner comparison is a statement about this cost model.

Can a stdlib static linter catch the lab-protocol faults the Opentrons simulator accepts?

research/osrobot-lint · 13 tests

Can a stdlib static linter catch the lab-protocol faults the Opentrons simulator accepts? It flags 362 of the 522 seeded faults to the simulator's 270, adding reused tips, tips left on, sub-minimum volumes and carry-over; stylised, partly circular.

Abstract

A stdlib linter for Opentrons Python API scripts follows tip state, used tip wells, declared well volumes and the liquid in the tip without running the script. On the 522 seeded faults of osrobot-lab it flags 362, against 270 raised by the real simulator and 366 flagged by the twin, adding reused tips, tips left on, sub-minimum volumes and carry-over; it cannot see the travel-height collisions. Agreement with the twin is partly circular (same author, same fault set), and every limit is an assumed value. Stylised.

How fast can a purpose-built robot fill a 96-well plate, and does the robot or the number of channels matter more?

research/osrobot-plate · 7 tests

How fast can a purpose-built robot fill a 96-well plate, and does the robot or the number of channels matter more? Eight channels cut the time by 86 to 88 % (9.5 s against 69.7 s for a gantry at 10 µL) while gantry against arm moves it 4 to 14 %, since about 76 % is fixed pipetting actions (stylised; every speed, price and accuracy figure assumed).

Abstract

A stdlib model times a 96-well plate fill on an XY gantry and a two-link arm with 1 or 8 channels. At 10 µL per well one channel takes 69.7 s (gantry) or 66.7 s (arm); eight channels take 9.5 and 9.0 s. Channel count cuts the time by 86–88%; robot type moves it by 4–14%, because 76–79% of the time is fixed aspirate and dispense actions, so well order saves only about 2%. Which robot is faster flips with the assumed speeds; the 8-channel gantry is cheapest per plate-hour by a small margin. With assumed joint noise the arm misses the 1.5 mm well radius often enough to hit all 96 wells with probability 0.005. Stylised: every speed, price and accuracy figure is assumed.

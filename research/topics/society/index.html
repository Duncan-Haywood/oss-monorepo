Research topic

Robot societies among people

Simulated societies of AI-driven robots living among simulated people: the conventions that emerge, who sets them, what they cost people, and watchable episodes made from seeded runs.

10 projects, each with its own page, code and tests. See them on the full research index.

How many robots does it take to change how a crowd walks?

research/society-robotnorms · 14 tests

How many robots does it take to change which side a crowd walks on? Robots that hold a programmed side tip a crowd once they are 16–32% of it, more when people remember more past partners (simulation matches mean field within 0.02); just short of that they make people collide up to 14× as often as adaptive robots, and placement matters more than numbers when people meet only neighbours. Stylised.

Abstract

Robots as a committed minority in a passing-side convention. Simulated people take the side most of their last M partners used; robots hold a programmed side. The tipping share grows with memory: 16%, 22%, 28% and 32% of the crowd for M = 5, 9, 17 and 25, within 0.02 of a mean-field prediction. Just below it, robots that hold their side make people collide up to 14× as often as robots that adapt (0.27 vs 0.020 conflicts per meeting). When people meet only neighbours, evenly spread robots tip everyone from 12–14%, while one block of robots converts under 10% of people even at half the crowd. Stylised: one binary choice per meeting, no geometry.

Do robots that keep to one side make people walk on it, or jam the corridor?

research/society-plaza · 13 tests

Do robots that keep to one side make people walk on it, or jam the corridor? In a social-force corridor, robots that adapt change nothing, but robots that hold the left cut people's speed from 0.91 to 0.39 of desired by 30% of the crowd and halve lane separation, while people move to their side only gradually (no sharp tipping, unlike the 1D model). Stylised.

Abstract

The 1D society-robotnorms passing-side model rebuilt as a social-force corridor (60 simulated walkers, 30 m × 6 m). Robots that adapt to people change nothing. Robots that hold the left slow people from 0.91 to 0.81 of desired speed at 5% of the crowd and to 0.39 at 30%, halve the lane separation (2.75 m to 1.43 m), and move people to their side only gradually (at most 54% of people at memory 9), with no sharp tipping in 300 s. Includes a seeded browser replay. Stylised: 8 seeds, one parameter set, simulated walkers only.

Can a robot fleet's reputation survive one bad incident?

research/society-trust · 10 tests

Can a robot fleet's reputation survive one bad incident? In a model where trust spreads by experience and gossip, a dent always recovers because people keep meeting robots; reputation is lost only through avoidance (at a 0.30 avoid-below threshold, 20/20, 18/20 and 0/20 crowds recover under neutral, 2× and 4× negativity in gossip), and robots that explain failures recovered every time. Stylised.

Abstract

Simulated people hold trust in a robot fleet, updated by their own encounters and by gossip in which bad news may travel further than good. Trust that is only dented recovers in every case. What sinks a reputation is avoidance: if people stop using robots below a trust threshold of 0.30, an incident at error rate 0.8 is recovered in 20/20 crowds with neutral gossip, 18/20 when bad news counts 2×, and 0/20 at 4× (final mean trust 0.947, 0.871, 0.282). Robots that explain failures (each costing 0.3× the trust) recovered in every setting. Stylised: 100 simulated people, one shared reputation, a hard avoidance threshold, no fitted parameters.

What does it cost robots to let people go first at a shared lift or narrow pavement?

research/society-commons · 11 tests

What does it cost robots to let people go first at a shared lift or narrow pavement? At load 0.9, deference cuts people's wait 6.5× (4.60 to 0.71) and raises robots' by 2.6 units; total waiting cost is conserved, so it pays only if a person's wait costs more than a robot's, and a lone defector waits far less (2.08 vs 7.65). Queueing model within 2.7% of closed forms.

Abstract

People and robots share a one-at-a-time crossing. At load 0.9, robots that always let waiting people go first cut people’s mean wait from 4.60 to 0.71 time units and raise the robots’ from 4.61 to 7.21; the total waiting cost is the same for every share of deferential robots (4.14–4.15), so deference moves waiting rather than removing it, and pays exactly when a person’s wait costs more than a robot’s. A lone selfish robot waits 2.08 against 7.65 for deferential ones (the dilemma). Under overload (load 1.2) half the robots deferring costs them 10.5% of their crossings and gives people 20% more. Simulation within 2.7% of M/G/1 closed forms. Stylised: one server, Poisson arrivals, fixed crossing times.

Do robot agents keep the norms they state?

research/society-llmtown · 8 tests

Do robot agents keep the norms they state? A pluggable town harness with a deterministic stub (no language model run): a robot stating "always yield" keeps it in 1 − 0.4×pressure of meetings (1.00 to 0.60), and people's cost per meeting rises from 0.20 to 1.08. Stylised.

Abstract

A harness for a small town of robot agents that state a door norm each day and then act among scripted people, scoring how often action matches statement and what it costs people. Any agent with state_norm and act plugs in; a deterministic stub is tested, and an optional model adapter is provided but was not run. With the stub, a robot stating ‘always yield’ keeps it in 1 − 0.4×pressure of meetings (1.00, 0.80, 0.60 at pressure 0, 0.5, 1; 20 seeds) and people’s cost per meeting rises from 0.20 to 1.08. These follow from the stub’s own rule: no language-model result is claimed. Stylised; non-adaptive scripted people.

Does the robots-in-a-corridor conclusion survive a more humanlike crowd?

research/society-twinfidelity · 6 tests

Does the robots-in-a-corridor conclusion survive a more humanlike crowd? Adaptive robots stay free and committed robots never flip the crowd in all five simulated crowds, but the size of the slowdown (loss of 0.17–0.49 of desired speed at 30% robots) and of personal-space intrusions (0.03–0.36 of agent time) depends strongly on how humanlike the people are. Stylised.

Abstract

The society-plaza corridor rerun with four crowds of simulated people: varied speeds and sideways-push weights, a 0.4 s reaction delay, 30% stubborn walkers, and all three (10 seeds per cell). Adaptive robots stay free and committed robots never flip the crowd (at most 58% prefer their side) in every crowd. The size of the slowdown (a loss of 0.17 to 0.49 of desired speed at 30% robots) and of personal-space intrusions (0.03 to 0.36 of agent time; reaction delay alone gives 0.087 with no robots) depends strongly on the crowd. Hand-chosen perturbations, not fitted to data; simulated walkers only.

How many robots does it take to change a crowd's walking side when some people never adapt?

research/society-stubborn · 9 tests

How many robots does it take to change a crowd's walking side when some people never adapt? With 0, 20, 40 and 50% stubborn walkers the mean-field tipping share (memory 9) rises 0.217, 0.240, 0.283, 0.329, simulation agrees within 0.02, and committed-robot conflict costs barely change. Stylised.

Abstract

The society-robotnorms committed-robot model with a share s of stubborn people who always walk right. The mean-field tipping share for memory 9 rises from 0.217 (s = 0) to 0.240, 0.283 and 0.329 at s = 0.2, 0.4 and 0.5, and memory 5 and 9 coincide from s = 0.4. Simulation (200 people, 12 seeds) agrees within 0.02 at every s when “tipped” means a majority of adaptive people on the robots' side. Conflict costs of committed and adaptive robots are unchanged by stubborn people. Stylised; simulated walkers only.

How many robots does it take to change a crowd's walking side when some people always walk on the robots' side?

research/society-blocs · 11 tests

How many robots does it take to change a crowd's walking side when some people always walk on the robots' side? Each 5% of always-robot-side people cuts the memory-9 mean-field tipping share (0.217) by about 0.04, 25% tip a crowd with no robots, opposing blocs raise the bar, and simulation agrees within 0.01. Stylised.

Abstract

The society-stubborn model with a share sl of people who always walk the robots' side and sr who always walk the status-quo side. For memory 9 the mean-field tipping robot share falls from 0.217 to 0.176, 0.131, 0.080 and 0.023 at sl = 0.05, 0.10, 0.15 and 0.20 (about 0.8 robots saved per ally), and a crowd with 25% allies tips with no robots. Opposing blocs raise the self-tipping share (0.219 to 0.300 as sr goes 0 to 0.3), equal blocs are a knife edge, and simulation (200 people, 12 seeds) agrees with mean field within 0.01 at seven bloc mixes. Opposing blocs alone cost 0.195 conflicts per human meeting. Stylised; simulated walkers only.

Does a stubborn block that slowly converts let a smaller robot minority tip a crowd's walking side?

research/society-converts · 8 tests

Does a stubborn block that slowly converts let a smaller robot minority tip a crowd's walking side? With 40% stubborn walkers, 24% robots never tip (0 of 12) but tip all 12 at 0.1% conversion per round (median round 721).

Abstract

The society-stubborn model with 40% stubborn walkers who each convert to an ordinary memory-rule walker with probability c per round. With the block fixed, 24% committed robots never tip the crowd (0 of 12) and 28% tip 5 of 12; with c = 0.001 both tip all 12 (median round 721 and 185), and at c = 0.1 at round 59 and 31. At 20% or fewer robots no crowd tipped at any c. Conversion mostly buys speed and moves the threshold towards the no-stubborn value. Stylised: simulated walkers, spontaneous conversion, 200 people, 12 seeds; the mean-field clock is only a rank-order check.

Can a simulation run be turned into a watchable, honest episode?

research/society-show · 10 tests

Can a simulation run be turned into a watchable, honest episode? Five seeded, labelled-fiction episodes of a robot town built on society-trust, with turning points found by code and a narration read from the record: even-handed gossip recovers, 4× negativity locks the town out, explained failures recover, and 2× ends both ways by seed. Stylised.

Abstract

Five seeded, clearly labelled fictional episodes of a robot town, built on society-trust: 48 invented townspeople, a trust-coloured grid, turning points found by code from the recorded data and a narration whose numbers all come from that record. Even-handed gossip recovers 46 rounds after the fix; gossip that weighs bad news 4× locks the town out (48 of 48 avoid working robots at round 399); robots that explain failures recover in 29 rounds with nobody avoiding; and at 2× the same settings recover on seed 0 and lock out on seed 1. Episodes are single illustrative runs, not statistics; stylised model, no viewer study.

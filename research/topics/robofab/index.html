Robot manufacturing loops

Can general-purpose robot arms make and assemble things, planned and checked in simulation first? Assembly sequences, insertion and cells, with every claim kept to the simulator.

Which assembly orders survive a better checker?

research/robofab-sequence · 10 tests

Assembly-order planning for a printed gearbox: exact subset DP, collision-only admits 10.6% of orders but support checking leaves 0.077%, the collision-only optimum is infeasible, greedy disassembly needs 4 direction changes vs 1 optimal. Stylised.

Abstract

Exact subset dynamic programming over assembly orders for a twelve-part printed gearbox, checked at three fidelity levels. Collision-only admits 4,227,168 of 39,916,800 orders (10.6%); adding quasi-static support leaves 30,760 (0.077%), and the collision-only optimum is infeasible once support is checked. Greedy disassembly needs 4 insertion-direction changes against an optimum of 1; one order written by the session agent (a single attempt, not a benchmark) passes with 2. Stylised axis-aligned boxes, no physics engine.

Does a quasi-static support rule agree with a physics engine?

research/robofab-settle · 7 tests

MuJoCo settle tests of all 2,048 partial gearbox assemblies vs the quasi-static support rule: 95.6% agreement, 81 mutual-support false accepts and 10 bore-capture false rejects, valid-order count unchanged at 30,760. Stylised.

Abstract

Validates the support rule of robofab-sequence against MuJoCo settle tests on all 2,048 partial assemblies of the printed gearbox. The rule agrees on 1,957 (95.6%): 81 states it accepts topple because a part can count as resting on a part that rests on it, and 10 it rejects hold because a gear bore captures the shaft. An acyclic-support variant reaches 99.5% (fitted on the same states). The count of valid orders is unchanged at 30,760 for three friction values, so this product's conclusions survive. Stylised boxes, static release, one product.

Cross-entropy-tuned five-parameter peg-in-hole search policies in MuJoCo, trained on three simulator fidelity levels or with domain randomisation and tested on all three plus a held-out parameter set. How much of the gap between “the twin passes” and “it fails for real” does each twin fidelity close?

research/robofab-program · 10 tests

Session-written pick-and-place programs checked in a kinematic SCARA twin at three fidelities against a held-out reference: of 69 reference-failing injected-bug mutants the levels reject 38, 46 and 60 with no false alarms; misses are unmodelled effects (stylised).

Abstract

A kinematic SCARA pick-and-place twin at three checking levels (geometry; plus joint, payload and deadline limits; plus stochastic grasp error, grip build-up and slip) against a held-out reference twin. Of 162 injected-bug mutants of three programs, 69 fail the reference; the levels reject 38, 46 and 60 of them, with no false alarms. The misses are effects the checker does not model, so the rates reflect how the reference was built. Four programs written by the building session: three failed the reference as drafted, and one failure (payload at long reach) was invisible to all three levels. Stylised, kinematic only, no language-model API called.

Which resource limits a small robot-printer-inspection cell, and does the busiest one tell you what to upgrade?

research/robofab-cell · 9 tests

Discrete-event twin of an arm, printers and an inspection station: printers have the highest utilisation at every size, yet at 6 printers an extra arm saves 10.9 min vs 2.3 for an extra inspection station; dispatch rules trade makespan for flow time; a mean-value twin is optimistic. Stylised, illustrative parameters.

Abstract

A discrete-event twin of one arm, 1–8 printers and one inspection station (30 parts, 200 replications, illustrative times). Printers have the highest utilisation at every size, yet at 6 printers an extra arm saves 10.9 min of makespan against 2.3 for an extra inspection station, because a printer is held while it waits for the arm. Dispatch rules trade makespan for mean flow time as textbook scheduling predicts, and a mean-value twin underestimates the stochastic makespan by 1–133 min. Stylised; parameters are invented, not measured.

Abstract

A hand-written discrete-event twin of one arm that loads and unloads P 3D printers, plus an inspection station with reprints, against a utilisation bound. The bound names the bottleneck (printers up to 10 printers, the arm from 12, about 12.7 good parts/h) and the simulation reaches it within 1–2% far from the switch but sits 10–11% below it at 10–12 printers. Is a print-failure camera worth it in a robot-arm 3D-printer cell?

research/robofab-cell-monitor · 8 tests

Is a print-failure camera worth it in a robot-arm printer cell? Blind printing against abort-on-failure: +6 to 10% good parts per hour at 2 to 10 printers, none once the arm limits the cell; break-even false-stop rate. Stylised.

Abstract

Adds mid-print failures (0.3 per printing hour) to the arm, printers and inspection cell and compares blind printing with a camera that aborts a failing print after a delay, including false stops, against an analytic bound and a 40-seed discrete-event simulation. With a 10 min delay the camera adds 6–10% good parts per hour at 2–10 printers, 4.5% at 12 and 0.8% at 16 where the arm limits the cell; the gain falls with detection delay (11% for an instant camera, about 0.5% at 80 min), vanishes between 0.1 and 0.2 false aborts per printing hour, and grows with the failure rate (28% at 1.0 per hour). Stylised: memoryless failures, a fixed detection delay, no real detector or measured farm.

Where should a print-failure detector's alarm threshold go in a robot-arm 3D-printer cell?

research/robofab-cell-detector · 9 tests

Where should a print-failure detector's alarm threshold go in a robot-arm printer cell? A look every 2 min makes a z = 2 threshold 19 to 23% worse than blind printing; the best z is 3 to 3.5, worth +6% (d' = 2) to +11% (d' = 4). Stylised.

Abstract

Replaces the monitor project's fixed detection delay with a detector that looks at a print every few minutes and has a Gaussian score (separation d′) and an alarm threshold z, and asks which z maximises good parts per hour, with an exact analytic bound and a 40-seed discrete-event simulation (6 printers, 0.3 failures per printing hour, looks every 2 min). A threshold that sounds strict (z = 2, 2.3% false alarms per look) makes the cell 19–23% worse than blind printing for every detector quality, because a 60 min print is looked at 30 times; the best z is 3–3.5, and the gain over blind printing is +5.8% at d′ = 2, +9.7% at 3 and +11.3% at 4, and within noise below d′ of about 1. Stylised: looks are independent, which real consecutive frames are not.

Where does a flow-equivalent-server decomposition of a robot-printer cell go wrong as the inspector speeds up or slows down?

research/robofab-cell-fescsweep · 4 tests

Where does the flow-equivalent-server decomposition of a robot-printer cell err as the inspector speed varies? Error is 0 for fast and slow inspectors but peaks at −2% to −5% just below the knee where inspector and subsystem rates match, ten times the earlier 0.3% mean. Stylised.

Abstract

A follow-up to the decomposition project, which reported a 0.32% mean error at one inspection time. Sweeping the inspection time from 0.1 to 100 min in 10 stylised cells (2–6 printers, Erlang-4 print time, illustrative times) against the exact Markov chain, the decomposition’s error is 0.000% for a fast inspector and at most 0.52% for a very slow one, but peaks at −2.2% to −5.0% (mean −3.2%) just below the knee t* = 1/H(N), where the inspector alone matches the subsystem’s rate (0.81–0.99 t*, grid-limited). It is never positive, and smaller cells err more. The explanation (ignored correlation between inspection queue and subsystem state) is untested. Stylised: one job class, exponential load, unload and inspect times.

Abstract

A follow-up to the exact Markov-chain project: Norton’s theorem applied to the same stylised cell (one arm, 2–6 printers held through load and unload, one inspector; Erlang print time; illustrative times). Abstract

An exact Markov-chain solution of a stylised cell (one arm, 2–6 printers held through load and unload, one inspector; Erlang print time, cv 0.33–1; illustrative times) used as a noise-free reference. It matches a simulation in 10 settings (largest |z| 1.73). Abstract

A follow-up to the held-printer project: the printer’s demand is increased by the arm’s queueing delay, found as a fixed point from the arm’s own queue with no fitted parameter. How much clearance does a printed two-pin cover need to assemble, and how wasteful is worst-case stacking? At position sd 0.10 mm and size sd 0.05 mm, 99% yield needs 0.62 mm diametral clearance and a ±3σ worst-case stack asks for 2.0 mm (stylised, assumed process spreads).

Abstract

Monte Carlo of a printed base with two pins and a cover with two holes, each centre with a Gaussian position error and each diameter a Gaussian size error, translation-only assembly (exact two-disk test). With exact sizes, yield is 1 − exp(−c2/(8σp2)) (tested against simulation).

How much lumber, labour and heat does advanced framing save in a house's exterior walls, and does it still carry the roof?

research/house-framing · 12 tests

How much does advanced (OVE) framing save in a house's exterior walls? Stylised 28 × 40 ft house: 42% less wall lumber and 27% fewer pieces than conventional 2x6, whole-wall R 16.2 to 18.6, studs at 39% of capacity under 30 psf snow; the lumber is worth only $450–900 a house, so labour carries the case. Not a code check. First project of track 12.

Abstract

An open takeoff of a stylised 28 × 40 ft one-storey house: every stud, plate, header, cripple and block under conventional and advanced (OVE) framing, with the NDS stud and header checks and a parallel-path whole-wall R-value. Advanced 2x6 framing at 24 in used 42% less exterior-wall lumber than conventional 2x6 and 27% fewer pieces, and raised the whole-wall R-value from 16.2 to 18.6. It still used 16% less lumber than conventional 2x4. At 30 psf of roof snow its studs work at 39% of capacity. The biggest single savings were 24 in spacing, the single top plate and header hangers. At assumed lumber prices the wall saving is only $450 to $900 a house, so the case for advanced framing rests on labour, which the model counts as pieces but does not price. Stylised: one house, exterior walls only, no lateral design, no labour rates, and not a code check or a design anyone should build from without a licensed review.

How far can a 2x joist, rafter, header or beam span, and which check limits it?

Open NDS sizing tool, no table copied: 2x10 SPF floor joists at 16 in span 15 ft 3 in, bending governs most floors and snow rafters, deflection most ceilings; deepest joist at 24 in uses 20–50% less lumber (vibration unchecked). Not validated against published tables. Milestone 2 of track 12.

Abstract

An open sizing tool for simple-span sawn-lumber members, derived from beam formulas and NDS base design values with no span table copied: bending, shear, deflection and bearing checks, a command line, and the governing check named for every result. For SPF No. 2 floor joists at 16 in the longest clear spans are 9 ft 2 in (2x6), 12 ft 1 in (2x8), 15 ft 3 in (2x10) and 17 ft 8 in (2x12). Bending governs 22 of 32 floor cells and live-load deflection the other 10; total-load deflection governs 31 of 32 ceiling cells; bending governs snow-loaded rafters in 29 of 32. The lightest passing floor joist is the deepest at 24 in, which uses 20–50% less lumber than 2x10 at 16 in, but floor vibration is not checked. A cross-check with house-framing found its header bearing area too generous for a single jack under a 2-ply header. Not validated against published span tables, no engineered lumber, no continuous spans, and not a design anyone should build from without a licensed review.

Does a floor-vibration frequency limit erase the lumber saved by deep, widely spaced joists?

research/house-vibration · 6 tests

Does a floor-vibration frequency limit erase the lumber saved by deep, widely spaced joists? Beam-theory first-mode frequency on the house-spans checks: limits up to 10 Hz change nothing, 12 Hz shortens a 2x10 at 16 in from 15 ft 3 in to 13 ft 11 in, 15 Hz moves the least-lumber 12 ft joist from 2x10 to 2x12. Joist alone, limit is a policy knob. Stylised. Track 12.

Abstract

A first-mode natural-frequency check (f1 = (π/2L2)√(EIg/w), derived and tested against the static-deflection form) added to the NDS strength and deflection checks of house-spans, for SPF No. 2 floor joists under 10 + 40 psf. The joist alone is modelled, with no subfloor composite action. Any limit up to 10 Hz changes nothing: the longest strength-passing 2x10 at 16 in (15 ft 3 in) already vibrates at 10.1 Hz. At 12 Hz it shortens to 13 ft 11 in, and at 15 Hz the least-lumber joist at a 12 ft span moves from 2x10 at 24 in (0.83 board feet per sq ft) to 2x12 at 24 in (1.00), while at 14 ft the 2x12 at 24 in no longer passes and 2x12 at 16 in (1.50) is needed. The limit in Hz is a policy choice swept here, not a code-book criterion, and the model is conservative by ignoring the subfloor and optimistic by ignoring live-load mass. Stylised.

How much of a floor-vibration penalty does the subfloor's T-beam action recover?

research/house-composite · 6 tests

How much of a floor-vibration penalty does the subfloor's T-beam action recover? Gamma-method partial-composite stiffness on house-vibration: at a 15 Hz limit a 2x10 at 16 in grows from 12 ft 6 in to 13 ft 2 in–14 ft 6 in, depending on two assumed inputs (connection efficiency and panel modulus). Stylised.

Abstract

The subfloor modelled as a partial flange of a T-beam by the gamma method (rederived from the parallel-axis theorem and tested against an independent transformed-section calculation), added to the frequency check of house-vibration for a 2x10 SPF No. 2 joist at 16 in. The result depends on two assumed, unsourced inputs, the connection efficiency γ and the panel modulus. At a panel modulus of 0.5 million psi the 15 ft first-mode frequency rises from 10.5 Hz to 12.6 Hz at γ 0.5 and 14.0 Hz at γ 1, and under a 15 Hz limit the span grows from 12 ft 6 in to 13 ft 2 in–14 ft 6 in. Strength checks stay joist-alone; live-load mass is left out, so gains are optimistic. Stylised, not validated against a measured floor, not a design.

Does a joist lapped over a middle support span farther than a simple-span joist?

research/house-continuous · 11 tests

Does a joist lapped over a middle support span farther than a simple span? FE beam with pattern loading and NDS checks: two-span peak moment equals a simple span's, so bending-governed floors and snow rafters gain nothing; deflection-governed ceiling joists gain up to 18.5%; 1.5 in bearing on the middle support cuts a 2x10 floor joist to 11 ft 4 in. Not validated against published tables. Follow-up to house-spans.

Abstract

A pure-Python finite-element beam with pattern loading and the NDS bending, shear, bearing and deflection checks of house-spans, for two- and three-span continuous sawn-lumber joists (SPF No. 2). Under full uniform load a two-span joist's peak moment, wL2/8 at the middle support, equals a simple span's, so members whose simple span is governed by bending (most floor joists, all snow rafters) gain nothing from two spans (0.0% in 16 floor cells; three spans add about 5%), while deflection-governed ceiling joists gain 0.6% to 18.5%. With only 1.5 in of bearing on the middle support, the 1.25wL reaction limits a 2x10 floor joist at 16 in to 11 ft 4 in, against 15 ft 3 in with 3.5 in. Stylised: equal spans, no vibration, not compared with published span tables. Not a structural design.

A 2x10 floor can have its beam 3.5 ft off the middle of a 28 ft house. Stylised; not a design.

Abstract

Two-span sawn-lumber joists (SPF No. 2) with unequal spans, reusing the finite-element beam of house-continuous and the NDS checks of house-spans with pattern loading and per-span deflection. For a 2x10 floor joist at 16 in, moving the beam off-centre costs 0.4% of total clear width at a short/long span ratio of 0.9, 4.3% at 0.7 and 13.3% at 0.5, because the short span relieves the long one (the long span grows from 15 ft 3 in to 17 ft 7 in); a 2x8 ceiling joist, which deflection governs, loses 5.1% at 0.9 and 24.1% at 0.5. A 28 ft wide house tolerates a beam 3.5 ft off the middle. Stylised. Not validated against published span tables, two spans only, no vibration, and not a design anyone should build from without a licensed review. The short span's end lifts off its support whenever live load is on the long span only (−103 lb per joist at a 10 ft short span, −271 lb at 6 ft; 2x10 floor).

Can a house plan be data, and which plan needs the least framing lumber per square foot?

Can a house plan be data, and which plan needs the least lumber per sq ft? Parametric one-storey plan to framing takeoff, bill of materials, room-rule checks and IFC4; best plans use 4.8 to 5.2 bf/sq ft at 640 to 1,024 sq ft with none feasible by stick framing at 1,400; local search hits the exhaustive optimum in 36 of 40 runs where random search hits none. Stylised, not a design.

Abstract

A single-storey house plan as a few parameters: rooms, walls and openings, a framing takeoff with members sized by the open NDS tools of house-spans and house-framing, a bill of materials, IRC-style room-size, light and egress checks, and an IFC4 export through IfcOpenShell. A search over 182,520 plans finds the least lumber per square foot: with two or more bedrooms the best plans use 4.8–5.2 board feet per sq ft at 640–1,024 sq ft and 5.9 at 1,216, and no stick-framed plan in the grid reaches 1,400 sq ft because joists and rafters run out of 2x12. Only 1.3% of the grid passes every check at 1,000–1,150 sq ft. At equal budgets a (1+1) local search found the exact optimum in 36 of 40 runs at 2,000 evaluations and random search in none. Stylised: one rectangular layout, stick framing only, board feet rather than prices, room rules paraphrased from memory, and not a design anyone should build from without a licensed review.

Where does a new home's price go, and which levers move it most?

research/house-cost · 8 tests

Where does a new home's price go, and which levers move it most? Share-anchored model (NAHB 2024): size moves the price most (87.8 to 112.0 for 1,600 to 2,400 sq ft), then storeys, finish level and lot; a 14% framing-lumber cut is worth 0.85% of price (2.4% with labour). Index not dollars, assumed driver splits. Milestone 4 of track 12.

Abstract

A driver-based price model anchored on NAHB's 2024 cost shares (construction 64.4% of the price, framing 16.6% of construction, lot 13.7%, profit 11.0%). Each construction stage scales with floor area, footprint or wall area, and the model reproduces every NAHB share at its 2,000 sq ft reference house. In a sweep, size moves the price most (index 87.8 to 112.0 for 1,600 to 2,400 sq ft), then one against three storeys (111.6 to 97.6), finish level, lot price and foundation type. A 14% cut in framing lumber, the gap between house-plan's default and best plans, is worth only 0.85% of the price, or 2.4% if it also cuts framing labour by 20%. Stylised: a price index, not dollars; the stage-driver splits and lever ranges are my assumptions; the Census reconciliation and a priced takeoff are not done.

Abstract

A variance-based (Sobol) global sensitivity analysis of house-cost, treating the ten lever ranges, the framing materials share and the assumed driver splits as random at once (4,096 base samples, bootstrap intervals). A degree-day net-present-cost search of 640 packages in eight climates: advanced 2x6 framing is in every optimum (13–15% lower cost than a 2x4 baseline); better windows and R-49 attics do not pay at assumed prices. Abstract

A degree-day net-present-cost search (not EnergyPlus) over 640 envelope packages of the house-framing house in eight US climates and two heating systems. The advanced 2x6 wall builds for $42 less than a conventional 2x4 and loses 40% less heat through the opaque wall, and is in every cost-optimal package: 13–15% lower 30-year cost than the baseline ($2,100–4,600). Past the wall the optimum is modest (attic R-30–38, 3 ACH50 air sealing, slab-edge insulation from about 3,000 degree-days); R-49 attics and better windows pay back in 23–190 years. At the assumed window prices the optimum picks U 0.50 windows, which a code floor of U 0.35 costs $440–1,270 to avoid; that result does not survive 4× energy prices. Stylised: every price is an assumption, climates are rounded from memory, and there is no solar gain.

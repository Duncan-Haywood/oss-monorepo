Laundry-hanging robots

A robot that takes clothes from a dryer or a loader and hangs every item, with hangers designed in a simulation feedback loop: drape models, hanger shapes, hang-or-fold decisions and the robot cell.

12 projects, each with its own page, code and tests. See them on the full research index.

What hanger shape keeps a shirt in shape, and can one hanger fit every size?

research/hanger-fit · 14 tests

What hanger shape keeps a shirt in shape, and can one hanger fit every size for a laundry-hanging robot? In a 2D cloth-drape simulation the best single fixed hanger leaves garments 2.1 cm RMS from their worn shape (5.4 cm for a child's tee), one adjustable-width hanger set per garment 0.18 cm, and a rack of four widths under 1 cm for every size; thin wire ends fold the cloth at the tip. Stylised.

Abstract

A 2D cloth-drape model (discrete elastic chain with penalty contact, solved by L-BFGS) scores how far a garment hangs from its worn shape, and CMA-ES designs the hanger. Over seven sizes from a child’s tee to XXL, the best single fixed hanger averages 2.1 cm RMS shape error (5.4 cm worst). One adjustable-width hanger set per garment averages 0.18 cm (0.22 worst), 12× better, and a rack of four widths keeps every size within 1 cm. Wire and plastic hangers fold the cloth at the tip (about 65°/cm against 14° worn); a 2–3 cm round end removes it, but a loop scored on shape alone converges on wire-thin ends. CMA-ES beats random search (0.19 vs 0.44 cm at 150 drapes). Stylised: 2D, no friction, assumed fabric constants.

Should a laundry robot hang a garment or fold it, and what can it sense to tell?

research/hanger-fold · 13 tests

Should a laundry robot hang a garment or fold it, and what can it sense to tell? A stretch-set and crease-set model on five fabric archetypes: woven shirts and linen are better hung, tees, fleece and heavy knits better folded; shoulder width, mass and stretch when lifted pick the better action for 89–91% of 600 simulated garments (always fold: 59%). The split depends mostly on the assumed crease model. Stylised, assumed fabric constants.

Abstract

Adds a stretch-set model for hanging (permanent shoulder lengthening from hanger-fit’s drape) and a crease-set model for folding to five fabric archetypes, prices both in cm of departure from the pressed shape, and learns a rule on 600 simulated garments. Woven shirts and linen are better hung, t-shirts, fleece and heavy knits better folded. Shoulder width, mass and stretch when lifted choose the better action for 89–91% of garments in 5-fold cross-validation (always fold: 59%; adding the fabric class: 94%); width alone adds nothing. The split is driven by the assumed crease model: a 6 h stack instead of 48 h moves the share better hung from 41% to 8%, while a hundredfold change in the stretch penalty moves it from 48% to 37%. Stylised: every fabric constant is assumed, the population is synthetic, no motion.

How does a laundry robot get a hanger into a garment, and what does it cost the neck?

research/hanger-insert · 20 tests

How does a laundry robot get a hanger into a garment, and what does it cost the neck? A swept-section model of a rigid, telescoping or folding hanger passing a neck or hem loop: rigid hangers need 43–61 cm of loop, a tee's neck stretches up to 33% and a buttoned shirt collar cannot take them at all (shirts go in at the hem, 10 s against 6 s); a hanger that collapses to a 10 cm half width needs 31 cm. The round end hanger-fit wants costs about 3 cm more. Stylised, assumed constants.

Abstract

A swept-section geometry model passes a hanger through a garment’s neck or hem loop: the loop must reach round the hull of the hanger’s cross-section, and a planner finds the path of least stretch. A rigid hanger needs 43–61 cm of loop (a whole arm), which stretches a tee’s neck by up to 33% against an assumed 35% limit, and a buttoned woven collar cannot take it, so every shirt goes in at the hem and has its hook threaded out blind (about 10 s against 6 s). A telescoping hanger collapsed to a 10 cm half width needs 31 cm and passes five of seven shirt sizes; folding the arms down 60° needs 37–40 cm. The 2–3 cm round end that hanger-fit needs adds only about 3 cm of loop, so insertion changes the width design, not the end. Stylised: planar hanger, perimeter bound rather than cloth, assumed stretch limits and robot constants.

How many dryers can one laundry-hanging robot serve, and what limits it?

research/hanger-cell · 12 tests

How many dryers can one laundry-hanging robot serve, and what limits it? A discrete-event model of the whole cell (pick, sense, feeder, insertion, rail, fold table) fed by hanger-insert and hanger-fold: with assumed times one arm clears a 25-item load in 8.9 min (about 5 dryers), a second arm adds 22% before the fold table binds, and the five hanger designs differ by at most 3%; pick and fold times dominate. Stylised, assumed station times.

Abstract

A discrete-event model of the whole cell (pick, sense, hanger feeder, insertion, rail and fold table) fed with hanger-insert’s insertion times and hanger-fold’s hang-or-fold shares. With assumed pick and fold times, one arm clears a 25-item dryer load in 8.9 min (169 items/h, about 5 dryers on a 45 min cycle); a second arm adds 22% and then the fold table is the bottleneck. The five hanger designs differ by at most 3% in time per load. Doubling the fold time adds 5.9 min and doubling the pick time 3.4, against under 1 min for insertion, though a threefold slower insertion costs a one-arm cell 22%. A poor picker (0.60 success) costs 13% throughput but leaves 7 of every 100 items for a person. Stylised: every station time except insertion is an assumed placeholder, no layout or collisions.

Can the hanger a cloth simulation designed be 3D-printed, and does it hold a garment?

research/hanger-print · 11 tests

Can the hanger a cloth simulation designed be 3D-printed, and does it hold a garment? hanger-fit's adjustable shape and four-width rack as parametric STL and OpenSCAD with a beam check: the round-ended arm has a safety factor of 40–70 in assumed PLA, a wire-like arm 0.13, and no useful width fits a 220 mm bed whole (one arm with its hub does). Stylised, assumed material constants.

Abstract

Exports hanger-fit’s adjustable shape and four-width rack as parametric STL and OpenSCAD, with a cantilever strength, deflection and print-bed check. Printed flat in PLA under assumed constants (1.5 kg garment, 2× motion factor), the round-ended arm (end radius 2.86 cm) has a safety factor of 40–70 at 12 mm depth and bends under 0.04 mm, so strength never limits it; a wire-like arm (end radius 1.5 mm) has a safety factor of 0.13 and fails. No hanger of useful width fits a 220 mm bed whole, but one arm with its hub does up to 24.8 cm. Stylised: assumed material constants, flat extrusion, OpenSCAD files not rendered, no two-piece joint.

How should a laundry robot pick one garment out of a tangled dryer load?

research/hanger-pick · 11 tests

How should a laundry robot pick one garment out of a tangled dryer load? In a stylised disc-pile model aiming at a visible opening takes 13.4 assumed seconds per garment against 22.7 for pinching the top and regrasping, and pushing garments aside first is slower (17.7 s); one arm then serves 151, 110 and 127 items an hour. Assumed times, not a robot.

Abstract

A stylised 2D pile of overlapping disc garments, 40% with the opening hidden by the garment itself, emptied by three scripted policies with assumed times. Aiming at a visible neck or waistband takes 13.4 s per garment against 22.7 s for pinching the top and regrasping; pushing garments aside first is slower (17.7 s) because neighbours rarely hide every opening, the garments themselves do. In hanger-cell the policies give 151, 110 and 127 items an hour for one arm, against 169 for its placeholder pick. Stylised: assumed times, disc cloth, not a measurement of a robot.

How long may a laundry robot spend finding a garment's opening before blind pinching is faster?

research/hanger-pickbreak · 5 tests

How long may a laundry robot spend finding a garment's opening before blind pinching is faster? On paired piles of the stylised pick model, aiming wins only if the detector costs under about 9 s per pick (95% interval 8.9 to 9.6, assumed times); the limit falls as regrasps get likelier to succeed or more openings are hidden. Stylised.

Abstract

On paired piles in the stylised hanger-pick model, aiming at a visible opening beats a blind pinch only if finding the opening costs under 9.2 s per pick (95% interval 8.9 to 9.6, assumed times). The threshold grows with the time of a regrasp (2.9 s to 18.7 s for 2 to 12 s), shrinks as regrasps succeed more often (14.7 s to 3.9 s) and as more openings are hidden (15.3 s to 0), and does not depend on pile size. Sweeping garments aside first beats aiming only when a sweep costs under half a second. Stylised: every time is an assumed placeholder.

How often may a laundry robot's opening detector aim wrong before blind pinching is faster?

research/hanger-misaim · 5 tests

How often may a laundry robot's opening detector aim wrong before blind pinching is faster? In the stylised hanger-pick model each 10 points of detector error cost about 1 s of look-time budget: 9.2 s per pick when perfect, 4.5 s at 50% wrong, none when always wrong. Stylised.

Abstract

In the stylised hanger-pick model a wrong aim costs one wasted grasp and then the same regrasp hunt as a blind pinch. The look-time budget of the detector falls almost linearly with its error: 9.2 s per pick when perfect, 6.3 s at 30% wrong, 4.5 s at 50%, about 0 when always wrong (300 paired piles, assumed times). At a 1 s look aiming still wins until about 88% of aims are wrong; at 5 s the limit is 43%, at 9 s 3%, and an 11 s look loses even when perfect. Stylised: every time and probability is an assumed placeholder; error is independent per pick.

Does the 2D hanger result survive in 3D, with front and back panels and a hanger arm of finite depth?

research/hanger-3d · 4 tests

Does the 2D hanger result survive in 3D? With an arm as deep as the garment a 3D cloth sheet matches the 2D drape (within 0.05 cm for round-ended hangers) and the best width is again 1 cm over the half shoulder; a shallow arm lets the front and back panels fall 5–8 cm and raises a wooden hanger's error from 0.4 to 2.0 cm. Stylised.

Abstract

A 3D cloth sheet (stretch, shear and bending in both directions, penalty contact with a swept arm) reproduces the 2D hanger-fit drape when the arm is as deep as the garment: shape error within 0.05 cm for the round-ended and wooden hangers and within 0.6 cm for thin wire (which is resolution-sensitive). The width finding holds: the best arm is 1 cm wider than the half shoulder for child, M and XXL tees, in 2D and 3D. What 2D cannot show is depth: on a 3 cm half-depth arm the wooden hanger's whole-sheet error rises from 0.4 to 2.0 cm, and on a rod to 3.8 cm, as the front and back panels fall 5–8 cm below the arm. Stylised: assumed fabric constants, one 20 cm strip, coarse grid (0.5 cm by 2.2 cm), no collar, sleeve seam or friction.

How many dryers can one laundry-hanging arm serve once the pick is simulated, not assumed?

research/hanger-cellpick · 6 tests

How many dryers can one laundry-hanging arm serve once the pick is simulated, not assumed? A one-arm cell drops from 169 to 147 garments an hour (5.1 to 4.4 dryers) with look-then-aim picking and to 104 with blind pinching; with two arms the fold table is the limit. Stylised.

Abstract

Replacing the assumed 8 s, 0.85 pick of the hanger-cell discrete-event model with the hanger-pick pile model cuts a one-arm cell from 169 to 147 garments hung or folded per hour (5.1 to 4.4 dryers) with look-then-aim picking, and to 104 with blind pinching. With two arms the fold table is the limit and the policy matters little (206 assumed, 201 aiming, 179 pinching); quartering all pick times still leaves about 6.2 dryers. Aiming leaves 2.8 garments per 100 for a person, pinching 7.2. Stylised: every station time is an assumed placeholder.

Can a laundry hanger be printed in two halves on a 220 mm bed, and is the bolted joint strong enough?

research/hanger-split · 13 tests

Can a laundry hanger be printed in two halves on a 220 mm bed, and is the bolted joint strong enough? A half-lap hub joint with two M4 bolts lets every width up to XXL print on a 220 mm bed; the joint (safety factor 3.7–13) is then weaker than the arm (40–70), and printed pegs fail. Stylised, assumed material constants; nothing printed.

Abstract

A half-lap hub joint with two M4 bolts lets every hanger-fit width up to the 25.8 cm XXL print on a 220 mm bed as one part printed twice. In a beam model with assumed PLA constants, the joint, not the arm, is then the weak point: safety factor 3.7–13 against 40–70 for the arm, set by bolt bearing on the half-depth boss, and the joint needs 4–9 mm of depth against the arm’s 0.8 mm. Printed pegs instead of bolts fail (safety factor 0.9–1.8 for 4 mm pegs). Stylised; nothing was printed or tested.

Does a second 3D cloth model, wrapped over the hanger arm, agree with the 2D hanger drape?

research/hanger-wrap · 5 tests

Does a second 3D cloth model, wrapped over the arm, agree with the 2D hanger drape? On the cases both run, it picks the same best arm width (22.8 cm for a size M tee; error correlation 0.994) and adds the bend across the arm (119 deg/cm over a 0.5 cm arm, 34 over a 2 cm one), but disagrees for a heavy knit on a thin arm and is not grid-converged. Stylised.

Abstract

An independent 3D mass-spring sheet that wraps over a round-ended arm (cross-check of hanger-3d), draped on the hangers of hanger-fit with the same fabric constants and mass. It picks the same best arm width as the 2D chain (22.8 cm for a size M tee; error correlation 0.994 over a width sweep), with error 0.1–0.4 cm higher near the optimum, and adds the bend across the arm: 119 deg/cm over a 0.5 cm arm against 34 over a 2 cm one. It disagrees for a heavy knit on a thin arm (17.8 cm against 5.6 cm error) and is not grid-converged (1.19 against 0.62 cm at 1 and 0.5 cm spacing). Stylised, assumed fabric constants.

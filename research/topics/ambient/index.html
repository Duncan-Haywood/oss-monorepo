Ambient AI assistant

How much latency and memory does an open-model voice pipeline cost on a CPU, and what reply time does it leave?

research/ambient-pipeline · 7 tests

CPU voice pipeline (Silero VAD, Whisper tiny.en, Qwen2.5-0.5B, Piper) on synthetic speech: per-stage latency/RTF/memory and a reply-latency budget of 0.9–1.5 s at 200–800 ms endpoint hangover, ~0.7 s of it compute. Server CPU, not a phone.

Abstract

A streaming Silero VAD, Whisper tiny.en (int8), Qwen2.5-0.5B (4-bit) and Piper chain on four CPU cores, on six synthetic sentences. VAD costs 0.15 ms per 32 ms frame, ASR 306 ms per segment (RTF 0.11), the first LLM sentence 267 ms, the first TTS chunk 88 ms, and the models add about 740 MB. End of speech to first audio is 0.94–1.55 s at 200–800 ms endpoint hangover, of which about 0.7 s is compute; a 200 ms hangover already cut one utterance in two, and a 0.5 s target is impossible once the hangover alone exceeds it. Stylised: synthetic clean speech, one server CPU not a phone, batch ASR, LLM answer quality not evaluated.

When should an always-on assistant speak, and does a policy tuned in a simulator keep its annoyance budget in a real room?

research/ambient-interject · 9 tests

When an always-on assistant should speak: wake, keyword, threshold and learned policies at equal annoyance, and what breaks when the simulator's room differs from the real one

Abstract

Simulated 10 s listening windows (needs at 3%, an intent score with d′ = 2, wake-phrase and keyword signals) compare interruption policies at equal annoyance. A wake word is precise (0.69) but catches 15% of needs; adding a keyword reaches 53% recall at 28.7 false interruptions per hour (0.72 for the wake word). At a budget of 1 false per hour a score threshold reaches recall 0.26 and a logistic policy that also uses the keyword and wake flag 0.33; recall at that budget climbs from 0.17 to 0.82 as d′ goes from 1 to 4. Tuned to 1 false per hour, policies keep the budget when only the need side shifts (weaker detector, rarer needs) but run at 3.6–3.9 per hour when the no-need background shifts (noisier score, more wake false alarms); 16 labelled hours restore it for the noisier score, and no threshold can for a wake detector that alone causes 3.5 false per hour. Stylised: independent windows, Gaussian scores, chosen costs, no real conversations.

How much does speech-recognition error hurt an always-on assistant's trigger policies at equal annoyance?

research/ambient-asrnoise · 6 tests

How much speech-recognition error hurts an always-on assistant's trigger policies at equal annoyance: from word error rate 0 to 0.5 a two-word wake phrase loses 74% of its recall and a transcript-reading threshold 76%, an acoustic-only score about 54%, and the transcript policy falls behind the acoustic one at WER 0.3 to 0.4 if its separation shrinks as (1-WER)^2 (stylised).

Abstract

The ambient-interject world with an ASR word error rate (WER) entering each signal by assumption: a two-word wake phrase needs both words right, a keyword one, a transcript-reading intent score loses separation as (1−WER)k, an acoustic score is unaffected. At 2 false interruptions per hour, going from WER 0 to 0.5 cuts the wake phrase's recall from 0.151 to 0.039 (−74%) and the transcript threshold's from 0.365 to 0.089 (−76%), while the acoustic threshold falls from 0.189 to 0.087. With k = 1 the transcript policy stays marginally ahead at WER 0.5; with k = 2 the acoustic policy overtakes it at WER 0.25–0.4. Thresholds tuned at WER 0 stay under the false-interruption budget when deployed at higher WER. Stylised: the WER-to-signal mapping is assumed, not fitted to real recognition, and ASR confidence is not modelled.

When moments of need cluster in time, does a cooldown, a boost or a back-off help an always-on assistant at equal annoyance?

research/ambient-cluster · 7 tests

When moments of need cluster in time, does a cooldown, boost or back-off help an always-on assistant at equal annoyance: an episode-aware boost gains up to 0.05 recall under strong clustering, a cooldown always loses recall, back-off is a wash; stylised simulation.

Abstract

Needs are made to cluster with a hidden episode/quiet chain (overall rate fixed at 3% of 10 s windows), and a plain score threshold is compared with three stateful rules at the same budget of false interruptions per hour, paired over 10 seeds. With strong clustering an episode-aware boost (lower the threshold after an accepted answer) raises recall by 0.039 at 1 false/h and 0.054 at 4 (0.467 to 0.521); a cooldown after speaking loses recall everywhere, up to 0.066; a back-off after a dismissal is within noise or slightly negative. Without clustering nothing helps. At the 1 false/h budget the boost also overshoots the budget slightly (1.19 vs 1.11). Stylised: false alarms are independent of the episode state, feedback is exact and immediate, every distribution is chosen, no real conversations.

Under a storage limit, which transcripts should an on-device assistant keep, and what survives?

research/ambient-memory · 9 tests

What an on-device assistant should keep from a week of simulated transcripts: BM25 retrieval, extractive summaries and byte-limited retention policies (stylised)

Abstract

Seven simulated days of 1500 utterances each with 6 planted facts per day. BM25 finds every fact (hit@5 = 1.00) when the key words are rare, which is the easy case. With the store limited to 10% of the week, dropping oldest or random utterances keeps 0.11 and 0.10 of the facts; keeping the highest mean-IDF utterances keeps 0.93, because the facts are rare-word lines by construction. A stylised expected-utility model chooses between speech, a glanceable display and silence by noise, other talkers, free eyes and message length; a Monte Carlo check agrees with the closed form to 0.001. How much reply latency can an always-on assistant hide by starting its pipeline before the speaker has finished?

research/ambient-speculate · 9 tests

How much reply latency starting the ASR/LLM/TTS chain during the end-of-speech hangover hides, from the measured ambient-pipeline stage times and an assumed within-turn pause model (hold-until-endpoint vs eager; extra runs per turn)

Abstract

Using the measured ASR, LLM and TTS times from ambient-pipeline (a 682 ms median chain on one server CPU, six synthetic utterances), the reply pipeline starts after S ms of silence instead of after the full end-of-speech hangover H and the reply is held until H confirms the turn is over. Holding saves exactly min(chain, H − S): at H = 1000 ms the median reply falls from 1744 to 1061 ms, at the price of 0.7 to 1.9 extra pipeline runs per turn depending on S, and the just-in-time start S = H − chain is the cheapest that hides everything. Pause durations inside a turn are assumed (five lognormal settings, not data); with median 400 ms pauses no hangover up to 1.2 s keeps cut-offs under 10%. Closed forms agree with a Monte Carlo check.

research/ambient-semend · 8 tests

Can a sentence-completeness cue let an always-on assistant stop waiting so long before it replies: a stylised two-hangover endpointer cuts the mean wait from 913 to 574 ms at a 10% cut-off budget if no pause sits at a complete point, but only to 837 ms if half do; cue and pauses assumed.

Abstract

A stylised signal-detection model: the endpointer waits a short hangover Hs after a sentence the cue calls complete and a longer Hl otherwise. With medium pauses (2 per turn, median 250 ms, assumed) and a 10% cut-off budget, a fixed hangover is 913 ms; a cue of quality d = 2 cuts the mean wait to 574 ms if no pause sits at a complete point, but only to 837 ms if half of them do, because the cue is blind to those. Abstract

How many audited messages an assistant needs to estimate a noisy urgency label's confusion matrix and prior well enough to act on it: about 50 at moderate accuracy, with smoothing helping at tiny audit sets (stylised)

Abstract

Extending the stylised urgency-label model, the assistant estimates the label’s class mix and confusion matrix from n audited messages and then acts by Bayes’ rule. With maximum-likelihood estimates, n = 10 is worse than ignoring the label (mean utility 0.363 against 0.382 at 70% label accuracy); n = 50 recovers 76% of the gain of knowing the true parameters and n = 200 recovers 92%. What does a noisy auditor do to an assistant that learns its urgency label’s reliability from audits?

research/ambient-noisyaudit · 7 tests

What a noisy human auditor does to an assistant that learns its urgency label's reliability from audits: taking audit labels as truth biases the estimate and loses 8% to 69% of the label's gain at a 0.9 to 0.7 auditor, and correcting for a known auditor accuracy recovers it (stylised)

Abstract

Extending the stylised urgency-label model, the audit labels are themselves wrong. Taking them as truth biases the estimated label accuracy (0.57 instead of 0.70 with a 0.8-accuracy auditor) and does not improve with more audits: the policy loses 47% of the label’s gain over ignoring it at label accuracy 0.7, and 31% at 0.9. A 0.9 auditor costs only 8–14%. Stylised; both label and auditor are made-up uniform-error matrices.

Abstract

Abstract

Extending the noisy-audit model, a wrongly assumed accuracy for a 0.8-accuracy auditor costs 31% of a 0.9 label’s gain if the auditor is trusted fully, 17% if assumed 0.9 and 6% if assumed 0.6 (about 39–47% at either extreme for a 0.7 label). A stylised follow-up to ambient-falsewake. On Gaussian AR(1) frame scores, wake counts per 1,000 s window are close to Poisson (Fano factor 1.05–1.5 at φ = 0.9–0.95).

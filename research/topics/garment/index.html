Where does a designer coat's price go, and what does a $20 to $50 replica keep?

research/garment-teardown · 7 tests

Bottom-up cost model for a lined coat applied to two reported designer prices: modelled factory cost is under 15% of price, the assumed markup chain does not close the gap, and a $20–50 replica needs very cheap cloth and labour. All inputs assumed. Stylised.

Abstract

A bottom-up cost model for a lined woven coat (fabric from pattern area and marker efficiency, trims, sewing minutes times wage, overhead, brand and retail multipliers) applied to two reported designer prices. Under assumed inputs the factory cost is $186–248 for a $2,850 trench and $469–551 for a $3,850 coat, under 15% of the price in every wage scenario, and the assumed multipliers do not close the gap. A cheap-cloth replica costs $46–100 to make; only a very lean case reaches $29–34. Stylised: every cost input is assumed, prices are from search snippets, and fit and durability are not scored.

Can a cheap cloth drape score how well a flat pattern fits a body, and is a fitted pattern body-specific?

research/garment-pattern-fit · 8 tests

Position-based cloth drape scores a flat skirt-tube pattern on two synthetic elliptical bodies by strain and waist ease; the best waist is near the body's waist perimeter and a pattern fitted to one body fits the other clearly worse. Stylised; strains depend on mesh and solver.

Abstract

A flat skirt-tube pattern is draped by position-based-dynamics cloth on two synthetic elliptical-section bodies and scored by circumferential strain and waist ease. The best waist is close to the body’s waist perimeter (×1.01 and ×1.03); tighter patterns strain, looser ones leave ease. A pattern optimised for one body costs 2.1–2.9 on the other against own optima of 0.15–0.75 (arbitrary cost units). Stylised: the body is a stack of ellipses, the cost weights are assumptions, the hem optimum sits at the edge of the search range, and the optimum cost moves from 0.75 to 1.34 on a finer mesh.

How much cloth does marker making waste, and do annealing and exact methods beat greedy nesting?

research/garment-nesting · 8 tests

Marker making as strip packing: on 20 random 8-box markers, shelf heuristics leave 17–20% extra length vs the exact optimum, annealing 0.5%; on two stylised garments, polygon nesting beats box packing at equal budget. Stylised; no seam allowance or multi-ply.

Abstract

Marker making as strip packing. On 20 random 8-box markers an exact MILP proves optimality on 19; shelf heuristics (NFDH, FFDH) are on average 20% and 17% longer than optimal, bottom-left 7.3%, and simulated annealing over placement order 0.5%. On two stylised garments (a shirt and a four-gore skirt, no seam allowance, one ply) true-polygon nesting with 180° turns beats bounding-box packing at the same annealing budget in every case, by about 5–9% for the shirt and 9–43% for the skirt. Stylised: the shapes are invented, polygon results have no exact reference, and the raster is conservative.

What do seam allowance and mixed sizes do to marker efficiency, and what does that do to a garment's cloth cost?

research/garment-markercost · 6 tests

A 1 cm seam allowance cut the stylised shirt's marker efficiency from 0.69 to 0.63 and the skirt's from 0.71 to 0.51; mixing three sizes in one marker shortened it 9%; swapped into the cost model, trench cloth rises from $26 to $33–41 at $7/m². Stylised; sensitivity, not a coat measurement.

Abstract

Follow-up to the nesting and cost-teardown projects. Adding a 1 cm seam allowance to stylised pieces lowered marker efficiency from 0.69 to 0.63 for a shirt and from 0.71 to 0.51 for a four-gore skirt (the skirt mostly loses its interlocking gores); nesting three shirt sizes in one marker was 9% shorter than three separate markers. Swapping the measured efficiencies into the teardown formula moves a $7/m2 trench’s cloth cost from $26 (assumed 0.80) to $33–41. Stylised: invented outlines, one ply, small annealing budget (differences of a few cm are noise), and the coats were not nested, so the cost step is a sensitivity, not a measurement.

How much cloth does a cutting plan save by mixing sizes in one marker, and is the saving real?

Cutting-order planning for a 3-size stylised shirt: laying several garments per marker saves 9–11% of cloth over one per marker; mixing sizes adds 0.4–3.7% in sample, but re-nesting the chosen markers with fresh seeds leaves +0.09%, so it is within nesting noise. Stylised; invented shapes.

Abstract

Cutting-order planning as an exact integer program (marker mixes and plies) over 34 nested markers of a stylised three-size shirt. Laying several garments per marker saves 9–11% of cloth against one garment per marker on 20 random orders. Mixing sizes adds a further 0.4–3.7% in sample, but re-nesting the markers the plan picked with fresh seeds leaves a +0.09% difference: the gain is within annealing noise (about 5%) and partly selection of luckily short markers. How far is a fixed 0.80 marker efficiency from a nested coat, and what does the difference do to the cost model?

research/garment-nestcost · 6 tests

Nesting a stylised coat's 10 shell and 5 lining pieces with a 1.5 cm seam allowance gives marker efficiencies of 0.68–0.73 (shell) and 0.59–0.68 (lining) at three cloth widths, below the fixed 0.80 in garment-teardown; its trench FOB moves +9% to +15% at the teardown's own areas. Abstract

Follow-up to the nesting and cost-teardown projects (stylised, preliminary). A coat drawn as 10 shell and 5 lining polygons, grown by a 1.5 cm seam allowance and nested by annealing at cloth widths of 110, 140 and 150 cm, gives marker efficiencies of 0.68–0.73 for the shell and 0.59–0.68 for the lining, against the fixed 0.80 assumed earlier (0.77–0.82 without allowance). How many clothing sizes does it take to fit a population, and does a regular S-M-L grid waste them?

research/garment-sizeset · 6 tests

How many sizes fit a population? On a stylised synthetic bust/hip population (+-4 cm), an exact size run beats a regular grid by 12 to 25 points at correlation 0.9 but not on fresh people at correlation 0; greedy is within 3.7 points of optimal (stylised, preliminary).

Abstract

Stylised and preliminary: a synthetic bust and hip population (assumed means 95 and 100 cm, sd 8 cm, no survey data) with a fit tolerance of ±4 cm. Choosing k sizes to fit the most people is maximum coverage; solved exactly (integer program), greedily and as a regular grid on 300 people, 3 seeds. At bust–hip correlation 0.9, exact sizes fit 12–25 points more people than the best grid and 6 sizes reach 80%; greedy is within 3.7 points of exact. At correlation 0 the gap is small, and on 20,000 fresh people the exact set is no better than the grid, so the optimiser overfits small samples. Limits: Gaussian two-measurement population, pass/fail box fit, no cost of extra sizes.

How many clothing sizes does a stretch fabric save?

research/garment-sizestretch · 5 tests

How many sizes does a stretch fabric save? On a stylised synthetic bust/hip population, 10% usable stretch cuts the sizes needed for 80% fit from 6 to more than 8 down to 2 or 3; an idealised upper bound (preliminary).

Abstract

Stylised and preliminary: follow-up to the size-set project on a synthetic bust and hip population (assumed means 95 and 100 cm, sd 8 cm, no survey data). A body fits a garment if it is at most 8 cm smaller and no more than stretch larger than the nominal size, in both measurements; the best k sizes are found exactly (integer program). Sizes needed to fit 80% fall from 6 to more than 8 with no stretch to 2–3 at 10% and 1 at 20%, and the loss on fresh people shrinks too. The stretch model is idealised: usable stretch is far below rated stretch, so these are upper bounds.

How much comfort can an infrared-transparent or gating textile buy, and which fabric properties matter?

research/garment-thermal · 11 tests

Fanger/ISO PMV and a two-node model extended with fabric infrared transmittance, emissivity and solar absorptance: indoors, transmittance 0→0.8 is worth ~2.4 °C of comfort at 28 °C; in sun, absorptance dominates. Stylised; assumed fabric properties, no reference for the optical extension.

Abstract

A Fanger/ISO 7730 PMV model and a Gagge-style two-node model extended with fabric infrared emissivity and transmittance, solar absorptance and vapour permeability. The no-optics PMV matches an open-source ISO implementation to 0.006 over 144 cases. Indoors without sun, infrared transmittance (0 to 0.8) is worth about 2.4 °C of comfort at 28 °C, more than cutting insulation from 0.7 to 0.3 clo (1.8 °C), but makes a 16 °C room feel 7.3 °C colder; in sun, solar absorptance (7.7 °C span) dominates the infrared properties. Stylised: assumed fabric properties, a parallel-path radiation model with no reference data, and recalled two-node constants.

How far is the repository's two-node thermal model from an open-source implementation of the Gagge model?

research/garment-twonodecheck · 3 tests

Cross-checks garment-thermal's two-node model against pythermalcomfort over 108 conditions: core temperature within 0.25 °C, skin temperature mean 0.6 °C (up to 2.1 °C) apart; a comparison of implementations, not of people.

Abstract

Runs the two-node model from the thermal-comfort project against pythermalcomfort over 108 conditions (18–36 °C, 30–80% humidity, 1–2 met, 0.3–1 clo). Core temperature agrees within 0.25 °C everywhere; skin temperature differs by 0.58 °C on average and up to 2.1 °C, mostly in cool air; evaporative loss differs by up to 14 W/m2 in hot humid air. The direction of change with insulation and humidity agrees in 23 of 24 cases. This compares two implementations, not either against people; causes of the skin-temperature gap are untested and the optical extension is not covered.

Which design choices explain the gap between the repository's two-node model and an open-source Gagge implementation?

research/garment-twonodealign · 3 tests

Switches nine design choices one at a time between garment-thermal's two-node model and pythermalcomfort's: variable skin mass fraction, convection coefficient and vasodilation gain explain most of the 0.58 °C skin-temperature gap; a comparison of implementations, not of people.

Abstract

Rebuilds the two-node model with nine switchable choices (body area, constants, convection, clothing radiation, skin mass fraction, vasodilation gain, sweat caps, update order, step count), each set to the repository's version or to the pythermalcomfort version read from its source. With all nine set to the reference's, the model matches it to 0.008 in every output over 108 conditions. Switching one at a time, the skin-temperature gap (mean 0.58 °C) falls most with the variable skin mass fraction (to 0.36), the convection coefficient (0.43) and the vasodilation gain (0.44); body area, constants, update order and step count each change it by under 0.01 °C. A comparison of two implementations, not of either against people; the effects interact, so the attribution depends on the order.

Abstract

What does a thermostat-driven infrared-gating textile do that its fixed closed and open states do not?

research/garment-twonodeswitch · 5 tests

A skin-temperature-triggered infrared-gating textile with hysteresis and actuation lag settles to a fixed closed or open state under constant rooms (no chatter, lag only delays the start); gains need changing conditions; stylised, preliminary, mostly null.

Abstract

Adds a skin-temperature-triggered gate (hysteresis band, first-order actuation lag) to the switchable two-node comfort model (stylised, preliminary). In constant 31, 24 and 16 C rooms over 3 h the gate settles into one of the two fixed states with at most one change, so it cannot beat the best fixed state; a 10 min lag only delays the start (open 94% vs 98% of minutes at 31 C). No fabric or person was measured.

If a thermostat infrared-gating band is tuned on some rooms, does it still help in rooms it never saw?

research/garment-twonodeheld · 4 tests

Tuning the thermostat infrared-gating band on training rooms and testing on held-out rooms: a narrow band just below 33.7 C carries over (about 0.01 to 0.06 C better than the untuned band) but chatters, up to 24 switches in 180 min (stylised).

Abstract

A stylised, preliminary follow-up to garment-twonodemet. A skin-temperature gate band is tuned by exhaustive grid search on rest-to-walk and walk-to-rest steps in 22, 24 and 26 C rooms, then scored unchanged on held-out rooms (23, 25, 20 and 28 C). The tuned band (a narrow 33.6–33.7 C) beats the untuned 33.2–34.2 C band and both fixed states on both held-out sets under both choice sets, by 0.01 to 0.06 C against the untuned band. It also beats the four-schedule oracle on training scenarios, because the oracle is restricted, and it chatters (up to 24 switches in 180 min) with instant, free switching, so the gain may not survive actuation lag or cost. Six training and eight held-out scenarios from one model; no real fabric or person.

Retuning the thermostat infrared-gating band with an actuation lag (0 to 10 min) and a cost per switch: lag cuts the gain over never switching from 0.23 to 0.15 C (stylised), a switch penalty widens the band and stops the chatter, and under one choice set the tuned band can lose to the untuned one.

Abstract

A stylised, preliminary follow-up to garment-twonodeheld. The skin-temperature gate band is retuned by exhaustive grid search for each actuation lag (0, 2, 5, 10 min) and each cost per switch (0, 0.01, 0.03 C), then scored on held-out rooms. A proportional infrared-gating transmittance against the tuned on/off thermostat gate: a negative result, within 0.010 C in every cell because the tuned ramp collapses to nearly on/off, and a softer ramp costs 0.02 to 0.07 C (stylised).

Abstract

A stylised, preliminary, negative follow-up to garment-twonodelag. A transmittance that ramps linearly with skin temperature is tuned by exhaustive search (72 bands) for each actuation lag (0 to 10 min) and scored on held-out rooms against the tuned on/off gate. Proportional wins in 5 of 16 cells and every difference is within 0.010 C: the search picks bands only 0.1 to 0.2 C wide, i.e. nearly on/off. Abstract

A stylised, preliminary follow-up to garment-twonodepen. Both gates are retuned on a wider set of rooms (20–28 C instead of 22–26 C) and scored on rooms held out of both. Is a second garment better than a dearer single one?

Is a second garment better than a dearer single one? A shell-and-layer pair beats the best single garment at the same budget in 54 of 66 wearing mixes at $50 and $100 (mean +0.49 C and +1.02 C) once thin cloth is removed; the apparent $20 gain is an artefact of thin cloth's negative assumed cost. Stylised; premiums and wearing shares assumed.

Abstract

Lets the wearer own two garments (a shell and a layer) and wear whichever is most comfortable in each of three scenarios, searching all lever pairs under one total added-retail budget on the replica trench. Abstract

If a wrong pick is a random wrong option the pair’s edge holds (+0.55 C at e = 0, +0.57 C at 0.3, $100 budget), because errors hurt a single garment’s all-or-nothing choice more than an overlapping pair. If every mistake is the worst owned option the $100 edge falls to +0.09 C at e = 0.3, with a break-even e of median 0.72 and as low as 0.04. At $50 the edge is negligible either way. Stylised: error rates and premiums are assumed, no wearer behaviour was observed, and the conclusion flips between the two error models.

Abstract

A thin-sheet shielding model of a conductive square weave (sheet resistance, aperture bound, metal mass) plus a Monte Carlo of random yarn breakage. Every design tried has a sheet shielding of 54–87 dB, but the aperture bound limits it: 36 dB at 2.4 GHz for a 1 mm pitch, about 15 dB at 28 GHz. Does contact resistance at yarn crossings matter for an RF-shielding fabric?

research/garment-contactres · 6 tests

Contact resistance at yarn crossings in the RF-shielding weave: no effect on an intact fabric, but with 20% of yarns broken a contact equal to one segment raises sheet resistance 22% and ten segments 2.3×. Stylised; contact values are a sweep, not measured.

Abstract

A two-layer resistor network of a conductive weave: warp and weft touch only through a contact resistor at each crossing, and yarn segments break at random. On an intact fabric the contacts carry no current, so they have no effect at all. Once yarns break they carry the bypass currents: with 20% of segments broken, the median sheet resistance is 1.74 segment-resistances with perfect contacts, 2.11 with contacts equal to one segment, 4.07 at ten and 16.7 at a hundred. For a stylised stainless weave that is 59.6 dB against 52.3 dB at ten segments (thin-sheet only, aperture limit excluded). Stylised: contact values are a sweep, not measurements; one lattice size, 20 draws, independent breaks.

Does it matter where an RF-shielding fabric is damaged, and whether worn contacts go with the breaks?

Abstract

Follow-up to the contact-resistance model, on a 24×24 conductive weave with 60 draws per cell and bootstrap intervals. When contacts next to a broken yarn are worn (contact resistance ×k), the median sheet resistance at 20% broken and a base contact of one segment rises from 2.05 segment-resistances (k = 1) to 3.03 (k = 10). Clustering matters more: a fold band across the current that holds the mean break fraction fixed but breaks 4× as often inside the band leaves the weave open in 57 of 60 draws at 20% broken, while the same band along the current direction lowers the resistance to 1.43. Stylised: square lattice, one lattice size, a rule-of-thumb wear multiplier rather than a measured one, no flexing physics, and no measurement.

Abstract

The 8-layer stacks optimised in garment-stackopt are fabricated virtually with independent Gaussian thickness error (3 and 5%) and scored on 200 held-out draws. Abstract

Does an assistive garment pay for its own mass?

Abstract

A one-joint ankle model of an exosuit-style garment: a suit adding a fraction of the biological push-off torque cuts modelled whole-body metabolic cost by 3.1, 8.8 and 14.1% at gains 0.1, 0.3 and 0.5, but a delay of 0.25 of the gait cycle removes the benefit and a stiff passive spring raises ankle cost 17%.

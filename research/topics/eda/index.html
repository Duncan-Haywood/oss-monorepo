Algorithmic chip-design loops

Chip-design loops that need no LLM: algorithms propose designs and flow settings, open tools score them.

25 projects, each with its own page, code and tests. See them on the full research index.

Can one harness check, score, cache and log candidate chip designs for any proposer?

research/eda-harness · 4 tests

Unattended design loop (lint, bit-exact RTL sim, gate-netlist check, Yosys synthesis, hash cache, JSONL log, pluggable proposers) run on chip-accel's 40-design grid: synthesis and the gate check are 99% of wall time (stylised, generic-gate counts, no place and route).

Abstract

A loop that takes a parameterised generator and a search space and runs each candidate through lint, bit- and cycle-exact RTL simulation, a gate-netlist simulation check and Yosys synthesis, caching by design hash and logging every evaluation as JSON. On chip-accel’s MAC engine (40 designs) all candidates pass; synthesis and the gate-level check take 99% of the 424 s of wall time and RTL simulation under 1%. The Pareto front matches chip-accel’s. A follow-up reuses one Yosys run for the gate check and the score: identical metrics on all 40 designs and 50% less wall time (561 s to 280 s, single timing runs). Stylised: one design family, generic-gate counts, simulation-based (not formal) equivalence, no place and route.

Does a space-filling grid order beat the harness's lexicographic order on other chip-design families, or was that luck of one table?

research/eda-gridxfer · 5 tests

Does a space-filling grid order beat the harness's lexicographic one on other families? Lexicographic is arbitrary (worst on MAC, best on FIR); farthest-point beat random from about 6 evaluations on every table, and eda-harness now offers it as an option (stylised, two 36-design families).

Abstract

The grid orders from eda-gridorder are replayed on the MAC and FIR design families of eda-surrogate (36 designs each, Yosys cell count against analytic cycles; nothing new synthesised). Lexicographic is not reliably bad but arbitrary: worst on MAC (90% of the front’s hypervolume after 25 evaluations against 3–4 for the others), best on FIR (4 against 5–9), because it wins exactly when the front sits at the first corner of the leading axis. Farthest-point beat random from about 6 evaluations on every table and found FIR’s full front in 16 evaluations against 28 for random; Halton was mixed. The harness gains an order option on its grid proposer. Stylised: two small families, two objectives, deterministic orders are single draws.

Does the order a grid proposer walks a chip-design space change how quickly it finds the Pareto front?

research/eda-gridorder · 7 tests

Does the order a grid walks a design space matter? The harness's lexicographic grid trails every random order to 30 evaluations; Halton reaches 90% of the front in 7 evaluations against 17 for random (stylised, one table, deterministic orders are single draws).

Abstract

The lexicographic grid that the harness ships is replayed against Halton, farthest-point and Latin orders and random shuffles on the 80-design table from eda-proposers (stdlib only, nothing new simulated). Lexicographic scores worse than all 300 random orders up to 30 evaluations and needs 75 evaluations for the whole front; a Halton order reaches 90% of the front's hypervolume in 7 evaluations against a median 17 for random, while random-start farthest-point and Latin orders gain only +0.010 to +0.042 over random from 8 to 20 evaluations and nothing by 40. Stylised: one small table, and the deterministic orders are single draws with no interval.

Which search method finds a chip design's Pareto front with the fewest evaluations?

research/eda-proposers · 10 tests

Random, grid, annealing, GA, NSGA-II and TPE at equal budgets by Pareto hypervolume on an 80-design logged space: TPE and NSGA-II lead random from about 16 to 30 evaluations, annealing trails it (stylised, tiny space, untuned).

Abstract

Random search, a grid, simulated annealing, a genetic algorithm, NSGA-II and a TPE-style Bayesian method are compared at equal evaluation budgets on an 80-design space (MAC lanes × bit width × ABC script) of a toy quantised-MLP accelerator, using Pareto hypervolume over 100 seeds. TPE reaches 0.917 of the true-front hypervolume at 16 evaluations against 0.891 for random, and 0.980 against 0.943 at 30; annealing trails random throughout. Stylised: the space is tiny (random search at 40 evaluations already scores 0.965), hyperparameters are untuned, objectives are generic-gate counts, and nothing is said about real chips.

Do cheap rungs of the chip-design tool ladder rank designs like synthesis, and how much synthesis time does screening save?

research/eda-fidelity · 7 tests

A three-rung fidelity ladder (analytic model, RTL simulation, synthesis) over 40 logged designs: the free rung ranks the objective at Spearman 0.74, and successive halving finds the optimum at 20 s of tool time against 205 s to synthesise everything (stylised, toy design family, no place and route).

Abstract

A three-rung ladder over 40 logged MAC-engine designs: a free analytic model (cells ≈ lanes×bits2), cycle-accurate simulation, and Yosys synthesis. The analytic rung ranks the cells×cycles objective with Spearman 0.74 against synthesis (0.95 for cells alone) and finds 2 of the 5 best designs. Successive halving that screens on the cheap rungs reaches the optimum with certainty at 20 s of tool time, against 0.10 for random search at the same budget and 205 s for synthesising everything. Stylised: one toy design family with its optimum at a corner, no place-and-route rung, and the search is a replay over the committed log.

Can cheap word-level RTL features predict synthesised cell counts well enough to screen a design search, and does that survive a new design family?

research/eda-surrogate · 12 tests

A cell-count surrogate from cheap word-level RTL features over 133 designs in four generator families: 3.9% median error on families it saw (non-negative linear model), 11% to 210% on a held-out family with rank agreement staying at Spearman 0.75 to 1.00, and it cuts syntheses to find a near-best design in a 36-design pool from 12.4 to 1-5 (stylised, synthesis-only target, no place and route).

Abstract

A surrogate for Yosys generic-gate cell counts, trained on 24 word-level features (multiplier, adder, mux and register sizes read after proc; opt, about 30× cheaper than synthesis) over 133 designs in four generator families I wrote: MAC engines, FIR filters, ALUs and sorting networks. On families it saw, a non-negative linear cost model has a 3.9% median error and within-family Spearman 0.995, beating boosted trees (8.5%). When a whole family is held out, median errors run from 10.5% to 210.7% and the best model changes with the family, but rank agreement stays at Spearman 0.75–1.00. Ordering a 36-design pool by predicted cells×cycles found a design within 2% of the best after 1–5 syntheses against 12.4 (MAC) and 18.6 (FIR) for random order, both optima sitting at an edge of the space, so the test is weak. Stylised: synthesis-only target, no place and route, small designs.

How many synthesised designs from a new family does a cell-count surrogate need before it is usable, and is it better to pool them with old families or train on them alone?

research/eda-adapt · 5 tests

Few-shot adaptation of the eda-surrogate cell-count model to an unseen design family: one synthesised design from the new family cuts the linear model's median error from 49% to 18%, training on the new family alone beats pooling from about three designs, boosted trees need about 12 (stylised, synthesis-only target, no place and route).

Abstract

A follow-up to eda-surrogate on its 133 designs in four generator families: when a word-level cell-count surrogate meets a family it has not seen, how many Yosys syntheses of that family restore it? Over 30 random draws per cell, one synthesised design pooled with the other families cuts the linear model’s median error from 48.6% to 18.4% (median over the four families), and training on the new family alone overtakes pooling from about three designs (4.8% against 10.4% at five). Boosted trees need far more (about 12 designs to get under 20%) and pooled boosting is worse than zero-shot on one family for up to five designs. Ranking is good throughout. Stylised: small clean families, random rather than chosen adaptation designs, a synthesis-only target, no place and route.

Which few designs of a new family should you synthesise to adapt a cell-count surrogate, and should they count for more?

research/eda-adaptpick · 7 tests

Which few designs of a new family to synthesise when adapting a cell-count surrogate: a farthest-point spread in feature space roughly halves the linear model's error against random draws at 5 designs (5.3% against 8.5%), the smallest designs are worst, and upweighting new points helps linear and ridge but not boosted trees (stylised, synthesis-only target).

Abstract

A follow-up to eda-adapt on the same 133 designs in four generator families. Choosing the k adaptation designs by farthest-point spread in feature space, instead of at random, roughly halves the linear model’s held-out median error at k = 5 (5.3% against 8.5%, new-family weight 5); picking the smallest designs is worst (15.2%). Upweighting the new family’s points helps the linear and ridge models from k = 5 but hurt boosted trees. Stylised: four small clean families, a synthesis-only cell-count target, deterministic choosers scored on different remaining sets, no confidence intervals.

Does the farthest-point spread still beat random draws when every chooser is scored on the same held-out designs?

research/eda-adaptfixed · 5 tests

eda-adaptpick's adaptation-design choosers re-scored on one fixed test set per family (4 families x 5 splits): the farthest-point spread still beats random draws for the linear model at every k (4.8% against 6.0% at 5 designs, w = 5) but wins in only 65% of cells, the smallest-first rule stays worst, and the earlier 'halves the error' margin shrinks to 20-45% at 5 to 8 designs.

Abstract

A check on eda-adaptpick, which scored each chooser on the designs it had not chosen. Here each of the four families is split five times into a pool (60%) and a fixed test set (40%); every chooser picks k pool designs from features only and all are scored on the same test set. The spread still beats random draws for the linear model at every k (4.8% against 6.0% at k = 5, weight 5; 2.9% against 5.2% at k = 8) but the margin is 20–45% at 5 to 8 designs, not the earlier halving, and for the linear model it wins in 50–80% of the 20 (family, split) cells rather than all. Picking the smallest designs stays worst. Stylised: 133 small designs, a Yosys cell-count target, no place and route, no significance test.

Does a cell-count surrogate used as a screen find a chip design’s Pareto front with fewer syntheses than random search or TPE?

research/eda-screen · 6 tests

A cell-count surrogate used as a screen in the design-search loop: 4 spread syntheses plus a log-linear fit reach 0.990 of the true front's hypervolume at 12 syntheses against 0.860 for random and 0.878 for TPE, of which about +0.05 comes from free cycles/error objectives (stylised, one 80-design space).

Abstract

On the 80-design accelerator table from eda-proposers, a screen that synthesises 4 spread designs, fits a log-linear cell-count model, and then synthesises designs in order of predicted Pareto rank finds 0.990 of the true front’s hypervolume at 12 syntheses (random 0.860, TPE 0.878; 100 seeds) and the whole front on 87% of seeds by 20. The screen reads cycles and error for free, which the baselines do not: with no cell-count model that alone gives 0.908, so the surrogate adds the rest. Stylised: one small smooth space, the surrogate fitted inside one family, no place and route.

How much error in a cheap objective can design-search proposers tolerate before they stop beating random search?

research/eda-noisy · 5 tests

With Gaussian error on the objectives a proposer sees, TPE's budget-30 lead over random falls from +0.037 to +0.006 (gone at error 0.8 of the objective range), NSGA-II and the genetic method lose theirs by 0.1, annealing is never better (stylised, synthetic noise, one 80-design space).

Abstract

The proposers of eda-proposers are shown objectives with Gaussian error (sigma 0 to 0.8 of each objective’s range, fixed per design) and scored on the true objectives, 100 paired seeds. At budget 30 TPE’s gain over random falls from +0.037 to +0.022 (sigma 0.2), +0.013 (0.4) and +0.006 (0.8, interval includes 0). NSGA-II and the genetic method are no better than random from sigma 0.1; annealing is worse than random at every sigma. Stylised: synthetic independent noise, one 80-design table, generic-gate cell counts, no place and route.

Does the ranking of design-search proposers survive a change of hypervolume reference point or quality indicator?

research/eda-refpoint · 6 tests

TPE stays best under five hypervolume reference points, recall, IGD+ and epsilon, but its lead over random at budget 30 ranges from +0.045 to +0.002 with the reference point (stylised, one 80-design space).

Abstract

The 100-seed proposer runs of eda-proposers re-scored under five hypervolume reference points, front recall, IGD+, an epsilon indicator and log-scaled cell counts. The winner (TPE at budgets 16 and 30) is the same under all of them, but its lead over random at budget 30 shrinks from +0.045 (reference 1.01) to +0.002 (reference 5.0), while recall shows +0.279. At budget 8 the order is decided by annealing and flips with the indicator. Stylised: one 80-design space, generic-gate counts, no place and route.

How much of a surrogate screen’s lead over random search comes from the free objectives?

research/eda-cheapaware · 8 tests

A random search that uses the free cycles/error objectives reaches 0.901 of the true front's hypervolume at 12 syntheses (random 0.860, screen 0.990), so +0.04 of the screen's lead is the free objectives and +0.09 the cell-count surrogate (stylised, one 80-design space).

Abstract

A follow-up to eda-screen on the same 80-design accelerator table. A random search that uses the free cycles and error objectives (no cell-count model) finds 0.901 of the true front’s hypervolume at 12 syntheses, against 0.860 for plain random, 0.878 for TPE and 0.990 for the 4-fit screen (100 seeds). So +0.041 of the screen’s +0.130 lead comes from the free objectives and +0.089 from the surrogate. The baseline stalls once the free-objective layer is spent and falls below random and TPE by 30 syntheses. Stylised: one 80-design space, generic-gate cell counts, no place and route.

Can a cost model trained on other chip-design families screen a new family’s Pareto front with a handful of syntheses?

research/eda-screenxfer · 7 tests

A Pareto screen whose cell-count model was trained only on other design families reaches 0.94 to 0.98 of the true front's hypervolume with 3 Yosys syntheses (random about 0.65), though the model's absolute error is large; ranking is what matters (stylised, MAC and FIR spaces of 27 designs).

Abstract

A follow-up to eda-screen and eda-adapt on 100 sampled 27-design spaces of two families (MAC engine, FIR filter). A screen whose cell-count model was trained only on the other families reaches 0.94 to 0.98 of the true front’s hypervolume with 3 Yosys syntheses and about 1.00 by 8, against 0.83 to 0.85 for random search at 8. The cost model’s absolute error is large (median 22–222% for ridge), but its ranking is what the screen uses; refitting on the screen’s own syntheses adds little, and spending the first syntheses on an adaptation spread hurts at tiny budgets. Stylised: tiny fronts (3–5 designs), generic-cell counts, one free objective, no place and route.

Does the best ABC recipe for one design transfer to another?

research/eda-flowtune · 11 tests

All 400 ABC pass sequences on eight small SKY130-mapped designs: one design's best recipe scores a mean 1.31× the other's optimum, and a transferred warm start helps only one design (stylised, toy designs, no place and route).

Abstract

All 400 ABC pass sequences (up to three passes of seven) mapped to SKY130 on eight small datapath designs, so every optimum is known. The best recipe differs per design and transfers badly: one design’s best recipe scores a mean 1.31× (worst 2.06×) the other’s optimum, and score rankings of recipes correlate only weakly across designs (Spearman −0.51 to 0.46). A leave-one-out consensus recipe is only slightly better than a median sequence (1.22× against 1.26×), and warm-starting a hill climber with it helps one design (mac4) but is within noise of random search on average at budgets 10 to 40. Stylised: eight hand-picked toy designs, ABC’s static delay, no place and route; the OpenROAD-settings half of the milestone is not done.

How many injected RTL bugs do random, coverage-directed and formal checks catch, and at what cost?

research/eda-verify · 5 tests

Mutation study of random, coverage-directed and bounded-formal RTL checks on the MAC accelerator: 64 random vectors kill 99% of killable mutants, formal 71% (timeouts), at ~50x the CPU cost (stylised).

Abstract

Mutation study on the MAC accelerator: 91 compilable mutants (operator, literal, deletion and ROM edits), of which 66 are killable and 16 are proved equivalent by induction. 64 random vectors kill 99% of the killable ones, 16 coverage-directed vectors 97% (16 random: 95%), and bounded Yosys formal checking 71% because 19 SAT runs timed out. Cost per kill: about 1 s for simulation against 52 s for a formal kill. Stylised: one design, one bound, one solver.

In what order should an automated design loop run random, directed and formal checks?

research/eda-checkorder · 5 tests

Replays the eda-verify mutation log to cost check cascades: random simulation before formal cuts CPU by 54% with more kills; the order among cheap stages barely matters (stylised re-analysis).

Abstract

Replay of the eda-verify mutation log (91 mutants, 20 seeds), costing each check cascade in CPU seconds. Formal alone takes 16,354 s for 47 kills and 16 equivalence proofs; random simulation first and formal only on the survivors takes about 7,500 s and finds 66 of 66 killable mutants, a 54% saving. The order among the cheap stages changes the total by under 2%; the remaining cost is formal working on equivalent or timed-out mutants, which no ordering removes. Stylised: a re-analysis of one design, one solver and its timeouts; nothing new was simulated.

When can a check cascade stop formal verification early without losing kills?

research/eda-earlystop · 7 tests

Replays the eda-verify log with a time cap on formal: a 170 s cap loses no kill and saves 41% of cascade CPU but forfeits every equivalence proof; a 230 s cap keeps 15 of 16 proofs and saves 23% (stylised re-analysis).

Abstract

Replay of the eda-verify mutation log with a time cap on the formal stage. Every formal kill in the log finished within 164 s, while equivalence proofs take about 222 s, so a 170 s cap loses no kill (66 of 66 killable mutants still found) and saves 41% of the cascade's CPU, but gives up all 16 equivalence proofs; a 230 s cap keeps 15 of them and saves 23%. Caps of 30 s or less start losing kills. A cap learned from half the mutants worked on the other half when the training half contained formal kills and failed (1.6 of 29 kills lost) when it contained none. Stylised: one design, one solver, 91 mutants; nothing new was simulated.

Can cheap features say which simulation survivors formal can prove equivalent?

research/eda-equivpredict · 8 tests

Predicts from the mutated source line which simulation survivors formal can prove equivalent: nearest-line prediction cuts formal CPU 55% and finds 14 of 16 proofs on one design (stylised, 27 mutants).

Abstract

Replay of the eda-verify log on the 27 mutants that 16 directed vectors do not kill (16 provably equivalent, 9 timeouts, 2 formal kills). Predicting from the mutated source line and running formal only on predicted-equivalent survivors costs 3,298 CPU-s against 7,414 s for always running formal (−55%) and still finds 14 of 16 proofs, 93% accurate under leave-one-line-out; smoother rules (3-NN, a one-split rule) find 12 of 16 and operator kind is no better than guessing. The signal is the design region the solver finds hard, not a transferable rule. Stylised: 27 mutants, one design, one solver; nothing new was simulated.

How sure is the 93% nearest-line equivalence predictor?

research/eda-equivinterval · 5 tests

Puts a line-clustered interval on eda-equivpredict's 93%: 25 of 27 survivors right, 95% CI 76.5–100% over 11 distinct lines, permutation p = 0.012 (stylised, one design).

Abstract

Uncertainty for eda-equivpredict, replayed from the same eda-verify log. The 27 survivors sit on only 11 distinct source lines, so a line-cluster bootstrap gives a 95% interval of 76.5–100% around the observed 25 of 27 (92.6%), against a 59.3% majority-class baseline, and a permutation test that shuffles line groups gives p = 0.012. One design, one solver, predictors chosen with the log in view. Stylised.

Does "the mutant synthesises to the golden netlist" predict formal equivalence on a second design family?

research/eda-equivxfer · 4 tests

Tests whether an identical post-synthesis netlist predicts formal equivalence on a second design (systolic array) as well as the MAC engine: right 3 of 3 times but finds only 3 of 28 proved equivalents (stylised, two designs).

Abstract

Follow-up to eda-conefeat on a design the idea was not fit on: a 2×4 systolic array from chip-systolic is mutated, 32 of 143 compiling mutants survive random simulation, and Yosys tries to prove them equivalent (12 proved, 20 timeouts at 60 s/120 s, none killed). An identical post-synthesis cell histogram is always right (3 of 3 across this design and the MAC engine) but finds only 3 of the 28 proved equivalents, so it saves about 3% of formal CPU; equivalent mutants usually change the netlist by −75 to +131 cells, and a zero change is as common among timeouts as among proofs. Two designs, short timeouts on the new one. Stylised.

Do synthesized-netlist features predict which simulation survivors Yosys can prove equivalent?

research/eda-conefeat · 3 tests

Tests whether Yosys netlist features (cell-count change, miter size) predict which eda-verify survivors are provably equivalent: none beats the 59.3% majority class under leave-one-line-out, against 92.6% for source line (negative, stylised, one design).

Abstract

Follow-up to eda-equivpredict on the same 27 survivors, now with Yosys installed. Position-free features (cell-count change after synthesis, miter size, identical cell histogram) are scored under leave-one-line-out. None beats guessing the majority class (16 of 27, 59.3%); the source-line rule gets 25 of 27. The nine solver timeouts barely change the netlist (|Δcells| 0 for six), but equivalent mutants span 0 to 178, and miter size is nearly constant (1,188–1,199). A negative result on one design; cone depth and fan-in were not tried (see eda-conehash).

Do fan-in cone features of a mutant predict which simulation survivors Yosys can prove equivalent?

research/eda-conehash · 5 tests

Fan-in cone features (changed sinks, cone gates and depth, registers reached) from hashed gate-level netlists vs which eda-verify survivors are provably equivalent: none beats the 59.3% majority class under leave-one-line-out (best 13 of 27; negative, stylised, one design).

Abstract

The next step eda-conefeat named. Golden and mutant are lowered to gate level without ABC, and each flip-flop and output is compared by a Merkle hash of its fan-in cone. Features of the changed cones (count, gates, depth, leaves, registers reached downstream) are scored under leave-one-line-out on the same 27 survivors. None beats the 59.3% majority class (best 13 of 27). One observation, found after looking at the table: all seven survivors that change exactly one sink are literal edits on two lines, and none is provably equivalent (six of the nine solver timeouts are among them). One design, one solver, stylised.

Do 20,000 random vectors catch any RTL bug that 64 missed, and do they contradict the formal equivalence proofs?

research/eda-deepdraw · 4 tests

20,000 further random vectors catch none of the 25 eda-verify survivors, so none contradicts a formal equivalence proof; a random-visible bug among the 9 formal timeouts has per-vector detection probability below 1.4e-4.

Abstract

A deep random draw on the 26 eda-verify mutants that 64-vector random testing never caught or flagged oddly (16 proved equivalent, 10 formal timeouts). 20,000 further uniform vectors catch none of the 25 survivors, so none contradicts a formal equivalence proof, and a random-visible bug among the 9 undecided mutants has per-vector detection probability below 1.4×10−4 (95%), sixteen times tighter than before. The one apparent failure, del@122, again fails exactly 10 vectors short of the draw, consistent with truncated output rather than a wrong value (not confirmed). Stylised: one design, uniform random inputs, same vector stream for every mutant.

How many random vectors does it take to see an RTL bug, and what can 1,280 clean vectors not rule out?

research/eda-detectprob · 7 tests

Per-vector detection probabilities of the eda-verify mutants: a geometric model fits the random-vector miss rate within 0.005, predicts 45 vectors for a 1% miss, and 1,280 clean vectors cannot rule out a bug rarer than 0.0023 per vector (stylised re-analysis).

Abstract

Detection-probability model of the eda-verify mutation log (91 mutants, 20 seeds). Random vectors catch 41 mutants on the first vector, 24 with a per-vector probability between 0.03 and 0.95, and never catch 25 (16 provably equivalent, 9 formal timeouts). A geometric first-failure model fits the observed miss rate within 0.005 at every budget up to 64 vectors, and predicts 45 vectors for a 1% miss rate and 118 for 0.1% (extrapolated beyond the data). The 1,280 clean vectors bound an undetected bug's per-vector probability below 0.0023 at 95%, so random testing cannot tell a rare bug from an equivalent mutant. Stylised: one design, 91 mutants, a tail set by 2–3 mutants; nothing new was simulated.

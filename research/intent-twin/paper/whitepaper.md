# Goal inference tuned against a simulated human: exact real error of a commit threshold set for a twin human of the wrong rationality

*Stylised: a two-goal sequential test on binary motion cues with a fixed cue accuracy per human. Pure Python; every number is from `experiments/results.txt` (seeded, seconds). No human data. Negative and loose results are reported as such.*

## Question
A collaborative robot is often trained and tuned against a simulated human whose noisiness ("rationality") is set by hand or fitted to a few demonstrations. If the robot commits to a goal as soon as its evidence crosses a threshold chosen to give error `α` against the simulated human, what error does it have against a real human whose cue accuracy differs, how much time does a cautious twin cost, what do a heterogeneous population and a finite calibration set do?

## Model
Two goals, equal prior. Each step the human's cue points at the true goal with probability `p` (odds `r=p/(1−p)`), else at the other goal. The robot tracks the lead `S` (cues for A minus cues for B) and commits to the leader at `|S|=h`; this is Wald's sequential probability ratio test, with log-likelihood ratio `S ln r_s` under the twin's cue accuracy `p_s`, so the twin designs `h(p_s,α)` = smallest integer with `1/(1+r_s^h) ≤ α`. Against a real human of accuracy `p`, gambler's ruin gives the exact wrong-goal probability `e(p,h)=1/(1+r^h)` and expected commitment time `E[T]=h(1−2e)/(2p−1)` (`h²` at `p=½`). For large `h`, `e ≈ r^{−h}`, so a twin-designed error `α` becomes `α^θ` with `θ = ln r / ln r_s` (an order-of-magnitude law: integer `h` rounds the twin's error below `α`). Cue accuracy across humans is modelled as `Beta(a,b)`.

## Results
1. **Closed forms hold** (60,000 trials): error 0.03263 / 0.03293 (p=0.7, h=4, exact / simulated), 0.00616 / 0.00680 (h=6), 0.03755 / 0.03800 (p=0.6, h=8), 0.00137 / 0.00138 (p=0.9, h=3); `E[T]` 9.347 / 9.357, 14.815 / 14.767, 36.996 / 36.959, 3.740 / 3.744; at `p=½` the error is 0.5 and `E[T]=25.0` (simulated 24.99) for `h=5`.
2. **A twin human that is too rational is optimistic.** Designed for `α=10⁻³` with `p_s=0.9` (`h=4`, twin error 1.5·10⁻⁴), a real human of accuracy 0.8 / 0.7 / 0.6 gives error 3.9·10⁻³ / 3.3·10⁻² / 0.165, i.e. 3.9× / 33× / 165× the target (simulation, 200,000 trials, at 0.7 and 0.6: 0.0331 and 0.1637 against exact 0.0326 and 0.1650). The same table for `p_s=0.8` (`h=5`) gives 14× at `p=0.7` and 116× at `p=0.6`; for `p_s=0.7` (`h=9`) 25× at `p=0.6`. The `α^θ` law gives 1.3·10⁻² / 7.0·10⁻² / 0.28 for the first row: it has the right shape but overshoots the exact value by 2–3× because of integer rounding.
3. **A twin that is too irrational is safe but slow.** With `p_s=0.6` (`h=18`, twin `E[T]` 89.9), real humans of accuracy 0.7 / 0.8 / 0.9 see error 2.4·10⁻⁷ / 1.5·10⁻¹¹ / 7·10⁻¹⁸ and commit 2.0× / 3.6× / 4.5× more slowly than the best threshold for their own accuracy (45.0 vs 22.5, 30.0 vs 8.3, 22.5 vs 5.0 cues).
4. **Heterogeneous humans** (`α=0.05`, twin built for the mean accuracy): with `Beta(160,40)` and `Beta(48,12)` (mean 0.8, sd 0.028 / 0.051) the mean-twin threshold `h=3` gives population error 0.0171 / 0.0214 against the twin's 0.0154 (still under target). With `Beta(21,9)` and `Beta(14,6)` (mean 0.7, sd 0.082 / 0.100) it gives 0.0703 / 0.0881 against a twin error 0.0326, so `h` must rise from 4 to 6 / 8 and `E[T]` from 9.3 to 16.4 / 24.2. Humans with `p<½` mislead the robot whatever `h` is: the population error cannot fall below `P(p<½)` = 0.0121 / 0.0318 here, so a target of 0.01 is unreachable for `Beta(21,9)`.
5. **Calibrating the twin from `n` real cues** (`p=0.7`, `α=0.01`, exact over Binomial(`n`,0.7), point estimate `(k+1)/(n+2)`, threshold capped at 200): the known-`p` threshold is `h=6` (error 0.0062). The probability that the designed threshold misses the target is 0.38 / 0.42 / 0.33 / 0.30 / 0.14 at `n` = 10 / 20 / 50 / 200 / 1000, although the mean real error is under target for `n≥50` (0.0099, 0.0078, 0.0069): the average hides the misses. Using the lower 95% confidence bound of `p` cuts the miss probability to 0.028 / 0.036 / 0.040 / 0.017 / 0.0036 and costs time: mean `E[T]` 339 / 262 / 106 / 21.8 / 16.9 against 90.5 / 43.2 / 19.0 / 15.2 / 14.7 (means are dominated by the few calibration sets that give `p̂` near ½ and hit the cap). Only `n≈200` or more is close to the known-`p` design.

## Limitations
Binary cues, a fixed accuracy per human, equal prior, i.i.d. cues (real humans have memory, intent changes and non-stationary noise), a symmetric two-goal task, and error counted as committing to the wrong goal with no cost for time or for an early wrong action. The `Beta` populations are assumed, not fitted. The plug-in and confidence-bound calibration rules are simple choices; no optimal calibration rule is claimed. The `α^θ` law is loose (results 2). The test is classical SPRT; the contribution is the exact real-human consequences of tuning it in a twin. No human-subject data.

## Next steps
More than two goals and continuous trajectories; Boltzmann-rational cues with a cost-dependent accuracy; learning `p_s` online during deployment with an e-process guarantee; a robot that infers the human's accuracy jointly with the goal; costs for wrong early actions; connect to explainable-planner and handover twins in this repository.

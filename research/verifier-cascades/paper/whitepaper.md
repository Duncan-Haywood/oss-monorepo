# Verifier cascades: what sequential, publicly visible verdicts cost a verification market

*Duncan Haywood. MIT licence. Code and experiments: `../src`, `../tests`, `../experiments`.*

## Abstract
Refereed and market-based verification of decentralised ML work (`verification-game`, `market-routing`, `lmsr-fees`) usually lets verifiers see one another's verdicts, which is the cheapest way to aggregate them. If verifiers are paid for being right and are Bayesian, this is exactly the Bikhchandani–Hirshleifer–Welch (1992) herding setting. I work out what it costs a verification market. (i) With private-signal accuracy `a` and a fair-coin fault state, a cascade forms once two more revealed signals point one way than the other; the cascade is on the wrong verdict with probability exactly `(1−a)²/(a²+(1−a)²)` and forms after `2/(a²+(1−a)²)` revealed signals on average (`a=0.7`: 0.155 and 3.45). (ii) The herd's accuracy is therefore capped at `a²/(a²+(1−a)²)` (0.845) for any number of verifiers, while an independent majority of 101 is right ≈100% of the time; the 30th sequential verdict is 0.845 versus 0.988 for a 30-verifier majority. (iii) A sealed (commit–reveal) first batch of `m` verifiers, followed by open sequential verdicts, lifts the cap: `m=3, 5, 11, 21` give 0.875, 0.901, 0.949, 0.982 at `a=0.7`, more accurate than a majority of the same (rounded-up) size at every `m` tested, and `m=0,1,2` are identical to pure sequential play. (iv) If the designer stops asking once a cascade is visible, sequential play is cost-efficient (0.845 for 3.45 paid verifiers, between a 3- and a 5-majority) but frozen: stopping is where the ceiling bites. The results are exact recursions checked against a Monte-Carlo of fully Bayesian agents that assumes nothing about the walk structure.

## 1. Model
A fair-coin fault state; each verifier receives an independent private signal correct with probability `a>½`, observes all earlier public verdicts, and reports the verdict maximising the probability of being right (payment for a correct verdict, any proper score reduces to it); ties go to the own signal. Track `d` = revealed correct minus revealed wrong signals (unknown to observers in sign; they see the verdict tally). If `|d|≤1` the verdict equals the own signal (at `|d|=1` an opposing signal gives a tie), so it reveals the signal and `d` moves by ±1; at `|d|≥2` both signals give the same verdict, so the verdict reveals nothing and every later verifier copies it. `bayes_run` derives verdicts from Bayes' rule with public log-odds counted exactly in units of `ln(a/(1−a))`; it does not use the walk.

## 2. Exact results
Two consecutive revealed signals starting from `d=0` end at `+2` w.p. `a²`, `−2` w.p. `(1−a)²`, and back at `0` w.p. `2a(1−a)`. So the cascade is wrong w.p. `(1−a)²/(a²+(1−a)²)`, and the expected number of revealed signals is `2/(a²+(1−a)²)` (`agents_used` sums the live probabilities and matches). Wrong-cascade probability: 0.401, 0.308, 0.225, 0.155, 0.059, 0.012 at `a` = 0.55, 0.6, 0.65, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9; near ½ it is `½−2(a−½)`, so herding throws away almost everything a weak verifier knows. The 1st, 2nd and 3rd verdicts have accuracy `a, a, a²(3−2a)` (0.700, 0.700, 0.784), identical to a majority of 1, 1(2), 3; from the fourth they lag: 0.819 at n=6 versus 0.837 (E2), 0.840 versus 0.901 at n=10.

## 3. Sealed batches
A batch of `m` simultaneous sealed verdicts reveals `m` signals at once with tally `d=2k−m` (`k` correct); `|d|≥2` starts a cascade immediately, `|d|≤1` continues the walk. `m=2` reproduces pure sequential play exactly (first two signals) and the accuracy grows with `m` (E3): at `a=0.7`, 0.845 (m≤2), 0.875, 0.901, 0.921, 0.949, 0.982, 0.9975 for m = 3, 5, 7, 11, 21, 41, at 4.5, 6.1, 7.8, 11.5, 21.1, 41.0 expected paid verifiers, each above the majority of the odd number just above the paid count. At `a=0.6`, `m=21` gives 0.846 versus the cap 0.692. A finite batch still leaves a residual wrong-cascade probability (the batch tally is wrong, or near zero and the walk restarts), but it shrinks fast in `m`.

## 4. Simulation check
100 000 markets of six fully Bayesian verifiers at `a=0.7`: wrong final verdict 0.1803 (Monte Carlo) versus 0.1807 (recursion); the same at `m=3, n=5` and every position `n≤6` within 0.012 (tests). An earlier float-odds implementation broke ties incorrectly (`lr·(1/lr)≠1`) and disagreed with the recursion by up to 0.055; log-odds counted in integer units fixed it, and the test now catches the mismatch.

## 5. Limits and implications
Equal, known accuracy; fair prior; i.i.d. symmetric signals; risk-neutral verifiers paid only for correctness; verdicts public and immediate. Heterogeneous or private accuracies, verifiers who value being contrarian, and a designer who pays for signals (peer prediction, `peer-prediction-effort`) or scores reports rather than verdicts all change the picture; a payment that rewards revealing the signal rather than the verdict is the natural fix and untreated here. The practical reading for a refereed network is that open, sequential re-execution votes are cheap and cap accuracy near the cascade bound, so at least the first three verifiers should be sealed, and unanimity after two like verdicts should not be taken as evidence. Related: Banerjee (1992), Bikhchandani–Hirshleifer–Welch (1992) for the cascade model, Frongillo's and Waggoner's work on eliciting information from strategic agents. Synthetic.

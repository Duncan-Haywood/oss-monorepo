# What does a 1:N model of a cargo UAV tell you about the 1:1 aircraft?

**Status: open research programme, milestones 1 to 11bk done in part (see below).** A stdlib Froude-scaling calculator
(`src/aerolog_scaling/`, 9 tests) and a first-order result: for a 1,000 kg, aspect-ratio-10 cargo
fixed-wing, the 1:10 model needs **1.27x** the power that Froude scaling predicts (1.17 to 1.35
over drag and aspect-ratio sweeps) because Reynolds number falls as n^1.5 and skin friction rises.
Flat-plate drag only: preliminary, and the proposal's hypotheses H1 to H5 are still open. See
[`PROPOSAL.md`](PROPOSAL.md) and [`LOG.md`](LOG.md).

**Live page:** https://marisstella.app/research/aerolog-scaling/

**Programme.** Track 22 (`aerolog`, autonomous flying machines for commercial logistics) in
[`../PROJECTS.md`](../PROJECTS.md), at both sizes: scale models and the full-size aircraft. This
directory asks which quantities a dynamically scaled model reproduces and which it misses.

## Method and reproduce

```bash
cd research/aerolog-scaling
PYTHONPATH=src python3 -m pytest tests -q          # 9 tests, under 1 s
PYTHONPATH=src python3 experiments/run.py > experiments/results.txt
```

`scaling.py` holds the ISA atmosphere, Sutherland viscosity, the Froude exponent table, Schlichting
flat-plate skin friction (laminar, turbulent, mixed), a drag-polar cruise point and a Breguet-type
battery fraction. Results: [`experiments/results.txt`](experiments/results.txt).

## Results (preliminary)

| n | Re (mac) | L/D | power / (n^3.5 x full), tripped |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3.1e6 | 22.8 | 1.00 |
| 0.2 | 2.7e5 | 19.7 | 1.16 |
| 0.1 | 9.7e4 | 18.0 | 1.27 |
| 0.05 | 3.4e4 | 16.1 | 1.42 |

An untripped model with attached laminar flow looks *better* than full scale near Re 3e5, which real
wings do not do (separation); that is a limit of the flat-plate model, not a finding.
Range scaled with length also means a model at fixed cell specific energy has much more battery
margin per body length (0.13x at n = 0.1), so scaled endurance does not test the full-size energy budget.

**Airfoil polars (milestone 4a, `experiments/airfoil_polars/`).** With NeuralFoil polars for a NACA 4412
in place of the flat plate, the 1:10 model needs 1.29 to 1.45 times the Froude-predicted power
(three boundary-layer states) and loses 0.37 to 0.52 of CLmax; both support H2 and H3 for a clean 2D
section. At 1:5 the power ratio is 1.11 to 1.19. The surrogate is unvalidated here and not usable at 1:20.

**Longitudinal modes (milestone 3a, `experiments/longitudinal_modes/`).** A 4-state longitudinal model with
NeuralFoil polars at each surface's own Reynolds number. For a tripped boundary layer at 1:10, the model flown
at the full-size trim angle trims 6.5% faster in Froude units (H1 refuted: bar 5%) and its short-period
frequency rises 5.1%; flown at the full-size CL, speed is matched and the frequencies stay within 1.3%, but
short-period damping falls 6.3%. With free transition at 1:10 (Re 1e5) the short period turns overdamped, a
laminar-bubble regime the surrogate is not trusted in. Phugoid damping is not reported: it is unstable even
at full size in this model and depends on the thrust law (milestones 5c, 5d: real part of the full-size phugoid +0.0127 at constant thrust, +0.0087 at constant power, +0.0037 at the measured fixed-RPM slope; more damped by 0.0065 to 0.0083 at n = 0.2). `experiments/thrust_phugoid_power/`. Gust response (milestones 9a, 9b): the similar model keeps the peak load-factor increment within 2% but is 27 to 33% high in peak pitch rate; with the tail redesigned, 24 of the 78 mode-matched designs match all three gust peaks within 5% at all three gust lengths (best 1.2%). Milestone 9c: that match does not survive a tail slope 0.75 to 1.0 of the surrogate's; none of the 78 keeps the gust peaks within 10% at every scale (median worst error 21.5% at 0.75), and the gust metrics are more sensitive than the modes.

**Propeller efficiency (milestone 5a, `experiments/propeller_efficiency/`).** From 151 measured efficiency
curves of 34 APC propellers in the UIUC Propeller Database (Re at 0.75 R from 1.5e4 to 1.2e5), peak efficiency
rises with blade Reynolds number in 97% of propellers (mean 0.21 per decade of Re, bootstrap CI 0.18 to 0.23).
Across all curves, mean peak efficiency is 0.603 at Re 2e4 to 4e4 and 0.669 at 8e4 to 1.6e5. Saturating fits
(Re^-0.5 and Re^-0.2) put the fall from Re 1e5 to 3.2e4 (inside the data) at 9.9 and 10.3 points, so the
direction of H4 is supported and 10 points is about the size of the effect at n = 0.1 for a model whose
propeller sits near Re 1e5. The full-size side (Re about 1e6, an assumed figure) is outside the database, so
the n = 0.1 gap there (19 to 25 points from the fits, 31 from a straight line) is an extrapolation and is not reported as a result.
Re is confounded with RPM within a propeller (advance ratio at peak and tip Mach move too).
Data: Brandt and Selig, "Propeller Performance Data at Low Reynolds Numbers", AIAA 2011-1255; UIUC Propeller Data Site, https://m-selig.ae.illinois.edu/props/propDB.html.

**Propeller thrust law (milestone 5b, `experiments/propeller_thrust_slope/`).** At fixed RPM the thrust-speed slope is dlnCT/dlnJ; from 137 measured APC curves it is -2.25 at peak efficiency (5th/95th percentile -2.86 / -1.54) and shows no detectable Reynolds dependence within propellers (+0.23 per decade, 95% interval [-0.24, 0.73]). Preliminary: not yet in the mode model.

**Measured electrical power against mass (milestone 6a, `experiments/rodrigues_energy/`).** On 134 cruise
flights of a DJI Matrice 100 (Rodrigues et al., *Patterns* 2022; data CC BY 4.0, Figshare
10.1184/R1/12683453.v1), electrical power scales with takeoff mass to the power **1.54** (95% bootstrap interval
over flights 1.41 to 1.67, speed and wind as covariates), consistent with the m^1.5 of rotor momentum theory
that Froude scaling turns into n^3.5. Electrical cruise power is 1.85 times the ideal induced power at every
payload (0, 250, 500 g), and falls 10% from 4 to 12 m/s (translational lift). This is a check inside one
airframe over a 13.6% mass range, not across scale; payload is partly confounded with flight day (density not
logged) and the interval resamples flights, not days. Data: Rodrigues et al., doi:10.1184/R1/12683453.v1.

**Correction search (milestone 7a, `experiments/correction_search/`).** At n = 0.2 the uncorrected model (same NACA 4412)
needs 1.157x the Froude power (free transition; 1.383 tripped). A search of 178 section and boundary-layer cases
(158 NACA 4-digit, 10 low-Reynolds sections, free or tripped, CL within +/-6%, lift-curve slope within 5%) finds none
within 5%: the best is **1.086**, a thin cambered section left untripped. H5 is refuted in this design space, which
holds geometric similarity; the polars are NeuralFoil, not yet checked against measured data.

**Non-similar corrections (milestone 7b, `experiments/nonsimilar_search/`).** Allowing wing area and aspect ratio to
depart from similarity reverses 7a: 17 of 144 (section, area factor, aspect ratio) cases match Froude power within 5%
at n = 0.2 (NACA 4412 with 1.3x the similar area: 0.996). Most do so by running 17 to 33% lower CL; only two also keep CL
within 6% and the finite-wing lift slope within 5% (e.g. NACA 5406, AR 12: 0.967). These are power matches at the trim
point, not corrections of the physics, and the polars are still NeuralFoil, unchecked against measured data.

**Do the matches keep the flight dynamics? (milestone 7c, `experiments/nonsimilar_modes/`).** No. Putting the 17 power
matches through the longitudinal-mode model, none keeps phugoid frequency, short-period frequency and damping within 5%
of the full-size aircraft (best worst-case deviation 15.5%); the two that passed the 7b proxies sit at +18% short-period
frequency and about -10% damping. The shift is the same in every case, including the uncorrected similar model
(+15%): it comes from the tail, whose Reynolds number is 1.7e5 at n = 0.2, not from the wing. So wing-only corrections
fix power but not the short period; the next step searches the tail (7d). NeuralFoil slope at that Re is unchecked.

**Energy per package against a diesel truck (milestone 2a, `experiments/energy_per_package/`).** On the same 134 Matrice 100
cruise flights, electrical energy per metre falls steeply with speed: 127 J/m at 4 m/s, 63 at 8, 48 at 10 (bootstrap CIs over
flights in `results.txt`). Costing a round trip (loaded out, empty back) at 8 and 10 m/s and comparing it with Stolaroff et al.'s
(2018) diesel truck (7.8 MJ per package, 151 packages per 100 miles), the drone uses less energy than the truck out to a
**one-way distance of 63 km at 8 m/s and 83 km at 10 m/s at Stolaroff's stop density** (31 and 41 km at twice the density, 16 and 21 km at four times). This counts
cruise only (no takeoff, landing or hover), so it flatters the drone, and the M100's endurance is far shorter than these distances:
range, not energy per package, is what limits a battery multicopter, as Stolaroff et al. found (about 4 km). The
pooled mean (86 J/m, 2.7 times Stolaroff's modelled 32 J/m for a different quadcopter) is not a result: 41 of 134 flights are at
4 m/s. Prediction 1 (50 to 80 J/m pooled) failed for that reason.

**Whole-flight energy and battery range (milestone 2b, `experiments/mission_energy/`).** Integrating battery power over
the full 134 route flights, **77% of the energy is not cruise** (climb, hover, acceleration; 95% at 8 m/s) because the legs
are short, and whole-flight energy is 154 J/m against 60 J/m cruise-only at 8 m/s. One TB48D pack (129.96 Wh, retailer
figure) at 80% usable then gives only about **1 km one way (regression) to 2 to 3 km (cruise-only slope)**, so 2a's
63 to 83 km break-even is not reachable by this airframe: battery energy is the binding limit. Extrapolates from
cruise windows under 150 m; pack count in the study not checked.

**Altitude and payload in the fixed energy (milestone 2c, `experiments/altitude_energy/`).** The flights span four altitudes (25 to 100 m) and four payload codes, not two as planned (the plan was wrong; see `LOG.md`). A pooled fit over 132 flights (R2 0.93) gives **9.6 kJ [8.9, 10.3] per extra 25 m of altitude** and 8.0 kJ [7.0, 8.9] per payload step, so 2b's E0 was an average over altitudes. The cruise slope stays unidentified (-13 to 103 J/m; cruise windows under 150 m), so no range is claimed beyond 2b's bracket. Preliminary; one airframe; altitude may be confounded with route and time of day.

**Hybrid with engine and tank mass (milestone 10e, `experiments/vtol_sizing/hybrid_mass_law.py`).** Adding engine+generator mass (swept 0.5 to 8 kW/kg, hover-sized) and tank mass to 10a's hybrid: at 1 kW/kg, chain efficiency 0.20 and non-energy fraction 0.479 it reaches 1,273 km (battery at 250 Wh/kg: 166 km) and beats the battery aircraft from 67 km (10a: 20 km). Engine specific power dominates: break-even is 145 to 167 km at 0.5 kW/kg and 4 to 7 km at 4 kW/kg. Inputs swept, not datasheet-fitted. Preliminary.

**Which resource binds first at a delivery-drone hub? (milestone 6, `experiments/hub_throughput/`).** A discrete-event model of landing pads, chargers and spare packs, checked against Erlang C (mean wait within 0.6% at utilisation 0.5 to 0.85). With a sourced 60 s battery swap (Matternet, vendor claim) and a 32-minute 20-to-90% charge (DJI Dock 2, vendor figure), swap raises the pad limit 8x (80 against 9.8 flights/h at 4 pads) but the chargers then bind at 10.7/h, so 4 chargers deliver only 1.09x the throughput of charging on the pad; chargers bound in every case run (4 to 8 pads, 4 to 16 chargers, 1 to 5 min load). Swapping moves the bottleneck from pad to charger and spare packs (about 3 times the charger count to stop queueing at 90% load). Poisson arrivals, linear charge time, small-multicopter figures, not a cargo VTOL. Preliminary.

**Measured charge taper, fresh and aged (milestone 6h, `experiments/hub_throughput/cargo_hub_aged_cells.py`).** On 543 measured CC-CV charges of four NASA Ames 18650 cells cycled to about 30% capacity fade (Saha and Goebel 2007; 0.75C to 4.2 V, then CV to C/100), charging 20 to 90% takes 1.19x the linear time when fresh and 1.60x when aged, and 30 to 100% takes 2.68x and 3.74x (6d's datasheet-fitted curve: 1.03x and 1.71x). Ageing moves the CV share of the charge from 26% to 41%. For 6d's hub at 6 flights/h with a 90% charge ceiling, spares rise from 9 (linear) to 12 (fresh) and 16 (aged); charging to 100% caps 8 chargers at 4.3 (fresh) and 3.1 (aged) flights/h, so the hub needs more chargers, not more packs. One old cell type at 0.75C, scaled to 1C as a ratio. Preliminary.

**Where does a battery cargo VTOL stop closing? (milestone 10a, `experiments/vtol_sizing/`).** A linear mass-budget model of a lift-plus-cruise VTOL (momentum-theory hover, L/D cruise, 20% energy reserve, payload at least 15% of MTOW). At 250 Wh/kg cells (L/D 12, disk loading 150 N/m2, structure 0.35 of MTOW) the one-way range is **about 200 km for a 100 kg payload**, rising 88 km per 100 Wh/kg of cell energy; 100 / 200 / 300 km need roughly 130 / 240 / 355 Wh/kg in that case and 100 to 210 / 165 to 390 / 240 to 575 Wh/kg across the L/D (10 to 16), disk-loading and structure-fraction sweep. Structure fraction and L/D move the answer most (range 154 to 227 km over structure 0.45 to 0.30). Prediction 1 narrowly missed (203 km against a 200 km bar); prediction 3 was wrong: with jet fuel at 11.9 kWh/kg a hybrid carries less mass than the pack at every range tried (20 km break-even at 250 Wh/kg), so in this model it closes to the 1,500 km search cap, which says the model lacks what limits real hybrids (tank and engine mass scaling, climb, certification), not that hybrids fly 1,500 km. Payload size barely matters above 25 kg because every mass is a fraction of MTOW: the size effect on structure is exactly what this model leaves out, and every airframe input is assumed and swept, not fitted to a published cargo VTOL. Preliminary.

**Does that sizing model match published VTOL weights? (milestone 10b, `experiments/nasa_concept_check/`).** Run against the two electric concept vehicles of Johnson, Silva and Solis (NASA, 2018): with one usable fraction (0.896, calibrated on the quadrotor) the energy model predicts the side-by-side helicopter's battery fraction to 0.3% (0.305 against 0.306), so the hover-plus-cruise-plus-reserve physics holds. The part that was wrong is the airframe: the published empty-weight-less-battery fractions are 0.58 and 0.48 of gross weight, not the 0.40 assumed in 10a, and they do not transfer (swapping them moves gross weight by +92% and -34%). With them, a 250 Wh/kg pack reaches 58 km (quadrotor) to 138 km (side-by-side) instead of 203 km. Two NDARC concept studies, not flown aircraft. Preliminary.

**Does it carry over to the fuel-burning designs? (milestone 10c, `experiments/nasa_fuel_check/`).** The same energy model applied to NASA's turboshaft side-by-side (3,831 lb) and tiltwing (11,654 lb) implies a specific fuel consumption of 1.07 and 1.20 times each engine's published MCP figure (0.616 and 0.591 lb/hp/h), a plausible part-power penalty, though reserve assumptions move the ratio between 1.01 and 1.31. Across all seven NASA designs (250 to 3,000 lb payload) the empty-weight-less-battery fraction stays between 0.48 and 0.62, so 10a's 0.40 was low for every one; the five fuel or generator designs span only 0.056, but the electric quadrotor (0.583) falls inside that band, so propulsion architecture alone does not explain it. Seven NDARC concept studies from one paper. Preliminary.

## Limitations

Flat-plate drag with a form factor, no airfoil polars, no CLmax, fixed powertrain efficiency (which
falls with scale, so the power ratio is optimistic). Airframe constants are design choices, swept
not sourced. The Froude exponents were checked against Table 4 of NASA TP-1435 (opened on NTRS, 2026-10-27;
the rows for velocity, time, angular rate, acceleration, mass and inertia, in `tests/`). Force, moment,
power, energy and Reynolds exponents are derived from those rows, not read from the table. Citations:
see "Citation status" in `PROPOSAL.md` (several ids and one author were wrong and are fixed).

**Paper (milestone 8a).** A first draft with the claims, a hypotheses table and limitations is in [`paper/draft.md`](paper/draft.md); `python3 paper/check_numbers.py` checks its numbers against the result files.

**Second paper: drone altitude-layer spacing (milestone 11 paper step, 2026-11-18).** [`paper/airspace_layers.md`](paper/airspace_layers.md) is draft v0 of a paper on **how far apart delivery-drone altitude layers should be**, written from milestones 11ah to 11an with no new simulation: with a 25 ft separation minimum and metre-scale altitude error, 5 two-level bands at 13.3 m fit under 400 ft, not 8 at 25 ft; the spacing rule is h - Z >= 3 sd of the altitude difference between two drones; and the sourced error to plan with is the differential one (barometer vs RTK about 1 m, hub-to-hub pressure about 0.5 m), not each drone's drift. Milestones 11ao to 11az (per-operator airframe speed offsets, cheap planners, the planner's cost and a leak-budget spacing rule) were added and, on 2026-11-22, folded into one results section (6.5 to 6.9) on the corrected spacing grid, with a table of every cheap planner against the full search: patching an offset-blind plan never beats it at the measured offset, any search with the offsets does, and a leak budget of a few per cent per adjacent pair picks the spacing, searched two band counts either side (11az; it holds on 24 fresh layouts, 11ba). Milestones 11bf to 11bh (a collision target per flight hour, a hold-until-clear rule for climbs and descents, and where to fly the hold) were folded on 2026-11-24 into one Section 6.10 with a figure of every collision term side by side: the spacing is ten orders of magnitude inside 1e-7, unmitigated transits three orders over it, and a hold rule on Remote ID-grade surveillance, hovered half a level off, brings both to the target. `python3 paper/check_airspace_numbers.py` ties its quoted numbers to the results files (357 checks), and `python3 paper/airspace_figures.py` draws its four figures ([band count](paper/figures/figA1_band_count.svg), [error terms](paper/figures/figA2_error_ladder.svg), [cheap planners](paper/figures/figA3_rule_costs.svg), [collision terms](paper/figures/figA4_collision_ledger.svg)). Preliminary; venue an aim (DASC or *Transportation Research Part C*).

## Next

**Milestone 6i** (`experiments/hub_throughput/analytic_spares.py`): a closed-form spare-pack rule (the M^X/D/c recursion for packs at the chargers, plus a Poisson count for packs in transit) gives the same smallest pack count as the hub simulator for at most 5% of orders waiting: 12, 16, 25 at 70% load and 17, 26, 45 at 85%, for single orders and batches of 2 and 4. It is optimistic within about 2 packs of the mean count, because waiting feeds back on itself. Batches of 8 at 8 pads add a pad-wait floor of about 5% that no spares remove.

**Milestone 6j** (`experiments/hub_throughput/analytic_wait_tail.py`): the same rule gives the whole waiting-time distribution. The charger wait is a Lindley recursion with Erlang gaps, and the pack pool is used in a fixed cycle. With that, the count for 6c's criterion (p95 wait <= 300 s) can be computed without a simulator. On a 1-pack grid the rule matches the simulator for single orders and batches of 2 and 4 at 70% load (11, 15, 24) and for single orders at 85% (16). In the other four cases it asks for 1 to 2 packs more (k = 8 at 70%: 41 against 39; at 85%: 25/24, 44/42, 81/79), so in the design region it errs toward over-provisioning. The fine grid also corrects 6c's k = 8 count at 70% load from 48 to 39.

**Milestone 6k** (`experiments/hub_throughput/analytic_trace_tail.py`): an exact time-varying version of the rule sizes the pack pool under a real daily demand profile (Chicago ride-hail shape, peak 1.76x the mean, above the charger limit), where stationary formulas have no answer. An order waits more than w iff at least S earlier packs are not ready at t + w. That count is the orders of the last D + T<sub>pad</sub> - w seconds plus the charger survivors max(N<sub>q</sub>(t + w - D) - c, 0), and N<sub>q</sub> follows a time-indexed recursion. Smallest pack count for p95 wait <= 300 s, rule against simulator: 23/23, 24/24 (70% load) and 43/43, 45/45 (85%) for single orders; 40/41, 42/43, 67/68, 68/68 for batches of 4. The stationary count at the mean rate (11, 16) is 12 to 29 packs short. Waits peak 4 hours after the evening demand peak. 6f's rounded-count arrival generator understated the need by 9 to 12 packs against Poisson-in-bin orders.

**Milestone 6l** (`experiments/hub_throughput/demand_variability.py`): with measured day-to-day and within-day demand variability (131 weekdays of Uber pickups in New York, Apr to Sep 2014, busiest 2.2 km cell; day-level CV 0.17 after trend and weekday, hourly CV 0.17), the hub needs **64 packs at 70% load and 111 at 85%** for p95 wait <= 300 s, against 47 and 71 for the mean day (6k's rule, which the simulator confirms for that model). A rule that averages 6k's exact calculation over single observed days is 2 and 21 packs short; run over three consecutive observed days (a post hoc diagnostic) it gives 63 and 108, because at 85% load a busy evening leaves on average 16 packs at the chargers the next morning. Even at the pooled count, about one weekday in eight misses the target on its own. Ride-hail, not delivery orders; preliminary. Figure: [`demand_variability_tail.svg`](experiments/hub_throughput/demand_variability_tail.svg). Data: FiveThirtyEight, uber-tlc-foil-response (NYC TLC FOIL), CC BY 4.0.

**Milestone 11i** (`experiments/hub_airspace/land_weights.py`): **does a land mask help a planner pick drone altitude layers without traffic data?** Spreading each operator's customers uniformly over New York City's land within its service disc (a 100 m mask from the city's shoreline-clipped borough boundaries, which hold 98.8% of the real destinations), instead of over the whole disc, brings the paper weight's split **within 5% of the closed form's in 17 of 24 layouts (13 without the mask)**, median excess 1.9% against 2.6%, worst 24% against 27%. It is better in 7 layouts and worse in 2 (sign test p = 0.18), and it **does not improve the ranking of which operator pairs conflict most** (r 0.964 against 0.967). Water is not most of the missing demand information; customer density on land is. Five of eight predictions refuted; swept radii, speeds and shares; preliminary. Results: [`land_weights_results.txt`](experiments/hub_airspace/land_weights_results.txt).

**Milestone 11k** (`experiments/hub_airspace/robust_selection.py`): **does a robust choice fix the paper split's misses?** 11j guessed that the census-weighted paper weights miss the best drone altitude-layer split because the max-cut sits on near-ties. It does not. Across 24 layouts the largest misses come where the paper weight's best split leads its runner-up by the most (Spearman +0.33, not negative), and **no hedging rule beats the plain argmax beyond noise**: voting over lognormal perturbations of the pair weights gives a mean excess of 2.9% against 3.3% (better in 2 layouts, worse in 2, sign test p = 1.0); CVaR and an ensemble of five paper weights are worse. The error is a bias in particular pair weights. The practical result: **the simulator's best split is in the residents-plus-jobs paper weight's top 5 in 21 of 24 layouts**, so ranking on paper and simulating five candidates gets almost all of the closed form's quality. Five of eight predictions refuted or held only by the letter; preliminary. Results: [`robust_selection_results.txt`](experiments/hub_airspace/robust_selection_results.txt).

**Milestone 11m** (`experiments/hub_airspace/operator_demand.py`): **which operator's census demand flips the drone layer split?** Usually one. In 5 of the 6 layouts where the census paper weight's split is more than 5% worse than the closed form's, replacing a single operator's census map with its real destinations fixes it, including both layouts every paper weight has missed since 11h (layout 0: 1.213 to 0.997; layout 17: 1.103 to 1.000). There one pair weight carries 81% and 100% of the decision error. Whole-disc summaries do not find the culprit (Spearman of pair error against leg-length or total-variation mismatch -0.15 and 0.19), and rescaling census maps by each operator's true mean leg length changes nothing (mean 1.032 against 1.033). Putting one operator's census map back into the real-demand weight breaks its split by more than 2% in 9 of 24 layouts. Four of eight predictions held (one only by the letter); preliminary. Results: [`operator_demand_results.txt`](experiments/hub_airspace/operator_demand_results.txt).

**Milestone 11q** (`experiments/hub_airspace/reference_rescore.py`): **do the drone layer-split rankings survive a much larger simulation reference?** Yes for the means, not for some single-layout claims. Re-scoring the splits each headline rule chose on five new 200,000-flight runs per layout (1,000,000 flights, a 95% interval per layout) moves every rule's mean by 0.3% or less and keeps their order: the real-map paper split 1.000 (its interval contains the closed form's split in all 24 layouts), census-plus-disclosure stopping 1.006 (one significant miss), census-only paper weights 1.031 (five significant misses). The best split on the single scoring run used since 11g was a winner's curse: 0.998 in-sample, 1.002 on the reference, worse than the closed form's in 7 of the 8 layouts where they differ. A split chosen on two reference runs and scored on the other three never beats the closed form's by more than 2%. Layout 16's 6.7% miss shrinks to 2.5% +- 2.6% and layout 22's 4.5% to 2.7%; layout 17's 5.4% +- 1.3% stands. Six of eight predictions held. Preliminary; same limits as 11n. Results: [`reference_rescore_results.txt`](experiments/hub_airspace/reference_rescore_results.txt).

**Milestone 11r** (`experiments/hub_airspace/exact_timing.py`): **can the drone conflict count be computed without simulating launch times, and is the closed form right?** Yes, and mostly. With Poisson launches and no interaction, the simulator's expected count is an average over pairs of flight paths of the length of the departure-offset interval that makes two legs meet, and that interval is exact in closed form (an ellipse cut by a box). The exact estimator matches the simulator (pooled ratio 1.0005 over 24 layouts; a fresh recount of the two outlying layouts gives 0.9999 and 0.9959), but it is only about 1.5x more efficient per CPU second, not 3x. It shows that 11d's closed form undercounts en-route conflicts by 7% (3% to 12%) while its pair weights correlate with the exact ones at r >= 0.998, so the closed form's layer split is within 0.05% of the exact optimum in 22 of 24 layouts and within 0.35% in the other two. Rule means and their order are unchanged (real-map paper split 1.001, stopping rule 1.009, census-only 1.031). Three of seven predictions held, one split (the correlation held, the 10% level did not) and three failed. Preliminary. Results: [`exact_timing_results.txt`](experiments/hub_airspace/exact_timing_results.txt), [`exact_timing_check_results.txt`](experiments/hub_airspace/exact_timing_check_results.txt).

**Milestone 11t** (`experiments/hub_airspace/start_term.py`): **can the closed form's missing take-off term be computed without sampling flight pairs?** Yes. Under the semicircular rule a flight's two legs trace the same segment in complementary layers, so a start term never depends on layers, and it factorises into one operator's expected airborne time within R of another operator's hub and delivery points: a finite sum over destination lists. The deterministic term matches 11s's sampled one (pooled 1.0002; 23 of 24 layouts within 3 standard errors), and with it the closed form is within 2% of the exact estimator in all 24 layouts (mean 1.0025). 60% of the term comes from drones turning for home at delivery points, 40% from hub launches; it scales as pi R^2 (3.93x from 50 m to 100 m). It does not reduce the estimate's noise (median sd ratio 1.02, refuting the prediction) and in pure Python it is 5x slower than sampling. Five of six predictions held. Preliminary. Results: [`start_term_results.txt`](experiments/hub_airspace/start_term_results.txt).

**Milestone 11x** (`experiments/hub_airspace/flux_term.py`): **can the drone conflict closed form be computed with no sampling at all, and how noisy were the sampled estimates?** Yes. The crossing (flux) term is an exact sum over every pair of destinations (about 2.5 billion crossing tests per eight-operator layout, 130 s in numpy), so with 11t's start term the whole closed form needs only hub sites, destination lists, shares and speeds. It matches the sampled closed form in expectation (pooled 1.004; fresh seeds 0.995 to 1.000) and the exact estimator within 2% in 22 of 24 layouts. As a noise-free reference it shows that the 400,000-pair estimators used since 11g carry about 0.8% heavy-tailed noise per layout (up to 1.9%; the top 1% of flight pairs carry a quarter to over half of the sum): the sampled closed form picked a layer split up to 2.75% worse than its own optimum in 5 of 24 layouts, and scoring on the sampled exact estimator, which shares those pairs, hid the losses. The census-free paper weight (11u's gas_d) is level-unbiased against the flux term (median 0.999), so its 0.90 to 1.07 scatter is raster error. Preliminary; results in `experiments/hub_airspace/flux_term_results.txt` and `flux_tail_check_results.txt`.

**Milestone 11ac** (`experiments/hub_airspace/split_scale.py`): **does the cheap drone layer-split rule still work with 12 or 16 delivery operators?** Yes. A 100 m raster of the operators' real destinations picks the best of 2,047 two-layer splits in 21 of 24 twelve-operator layouts and the best of 32,767 in 16 of 24 sixteen-operator layouts, losing at most 0.44% against the simulator-checked closed form at about 1/50 of its cost. Census maps lose 0.8% to 1.5%, uniform discs 4% to 7%, hub distance 10% to 12%. Seven of eight predictions held. The refuted one went the other way: a two-layer split is worth less as operators are added (the average split leaves 1.6 and 1.5 times the best split's conflicts, against 2.0 with eight), because one group bit cannot separate most interacting pairs; more layers are the next question. Preliminary; results in `experiments/hub_airspace/split_scale_results.txt`.

**Milestone 11al** (`experiments/hub_airspace/drift_share.py`): **does barometer drift since take-off change how far apart drone altitude layers should be?** No, as long as the planner knows that nearby drones share most of it. Two drones zeroed at take-off both read the current pressure, so it cancels in the gap between them; what is left is the pressure change between their take-off times plus the pressure difference between their hubs. From the same 2014 NOAA ISD New York reports as 11ak: delivery missions in the 48 layouts last a median **3.4 min**, so a drone's own drift is only 0.2 m, and the pressure part of the gap between two drones is about **0.55 m** (sd), almost all of it the hub-to-hub difference at 3.8 km (a smooth-field scaling; the station pairs show a 2.2 m floor the data cannot attribute, so up to about 2.3 m). For 30 and 60 min missions sharing halves the pair error (0.55 and 0.52 of the independent figure). With 25 ft separation and 1 m height-keeping noise the best stack keeps **13.3 m spacing (5 bands)** in 24 of 24 layouts at every mission length, and pressure costs 0% (delivery), 0.5% (30 min) and **2.3% to 2.7%** (60 min) more losses of separation. Reading 11ak's 3.0 m per-drone drift into 11ai as an independent error instead widens the stack to 17.1 m (4 bands) and costs **12%** more at 60 min. Of six predictions, two held, three split and one was refuted. Preliminary; height-keeping noise still swept. Results: [`drift_share_results.txt`](experiments/hub_airspace/drift_share_results.txt), [`drift_share_stats_results.txt`](experiments/hub_airspace/drift_share_stats_results.txt), [`drift_share_space_check_results.txt`](experiments/hub_airspace/drift_share_space_check_results.txt).

**Milestone 11an** (`experiments/hub_airspace/rtk_baro.py`): **how large is a drone barometer's altitude error in forward flight, measured against RTK GNSS height, and does it change drone layer spacing?** About 0.8 m, and no. Of 400 public PX4 Flight Review logs (PX4: Meier, Honegger and Pollefeys 2015, doi:10.1109/ICRA.2015.7140074), 98 had RTK-fixed GNSS solutions and **7** had 30 s of level forward flight with the fix held. Of seven predictions, four held and three were refuted (only 7 usable flights; median 0.83 m, just above the predicted 0.25 to 0.80 m; correlation 0.2 s, not 5 s). **Milestone 11ao** (`experiments/hub_airspace/speed_offset.py`): **does a drone barometer's speed-dependent static-pressure offset, which differs between airframes, change how far apart delivery-drone altitude layers should be?** Not at the size measured, but it would at delivery cruise speeds. Two of five predictions held (no change at a quarter of the offset; knowing the spread is enough); the three predicting a wider stack and a large cost at the measured size were refuted. **Milestone 11bi** (`experiments/hub_airspace/stale_track.py`): **does a drone's hold-until-clear rule for climbs and descents still work on broadcast Remote ID, with stale, delayed and lost messages?** Yes, if the broadcast reports speed to better than 1 m/s; the speed accuracy matters far more than message loss. A receiver dead-reckons each intruder from its newest message (1 per second, at most 1 s latency, per 14 CFR 89.310 and 89.320), so the position error grows with the message's age times the velocity error. On 11bg's 48 New York layouts, the radius that meets 1e-7 collisions per flight hour grows from 50 m (intent known) to **52 m** at <0.3 m/s, **74 to 106 m** at <1 m/s (from no loss to 90% lost; an urban Bluetooth study measured 50% to 74% loss uncoded) with a hold under **0.16 s per flight**, but **234 to 384 m** at <3 m/s, where the hold is 0.9 to 4.3 s per flight and 14 to 351 s at ten times the demand: the rule stops scaling. Scaling the radius per track by its age helps only when most messages are lost. 4 of 6 pre-registered predictions held. Preliminary (independent losses, a full-column bound on the prediction horizon, no unseen launches). Results: [`stale_track_results.txt`](experiments/hub_airspace/stale_track_results.txt).

**Milestone 11bk** (`experiments/hub_airspace/track_age.py`): **should a drone's Remote ID receiver stop trusting tracks older than a cut-off (a maximum track age), or scale its protection radius with track age?** Scale the radius. With one blockage staling every track at once, at <1 m/s speed accuracy, half the messages lost and 30 s bursts, the optimal age-scheduled radius holds **0.15 to 0.17 s per flight** against **0.64 to 0.77 s** for one fixed radius (about a tenth of it at 100 s bursts), and capping every decision's miss probability at ten times the mean keeps almost all of that saving. A blanket wait whenever the newest message is older than 60 s costs **6.2 to 6.7 s per flight** at today's demand; a cut-off pays only at ten times the demand with 100 s bursts (51 to 60 s against FR's 2e4 to 1e5 s) or at <3 m/s. 4 of 6 pre-registered predictions held. Preliminary: ages frozen over a hold, one common channel per receiver, swept burst lengths. Results: [`track_age_results.txt`](experiments/hub_airspace/track_age_results.txt).

**Milestone 11bj** (`experiments/hub_airspace/burst_loss.py`): **does bursty Remote ID message loss (a building shadowing the receiver for many seconds) break the hold-until-clear rule?** Not until bursts are long: what matters is the mean burst length, not the mean loss rate. With a Gilbert two-state channel at the same mean loss, at <1 m/s speed accuracy and half the messages lost the 1e-7 radius grows from 76 m (independent loss) to **99 m** at 10 s bursts and **200 m** at 30 s, and the hold from 0.07 s to **0.13 s** and **0.64 to 0.77 s** per flight; it passes 1 s per flight only once bursts average **32 to 56 s** (126 to 224 s at <0.3 m/s). Going from 3 to 30 s bursts multiplies the hold about 10 times; going from 50% to 90% loss at 30 s bursts, 1.3 times. So a procedure should bound the time since a track's last message, not the reception rate. 2 of 6 pre-registered predictions held (the rule proved more robust than predicted). Preliminary: burst lengths are swept (none measured for Remote ID were found), with a geometric burst law. Results: [`burst_loss_results.txt`](experiments/hub_airspace/burst_loss_results.txt).

**Milestone 11bh** (`experiments/hub_airspace/hold_level.py`): **where should a delivery drone hover while it waits to descend through other operators' altitude layers?** Half a layer from its own level, on whichever side is emptier at that point. On 11bg's 48 New York layouts and hold rule, hovering in the own level carries up to **1.57e-7 collisions per flight hour** (median 2e-8 to 4e-8; 11bg reported twice that by counting both headings of a band's traffic at one level, corrected here), and between levels on the emptier side at most **5.7e-10** (median about 1e-11). **Milestone 11bg** (`experiments/hub_airspace/transit_hold.py`): **what does it cost to make drone climbs and descents through occupied altitude layers meet a collision target?** Very little at today's modelled density. A hold-until-clear (gap acceptance) rule, where a drone waits until no lower-layer drone is predicted within a protection radius of its climb point, priced with Adams' delay formula on exact per-point traffic flux over 48 New York layouts: with intruders reported at the Remote ID accuracy floor (100 ft at 95%, 14 CFR 89.310) a 50 m radius and a 25 ft vertical window meet 1e-7 collisions per flight hour for a mean hold of **0.026 to 0.030 s per flight** (0.27 to 0.31 s at ten times the demand), while flying a single layer instead costs **4.2 times** the losses of separation. The rule saturates between 30 and 100 times the demand, near other operators' hubs. 4 of 6 pre-registered predictions held; results in `experiments/hub_airspace/transit_hold_results.txt`. Preliminary.

**Milestone 11bf** (`experiments/hub_airspace/collision_target.py`): **does a collision target per flight hour change how many drone altitude layers to fly?** Not through the spacing. **Milestone 11am** (`experiments/hub_airspace/px4_height.py`): **how well do real drones hold altitude, and does it change how far apart drone altitude layers should be?** From 17 public PX4 Flight Review logs (PX4: Meier, Honegger and Pollefeys 2015, doi:10.1109/ICRA.2015.7140074) of multirotors flying level Mission legs, the altitude controller holds its own estimate to a median **4.3 cm** RMS (fleet 4.4 cm, near-Gaussian, 1.4 s correlation time), so control error is negligible. Of seven predictions, three held, one split and three were refuted. Preliminary: 17 uploaded test and demonstration flights, not delivery operations. **Milestone 11aj** (`experiments/hub_airspace/ref_mix.py`): **should drones on barometric and GNSS altitude be stacked in separate layers?** No. When half the operators fly barometric altitude and half GNSS, the gap between two drones on different references carries the mismatch between the references (about 8.3 m per hPa of pressure-setting error), while drones on the same reference share and cancel it. With a 25 ft separation, 1 m error per drone and a 3 m reference mismatch, a planner that knows each operator's reference stacks the two classes apart in **0 of 24** layouts at 12 and at 16 operators, and gains only a median **0.8% and 0.2%** over a planner that ignores the mismatch. Forcing a "barometric below, GNSS above" split costs **3.2% and 8.0%** more. Widening every level to cover the mixed pairs (17 m, 4 bands) costs **7.5% and 8.8%** more. The mixed airspace as a whole costs 2.6% to 3.5% more losses of separation than one shared reference (5% to 8% at a 5 m mismatch). The spacing stays at 13.3 m (5 bands). So set the spacing by the same-reference error, assign bands without regard to reference, and fix a large mismatch at its source. Three of six predictions held. The mismatch (0 to 5 m) is swept, not sourced. Preliminary; results in `experiments/hub_airspace/ref_mix_results.txt` and `ref_mix_posthoc_results.txt`, figure `experiments/hub_airspace/ref_mix.svg`.

**Milestone 11ai** (`experiments/hub_airspace/level_leak.py`): **how far apart should drone altitude layers be once drones cannot hold their altitude exactly?** Further than the separation minimum, by about three standard deviations of the altitude difference. **Milestone 11s** (`experiments/hub_airspace/steiner_bias.py`): **why does the closed-form drone conflict count run 7% low, and can it be fixed?** Two straight flight legs conflict at most once, and the episode begins either as the paths cross into the separation disc (flux) or because one drone takes off or turns for home already inside it (a start). Restricting a short run to the paper's top five splits halves the loss at 5,000 flights (1.9% against 3.8%, sign test p = 0.02) but stops helping by 20,000 and hurts at 50,000, because the top five exclude the best split in 3 of 24 layouts. For a simulator that needs one run per candidate split (one with conflict resolution), the paper's **top 10 at 20,000 flights matches full search for 3 CPU s against 39 s**. Three of eight predictions refuted; preliminary. Results: [`sim_budget_results.txt`](experiments/hub_airspace/sim_budget_results.txt).

**Milestone 11j** (`experiments/hub_airspace/demand_weights.py`): **do census residents or jobs let a planner pick drone altitude layers without traffic data?** Weighting each operator's customers inside its service disc by where people work (LODES 2022, 3.06 M jobs at block points) instead of uniformly over land makes the paper weights **rank which operator pairs conflict most almost as the simulator does: median Pearson r 0.990 against 0.964 for the land mask, worst layout 0.948 against 0.692**, and gets their scale right (total 1.01x the closed form's, against 0.74x). It does **not** make the split much better: median excess over the closed form 1.2% against 1.9%, within 5% in 17 of 24 (unchanged), with three layouts fixed (15% to 24% worse with the land mask, within 2.1% now) and three broken (to 9% to 23% worse). Residents plus jobs does best: median excess 0.0%, within 5% in 18 of 24, picking the simulator's best split in 12 of 24, never worse than jobs alone (better in 6; sign test p = 0.03, a comparison made after the run). 2020 residents alone (block-group centroids) do no better than the land mask. A likely cause of the remaining misses, not yet tested: near-ties in the max-cut, where a small error in one pair weight flips the split. Five of nine predictions refuted; swept radii, speeds and shares; one city; preliminary. Data: [`demand_extract.py`](experiments/hub_airspace/demand_extract.py), results: [`demand_weights_results.txt`](experiments/hub_airspace/demand_weights_results.txt).

**Milestone 11h** (`experiments/hub_airspace/paper_weights.py`): **can a planner pick drone altitude layers on paper, without traffic data?** A kinetic-gas integral over a 100 m grid (the local, hub-radial density field, not the homogeneous gas model that 11a and 11c found 3.4x to 7x low), with each operator's flights spread uniformly over its service disc (inputs: hub sites, radii, speeds, shares), lands a **median 2.6% above the closed form's split on 11g's 24 layouts, against 13.6% for hub distance**, in 0.13 CPU s per layout (15x less than the closed form); it is within 5% in 13 of 24 and up to 27% worse in the rest. The same integral on the real destination list reproduces the closed form (pair-weight r >= 0.995, within 2.1% in all 24), so the remaining gap is the demand map. Correcting hub distance for speed alone does not help (median 1.15x). Swept radii, speeds and shares; preliminary. Results: [`paper_weights_results.txt`](experiments/hub_airspace/paper_weights_results.txt).

**Milestone 11g** (`experiments/hub_airspace/random_layouts.py`): **when does a traffic model beat hub distance for assigning drone operators to altitude layers?** On 24 random eight-operator layouts over New York with mixed service radii (1.5 to 8 km), speeds (12 to 28 m/s) and market shares, max-cut on the closed form's pairwise conflict weights stays **within 1.6% of the simulator's best split in all 24** (the same split in 20), while max-cut on market share over hub distance, 11f's near-free heuristic, is **at least 5% worse in 19 of 24 layouts (median 1.14x, worst 1.75x)**. Splitting by shared destinations is worse still (median 1.16x and 1.20x for two demand weights). So 11f's "distance is nearly enough" held only for equal-radius operators at k-means sites. Radii, speeds and shares swept, not sourced; preliminary. Results: [`random_layouts_results.txt`](experiments/hub_airspace/random_layouts_results.txt).

**Milestone 11f** (`experiments/hub_airspace/graph_partition.py`): with **eight drone delivery operators** sharing New York, **which operators should share an altitude layer**? Treating it as a graph partition (max-cut on pairwise conflict weights from the closed form), the chosen split is the simulator's own best of all 127 in every scenario, and on top of the semicircular rule it leaves **20% to 29% of the en-route conflicts**, against about 44% for an average balanced four-four split; splitting by speed leaves 1.5x to 2.7x the best split's conflicts. The practical surprise: **max-cut on market shares over hub distance, with no traffic model, is within 2.2%**, while splitting by shared destinations is 1.3x to 1.6x worse. One base-rule simulation scores every partition exactly (tested). Terminal conflicts still favour leg direction. Speeds, shares and sites illustrative; preliminary. Results: [`graph_partition_results.txt`](experiments/hub_airspace/graph_partition_results.txt).

**Milestone 11e** (`experiments/hub_airspace/operator_layers.py`): when a UTM service has more altitude layers than the semicircular rule needs, what should the extra layer bit separate: **outbound and inbound legs, fast and slow drones, or operators**? With four delivery operators sharing New York, putting the operators whose service areas overlap most in different layers (semicircular x operator pairing) leaves **2.3x to 3.7x fewer en-route conflicts** than semicircular x leg direction, and the obvious fix for mixed fleets, **a speed band, does worse than leg direction** (0.0164 against 0.0115 per flight). The closed form ranks the five four-layer rules in the simulator's order in two of three scenarios and picks a rule tied with the best in all three, so the assignment can be chosen without simulating. Caveat: pairing does nothing for conflicts inside 250 m of a hub, which leg direction halves, so on all conflicts leg direction still wins. Speeds, shares and sites illustrative; preliminary. Results: [`operator_layers_results.txt`](experiments/hub_airspace/operator_layers_results.txt).

**Milestone 11d** (`experiments/hub_airspace/mixed_fleet.py`): does the semicircular rule still beat the outbound/inbound split when **drone delivery operators fly at different speeds** and hold unequal market shares? A closed form for unequal speeds derived here (crossing window 2R |va - vb| / |va x vb|) matches the simulator within 3.6% in one layer. With four operators at 12, 16, 24 and 28 m/s, **the semicircular rule still leaves 57% fewer en-route conflicts than the split** (68% at equal speeds), but mixed speeds raise its conflicts by 76% (overtaking on near-parallel tracks), against 31% for the split and 11% for one layer. When the largest operator (55% of flights) is also the slowest, one layer's en-route conflicts per flight rise 1.52x. Speeds and shares swept, not sourced; preliminary. Results: [`mixed_fleet_results.txt`](experiments/hub_airspace/mixed_fleet_results.txt).

**Milestone 11c** (`experiments/hub_airspace/multi_hub.py`): what happens with several hubs and several drone delivery operators in one city? With one operator, nearest-hub service keeps every hub's flights inside its own convex Voronoi cell, so **hubs never interfere** (zero cross-hub conflicts in the simulator) and en-route conflicts per flight fall from 0.40 to 0.023 going from 1 to 8 hubs at 240 flights/h. With four operators whose 5 km service areas overlap, **31.5% of en-route conflicts are crossings between operators**, and a closed form with a crossing term derived here matches the simulator within 7.4%. The gas model is 3.4x low even for the crossing part. Of two-layer rules, **the semicircular rule (eastbound and westbound tracks in different layers) leaves 68% fewer en-route conflicts than an outbound/inbound split** (7.6% against 23.7% of one layer); four layers leave 3.2%. Straight legs, equal speeds, k-means operator sites; preliminary. Results: [`multi_hub_results.txt`](experiments/hub_airspace/multi_hub_results.txt).

**Milestone 11b** (`experiments/hub_airspace/wind.py`): does wind change how often a delivery hub's own drones lose separation? A closed form derived here generalises 11a's to any ground speeds (every encounter scales with the time offset between the two hub events) and matches the simulator within 1.7% in 8 base cases and 6.1% across a 30-cell wind sweep. **Wind raises one layer's en-route conflicts by about as much as it lengthens time in the layer** (within 1.7% at every grid point; v/H = sqrt(1 - w^2 sin^2 beta)/(1 - w^2)): 18% with a wind of 0.4 times airspeed along Manhattan's main bearing, 11% across it. Over 4,392 measured ERA5 100 m wind hours (NYC, Apr to Sep 2014) the average hour adds **6.1% at 20 m/s** (p90 13%, max 47%) , 10.0% at 15 m/s and 2.7% at 30 m/s. The outbound/inbound layer split survives wind: equal-airspeed aircraft overtake each other at under 1% of the single layer's rate, less than a +-15% airspeed spread does. Steady wind per hour, one hub, straight legs; preliminary. Figure: [`wind_factor.svg`](experiments/hub_airspace/wind_factor.svg). Data: ERA5 via Open-Meteo, CC BY 4.0.

**Milestone 11a** (`experiments/hub_airspace/`): how often do a delivery hub's own drones lose separation in one cruise layer? With 17,250 measured NYC destinations (Uber pickups, April 2014) within 5 km of a Manhattan hub, 20 m/s cruise and a 50 m separation radius, **en-route conflicts per flight grow linearly with traffic and reach 0.1 per flight at 62 flights/h**. A closed form derived here, C = (lambda^2 / v) E[min(2 min(d1, d2), R / sin(dphi/2))], needs only the destination distribution and matches an exact closest-approach simulator within 1% in the base case (44 of 45 sweep cells within 10%). Manhattan's narrow bearings double the conflicts against uniform bearings; the gas model, the usual capacity estimate, is 7x low (17x at 10 km). Separate outbound and inbound layers remove 95% of en-route conflicts. No wind, no other operators, straight legs; preliminary. Figure: [`conflicts_per_flight.svg`](experiments/hub_airspace/conflicts_per_flight.svg). Data: FiveThirtyEight, uber-tlc-foil-response (NYC TLC FOIL), CC BY 4.0.

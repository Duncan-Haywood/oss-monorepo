## 1. Secularization theory and its critics (Weber, Berger, Bruce, Norris & Inglehart, Taylor, Stark & Iannaccone, Voas & Chaves, Casanova)

### Takeaway
The classic thesis (Weber to early Berger to Bruce) says modernization erodes religion's social significance. Its critics take two lines. Supply-side theorists (Stark, Iannaccone) and the later Berger say demand is constant and the West is the exception. Norris & Inglehart, by contrast, tie religiosity to existential insecurity. Since 2016 the strongest empirical work (Voas & Chaves) has removed the US as the main counterexample. Pew's 2010–2020 global data show net disaffiliation through switching in most regions, while global religious *share* stays high because of fertility in religious, poorer populations. That pattern fits Norris & Inglehart's prediction.

### Cited Findings
**Voas & Chaves (2016)** [V]
- Mark Chaves and David Voas, "Is the United States a Counterexample to the Secularization Thesis?", *American Journal of Sociology* 121(5): 1517–1556 (pub. 2016) — [Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1122832); open-access PDF at [UCL Discovery](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1476386).
- Argument: American religiosity has been declining for decades. The decline comes from the same generational replacement seen elsewhere in the West, where each cohort is less religious than the one before. The US should therefore no longer be treated as a counterexample. The argument rests on trends, not levels — [DukeSpace record](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/items/ee306b8e-049f-4bf6-af14-3149a7e5d1d5); Voas's summary on the [LSE USAPP blog, 5 Sept 2016](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/usappblog/2016/09/05/religion-is-in-decline-in-the-west-and-america-is-no-exception).
- In that blog, Voas writes that the change comes from cohort replacement, not from individuals losing faith over their lifetimes — [LSE USAPP](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/usappblog/2016/09/05/religion-is-in-decline-in-the-west-and-america-is-no-exception).

**Other theorists: standard bibliographic facts [UNVERIFIED this session, no page fetched]**
- Max Weber: "disenchantment of the world" (*Entzauberung der Welt*), from "Science as a Vocation" (lecture 1917, pub. 1919). See also *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1904–05).
- Peter L. Berger, *The Sacred Canopy* (Doubleday, 1967). Classic secularization statement, built on the loss of a shared "plausibility structure" under pluralism.
- Peter L. Berger (ed.), *The Desecularization of the World: Resurgent Religion and World Politics* (Eerdmans / Ethics and Public Policy Center, 1999). Berger's opening essay says his earlier claim was "essentially mistaken" and the world is "as furiously religious as it ever was," with Western Europe and a global intellectual elite as the exceptions. (Paraphrase; check exact wording.)
- Steve Bruce, *God is Dead: Secularization in the West* (Blackwell, 2002). A strong defense of the "secularization paradigm," including the claim that decline in Britain is driven by cohorts.
- Pippa Norris & Ronald Inglehart, *Sacred and Secular: Religion and Politics Worldwide* (Cambridge UP, 2004; 2nd ed. 2011). Existential-security thesis: religiosity falls as human security rises, but the world as a whole grows more religious because poorer, more religious societies have higher fertility.
- Charles Taylor, *A Secular Age* (Harvard/Belknap, 2007). Taylor, a Catholic philosopher, rejects "subtraction stories." He defines "Secularity 3" as a shift from a society where belief in God was unchallenged to one where it is "one option among others." His terms include the "immanent frame" and the "nova effect."
- Rodney Stark & Laurence Iannaccone, "A Supply-Side Reinterpretation of the 'Secularization' of Europe," *Journal for the Scientific Study of Religion* 33(3), 1994. See also Stark, "Secularization, R.I.P.," *Sociology of Religion* 60(3), 1999. Religious-economies model: demand is stable, state monopolies make supply lazy, and pluralism or competition raises participation.
- José Casanova, *Public Religions in the Modern World* (Univ. of Chicago Press, 1994). Splits "secularization" into three claims: (1) differentiation of secular spheres from religion, (2) decline of belief and practice, (3) privatization of religion. Casanova accepts (1), says (2) does not hold universally, and rejects (3), citing the "deprivatization" of religion in the 1980s (Poland, Latin America, the US Christian Right).

**Empirical tests from the 2025 Pew global data (relevant to theory)** [V]
- Among adults aged 18–54, about **3.2 people left religion for every one who joined**, worldwide. Christians had **3.1 leavers per joiner**. The unaffiliated gained the most from switching. Muslims grew mainly through natural increase (young age structure, high fertility) — [Pew Research Center, "How the Global Religious Landscape Changed From 2010 to 2020," pub. 9 June 2025, data 2010–2020](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/06/09/how-the-global-religious-landscape-changed-from-2010-to-2020/).

### Inferences
- The 2010–2020 Pew pattern looks like Norris & Inglehart's prediction: switching runs secular, while religious *share* holds up through fertility in religious, less secure populations. It is harder to square with a pure supply-side model, which predicts no net drift.
- Voas & Chaves plus the Pew US plateau (Section 7) suggest that cohort replacement, not individual apostasy, drives decline, with periods of stability between cohorts.
- Berger's 1999 recantation is best read as a claim about *global* religiosity (true by headcount). It is not evidence against secularization in rich democracies, and Berger himself excepted Europe.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch primary pages for Berger 1999, Bruce 2002, Norris & Inglehart, Taylor, Stark & Iannaccone, or Casanova. Bibliographic details are from background knowledge.
- I found no fresh 2025–26 WVS/EVS time series this session. The WVS Wave 7 (2017–2022) and EVS 2017 datasets exist, but I verified no specific trend numbers. Norris & Inglehart's later book *Religion's Sudden Decline* (Inglehart, OUP 2021) reports falling religiosity in 43 of 49 countries, 2007–2019 [UNVERIFIED].
- I verified no Gallup figures this session (for example, Gallup's US church-membership series falling below 50% in 2020: 47% [UNVERIFIED]).

## 2. Global data: Pew 2025 (2010–2020), Pew 2015 projections to 2050, fertility, switching

### Takeaway
In 2020 Christians were still the largest group (about 2.3 bn, 28.8%), but they lost about 1.8 points of share. Muslims gained the same amount (to 25.6%), adding 347 m people, more than all other groups combined. The "nones" rose to 1.9 bn (24.2%). Pew's 2015 projection that the unaffiliated share would *fall* by 2050 relied on an old 2010 baseline, and Pew now says that baseline has been revised.

### Cited Findings
**Pew 2025 report (data 2010–2020, pub. 9 June 2025)** [V] — [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/06/09/how-the-global-religious-landscape-changed-from-2010-to-2020/)
- Christians: **2.3 billion** in 2020, **+122 million** since 2010. Share fell **1.8 points** to **28.8%**.
- Muslims: **+347 million**, more than all other religions combined. Share rose **1.8 points** to **25.6%**. (The page I fetched gave no absolute count. About 2.0 bn is implied by 25.6% of world population [UNVERIFIED as a printed figure].)
- Religiously unaffiliated: **1.9 billion**, **+270 million**. Share rose from **23.3%** to **24.2%**. Third-largest group.
- Hindus: **1.2 billion** (+126 m), share steady at **14.9%**.
- Buddhists: **324 million**, **−19 million**. The only major group to shrink in absolute numbers. Share fell to **4.1%**.
- Jews: **14.8 million**, about +1 m.
- People with any religion: **75.8%** of the world in 2020, down from **76.7%** in 2010.
- Christian-majority countries: **124 (2010) → 120 (2020)**. Christians fell below 50% in the UK (49%), Australia (47%), France (46%), and Uruguay (44%).
- Unaffiliated-majority countries: **7 → 10**, with the Netherlands (54%), Uruguay (52%), and New Zealand (51%) added.
- In 2020, **30.7%** of the world's Christians lived in sub-Saharan Africa, against **22.3%** in Europe.
- United States: Christians **78.3% (2010) → 64.0% (2020)**. Unaffiliated **16.5% → 29.7%** (Pew's 2020 demographic estimate, which differs in method from the RLS in Section 7).

**Pew 2015 "Future of World Religions" projections (baseline 2010, pub. 2 April 2015)** [V]
- By 2050, Muslims were projected at **2.8 bn (30%)** and Christians at **2.9 bn (31%)**, "nearly equal… possibly for the first time in history." The unaffiliated share was projected to fall from **16% (2010) to 13% (2050)**, because the large unaffiliated populations of China and Japan are old and have low fertility. In absolute terms they were projected to grow from 1.1 to 1.2 bn — [Pew, 2 Apr 2015](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2015/04/02/religious-projections-2010-2050).
- Pew's own note: the 2010 baseline has been revised. Readers should use the 2025 report for 2010 and 2020 estimates, and "new projections past 2020 are not yet available" — [Pew 2015 page note](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2015/04/02/religious-projections-2010-2050).
- Note the scale of the revision. The 2015 report put the 2010 unaffiliated share at 16%. The 2025 report puts it at 23.3% for 2010. These are not directly comparable, because China's estimate was re-measured.

**Fertility by religion** [UNVERIFIED this session]
- Pew 2015 gave total fertility rates for 2010–2015: Muslims about **3.1**, Christians about **2.7**, unaffiliated about **1.7**, world about **2.5**. Check against Pew's 2015 report or the 2017 "The Changing Global Religious Landscape."

### Inferences
- The Pew 2025 findings confirm the brief's framing: Christian share down, Muslim share up, nones up. "Nones growth" is +0.9 points of share, much smaller than the gains Muslims made.
- The 2015 projection that the unaffiliated share would decline already looks weak. By the 2025 estimates it *rose* 2010–2020, though the baselines differ.

### Gaps
- No post-2020 global Pew projection exists, per Pew.
- Detailed switching matrices by country (Pew 2025 has them) were not extracted.

## 3. Catholic Church: global numbers, shift South, US Mass attendance, vocations

### Takeaway
There were 1.405–1.406 bn baptized Catholics in mid-2023, 17.8% of humanity. Growth is concentrated in Africa, priests are slowly declining, seminarians are falling year after year, and permanent deacons are rising. In the US, CARA's modeled weekly Mass attendance is about 24%, back to the pre-pandemic level of 24.4%.

### Cited Findings
**Fides "Catholic Church Statistics 2025" (from the Statistical Yearbook of the Church; Catholic counts as of 30 June 2023, other data as of 31 Dec 2023; pub. 17 Oct 2025)** [V] — [Agenzia Fides](https://www.fides.org/en/news/76919-VATICAN_Catholic_Church_Statistics_2025); [Fides PDF](https://www.fides.org/de/attachments/view/file/Catholic_Church_Statistics_2025.pdf)
- Catholics: **1,405,454,000**, up **15,881,000** on the previous year. **17.8%** of the world, +0.1 points.
- Increase by continent: Africa **+8,309,000**, America **+5,668,000**, Asia **+954,000**, Europe **+740,000**, Oceania **+210,000**.
- Bishops: **5,430** (+77). Of these, 4,258 are diocesan and 1,172 religious.
- Priests: **406,996** (−734). Of these, diocesan **278,742** (−429) and religious **128,254** (−305).
- Permanent deacons: **51,433** (+1,234).
- Women religious: **589,423** (−9,805).
- Major seminarians: **106,495**, down from **108,481**, a drop of about 1,986 (my arithmetic). Minor seminarians: **95,021** (−140).

**Annuarium Statisticum Ecclesiae 2023 / Pontifical Yearbook 2025 (data 2023, pub. March 2025)** [V via secondary summaries]
- Catholics rose **1.15%** between 2022 and 2023, from about **1.39 bn to 1.406 bn**. Africa rose from **272 m to 281 m** (+3.31%) — [Vatican News, March 2025](https://www.vaticannews.va/en/vatican-city/news/2025-03/pontifical-yearbook-2025-priests-religious-statistics.html); [CNA/EWTN, 21 Mar 2025](https://ewtnnews.com/vatican/worldwide-catholic-population-hits-14-billion).
- Priests in 2022: **407,730**, down 142 from 407,872 in 2021 — same sources.
- There is a later report, *[UNVERIFIED; secondary only]*: Catholic World News, summarizing Fides on the 2026 yearbook (data 2024), says priests rose by **425 to 407,421** in 2024, "ending five years of decline," and that the 13-year decline in seminarians "accelerated" — [CatholicCulture.org](https://www.catholicculture.org/news/headlines/index.cfm?storyid=70979). This conflicts with the Fides 2025 total of 406,996 for 2023 (406,996 + 425 = 407,421, so it is arithmetically consistent if it refers to 2024). Confirm against the primary source.
- Asia: bishops and priests still growing (+1.6%), seminarians **−4.2%** — [AsiaNews](https://www.asianews.it/en/articles/catholics-are-still-growing-in-asia-but-priestly-vocations-decline-further).

**US Mass attendance (CARA)** [V via secondary reporting of CARA's "Nineteen Sixty-four" blog, 5 Feb 2025]
- Before the pandemic (2019), weekly Mass attendance averaged **24.4%**. From May 2023 to the first week of 2025 it averaged **24%**, "an overall return to pre-pandemic levels." The method combines CARA national surveys with Google Trends search volumes, so it is a *modeled* estimate, not a headcount. Christmas attendance returned to pre-pandemic levels in 2024. Easter and Ash Wednesday had already recovered in 2023 — [CNA, 10 Feb 2025](https://catholicnewsagency.com/amp/news/262131/cara-mass-attendance-ticks-back-up-nationally-after-pandemic-falter-data-suggests); [Aleteia, 9 Feb 2025](https://aleteia.org/2025/02/09/has-mass-attendance-finally-recovered-from-the-pandemic/).
- Long-run benchmark [UNVERIFIED this session]: CARA/Gallup put weekly Catholic attendance at about 55% around 1965, consistent with the fall to the 24% range today.
- US Catholic identity has held at **19–21%** in all but one of 11 Pew surveys since 2014. It was **19%** in 2023–24 — [Pew RLS, 26 Feb 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/02/26/decline-of-christianity-in-the-us-has-slowed-may-have-leveled-off/).

### Inferences
- Catholic growth tracks the Pew pattern: demography in Africa versus stagnation in Europe. About 52% of the 2022–23 increase was African.
- Fewer priests and more Catholics means the number of Catholics per priest keeps rising. That ratio is not computed in the sources, but roughly 3,450 Catholics per priest follows from 1.405 bn ÷ 406,996 (my arithmetic).

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve continent *totals* for 2023 (only the changes) or the Europe/Africa share of Catholics. A common Fides-derived figure, about 20% of Catholics living in Africa, is [UNVERIFIED].
- CARA's own pages were not fetched directly.

## 4. Orthodox Christianity: Pew 2017, Russia's belief–practice gap, the 2018 schism, Ethiopia

### Takeaway
There are nearly 260 m Orthodox Christians, a falling share of Christianity (20% in 1910, 12% today). Russia's post-Soviet revival is mostly a matter of identity: 6% of Russian Orthodox attend weekly, against 78% in Ethiopia. The 2018 Moscow–Constantinople break over Ukraine is the most serious split in Orthodoxy in centuries and remains unhealed.

### Cited Findings
**Pew, "Orthodox Christianity in the 21st Century" (pub. 8 Nov 2017; surveys June 2015–July 2016 in Central and Eastern Europe, Ethiopia 2015, US 2014)** [V] — [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2017/11/08/orthodox-christianity-in-the-21st-century/); [full report PDF](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2017/12/Orthodoxy-II-FULL-REPORT-12-5.pdf)
- "Nearly 260 million" Orthodox. The population "more than doubled" over the century, yet fell from about **20% of Christians (1910) to 12%** and from about **7% to 4%** of world population.
- Europe's share of Orthodox: **91% (1910) → 77%**.
- Russia: more than **100 million** Orthodox, a resurgence after 1991.
- Ethiopia: about **36 million** Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo, nearly **14%** of world Orthodoxy.
- Weekly attendance: Russia **6%**, Bosnia 10%, US 31%, Ethiopia **78%**.
- Religion "very important": Russia **15%**, Bosnia 46%, US 52%, Ethiopia nearly all.
- Daily prayer: Russia **18%**, Bosnia 28%, US 57%, Ethiopia **65%**.
- Icons at home: median of **90%** across 14 CEE countries, **73%** in Ethiopia.

**2018 Moscow–Constantinople schism** [V]
- On **15 Oct 2018**, meeting in Minsk, the Russian Orthodox Holy Synod broke Eucharistic communion with the Ecumenical Patriarchate. Moscow was responding to Constantinople's move toward Ukrainian autocephaly and its annulment of the **1686** synodal letter that had let Moscow ordain the Metropolitan of Kyiv — [CNA](https://www.catholicnewsagency.com/news/will-pope-francis-have-an-impact-on-orthodoxys-ukraine-dispute-55140); [AsiaNews](https://www.asianews.it/news-en/Moscow-breaks-communion-with-Constantinople-45217.html).
- Patriarch Bartholomew signed the tomos of autocephaly for the Orthodox Church of Ukraine (Metropolitan Epiphanius) in Istanbul on **5–6 Jan 2019**. Sources differ by a day: the Liturgy was on 5 Jan, and the tomos was formally handed over on Epiphany, 6 Jan — [ICDS Estonia, 10 Jan 2019](https://icds.ee/en/a-tomos-for-ukraines-orthodox-church-the-final-schism/). Moscow calls it the "legalization of schism" — same source.

### Inferences
- Russia shows clearly that identity can be high while practice stays low ("believing without belonging" reversed: belonging without practising). This supports Casanova's distinction that public religion can grow even as private practice stays low.
- Ethiopia's Orthodox are the least studied and most observant large Orthodox population. That supports the Global South thesis (Section 5) inside Orthodoxy too.

### Gaps
- No post-2022 survey was checked for Russian attendance after the invasion of Ukraine. The ROC's own Easter attendance counts (e.g., Interior Ministry figures of about 1–4 m nationally) are [UNVERIFIED].
- Ethiopian Orthodox numbers after the 2023 internal synod crisis and the Tigray war are not verified.

## 5. Reformation, Enlightenment, Awakenings, Pentecostalism, Global South

### Takeaway
Econometric work (Becker & Woessmann 2009) attributes most of the Protestant economic advantage to literacy from Bible reading, not to Weber's work ethic. Pentecostal/charismatic Christianity grew from about 1 m (1900) to about 640–670 m by the 2010s–2020s by World Christian Database counts, which depend heavily on definitions. Africa's Christian population went from about 9 m (1900) to more than 500 m.

### Cited Findings
- Sascha O. Becker & Ludger Woessmann, "Was Weber Wrong? A Human Capital Theory of Protestant Economic History," *QJE* 124(2): 531–596 (pub. 2009), [doi:10.1162/qjec.2009.124.2.531](https://doi.org/10.1162/qjec.2009.124.2.531). Data: **452 Prussian counties**, late 19th century (1871 census). They use distance to Wittenberg as an instrument. Protestants' higher literacy accounts for "most of the gap" in prosperity. The 2007 IZA working paper said "the whole gap" — [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/article/oupqjecon/v_3a124_3ay_3a2009_3ai_3a2_3ap_3a531-596..htm); [DIW](https://www.diw.de/de/diw_01.c.763684.de/s_5368.html) [V].
- Pentecostals/Charismatics: **981,000 (1900)** and **643,661,000** "today" (an older WCD/Gordon-Conwell edition, c. 2015–2018). An earlier edition projected **669 m** for 2017. Editions differ and are not comparable — [Boston Pilot / CWR column](https://thebostonpilot.com/article.php?ID=173251); [Lifeway Research](https://research.lifeway.com/?p=109206) [V as secondary reporting of WCD].
- In the Gordon-Conwell 2023 status report, Pentecostals/Charismatics had the highest annual growth rate from 2000 to 2022 (**1.88%**), ahead of evangelicals (1.79%). They are projected to reach **1 billion by 2050**, second only to Catholics — [Lifeway summary](https://research.lifeway.com/?p=109206) [V secondary].
- Christians in Africa: **8.7 m (1900)**, about **542 m** (older edition "today"), and a projection of **1.2–1.28 bn by 2050** — [Boston Pilot](https://thebostonpilot.com/article.php?ID=173251); the 2024 edition of "Status of Global Christianity" is in *IBMR* via [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/23969393241283291) (403 through proxy, not read).
- [UNVERIFIED this session] Azusa Street Revival, Los Angeles, from April 1906 (William J. Seymour). Brad S. Gregory, *The Unintended Reformation* (Harvard/Belknap, 2012) argues that the Reformation's doctrinal disagreement produced modern pluralism and secularized knowledge without meaning to. Philip Jenkins, *The Next Christendom* (OUP 2002; 3rd ed. 2011) argues Christianity's center of gravity has moved to Africa, Latin America, and Asia. First Great Awakening c. 1730s–40s (Edwards, Whitefield); Second c. 1790s–1840s.

### Inferences
- Pew's 2020 figure (30.7% of Christians in sub-Saharan Africa vs 22.3% in Europe) independently confirms Jenkins's thesis with census-grade data (Section 2).
- Pentecostal counts swing by hundreds of millions depending on whether "charismatics" inside Catholic and mainline churches and African "independents" are included. Bloggers should cite the edition and its definition.

### Gaps
- I could not access the 2025 Gordon-Conwell "Status of Global Christianity" numbers (SAGE returned 403). The 2025 Pentecostal total is unverified.
- No primary pages were fetched for Gregory, Jenkins, Azusa Street, or the Awakenings.

## 6. Recent developments: US "nones" plateau, the UK "Quiet Revival" and its retraction, US conversions to Orthodoxy

### Takeaway
The Pew 2023–24 RLS shows the US Christian decline paused at about 60–64% since 2019. The UK "Quiet Revival" headline (monthly churchgoing up from 8% to 12%, young men above 20%) was undermined in March 2026, when YouGov found fraudulent respondents and apologized. Reports of a surge of converts to US Orthodoxy rest mostly on parish anecdotes and in-house studies. The Pew sex-ratio shift (64% male) is the strongest piece of hard evidence, and it comes from small subsamples.

### Cited Findings
**Pew Religious Landscape Study 2023–24 (fielded 17 Jul 2023–4 Mar 2024, N=36,908, response rate 20%, ±0.8 pts; pub. 26 Feb 2025)** [V] — [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/02/26/decline-of-christianity-in-the-us-has-slowed-may-have-leveled-off/)
- Christians: **78% (2007) → 71% (2014) → 62% (2023–24)**.
- Nones: **16% → 23% → 29%**.
- Protestants: **40%** in 2023–24, down from about 51% in 2007, and between 40–42% since 2019. Catholics: **19%**.
- Since 2019, Christians have been "relatively stable, hovering between 60% and 64%."
- Ages 18–24 vs 74+: Christian **46% vs 80%**, pray daily **27% vs 58%**, attend monthly **25% vs 49%**, nones **43% vs 13%**. People born 2000–2006 are about as likely as 1990s births to identify as Christian. That is a cohort plateau, not a revival.

**UK "The Quiet Revival" (Bible Society; YouGov fieldwork 2024; report pub. April 2025)** [V]
- Claims: share of adults in England & Wales who identify as Christian and attend at least monthly rose from **8% (2018) to 12% (2024)**. Ages 18–24 rose from **4% to 16%**, young men to "over a fifth," young women from 4% to 12% — [Christian Post, 28 Mar 2026](http://www.christianpost.com/news/yougov-retracts-bible-societys-quiet-revival-study.html).
- Pre-retraction critiques: YouGov's opt-in panel was vulnerable to "bogus respondents" (Pew's Conrad Hackett). The sample's Christian identity (33%) was below the 2021 Census (46%). Results conflicted with probability surveys (BSA) and with denominational attendance counts. Sir John Curtice called for replication — [Christian Today, "The Quiet Non-Revival"](https://www.christiantoday.com/news/the-quiet-non-revival); [Religion Media Centre](https://religionmediacentre.org.uk/news/quiet-revival-data-is-flawed-nonetheless-the-story-is-true/); [Baptist News Global](https://baptistnews.com/article/one-group-still-sees-revival-in-uk-although-other-polls-dont-agree/).
- **Retraction (about 26 Mar 2026):** YouGov's re-analysis found "a number of respondents who we can now identify as fraudulent." CEO Stephan Shakespeare: "YouGov takes full responsibility… and we apologise." YouGov said Bible Society had reported the data accurately, and the survey is being rerun. Bible Society CEO Paul Williams was "deeply disappointed" but said other probability surveys show greater engagement among young adults. The organization still says Christianity is growing — [Christian Post, 28 Mar 2026](http://www.christianpost.com/news/yougov-retracts-bible-societys-quiet-revival-study.html).

**US conversions to Orthodoxy (2023–2025 reporting)** [V as reporting; evidence quality low to moderate]
- Pew data, as cited by the BBC (24 May 2025), show US Orthodox Christians are now **64% male**, up from **46% in 2007**. The BBC says the true increase in converts is "hard to gauge." An OCA study of **773 converts** found many cited the pandemic as their trigger — [Baylor Religion Watch, 2025](https://religionwatch.web.baylor.edu/news/story/2025/eastern-orthodoxy-religion-young-men).
- Orthodox Studies Institute researchers (part of an Orthodox college) claim about **1% of Gen Z is Orthodox vs about 0.5% of Gen X**. They project membership growth of +69% (conservative) to +115% by 2040. Parish reports describe catechumen classes growing from near zero to 10+, and from about 12 to 50–150 in convert-friendly parishes. Some parishes declined or stalled — [Religion Unplugged, 20 & 26 Nov 2025](https://religionunplugged.com/news/2025/11/26/on-religion-the-flood-of-converts-reshaping-american-orthodoxy-part-2); [OrthoChristian](https://orthochristian.com/173357.html); NYT reporting republished by [Northeastern](https://cssh.northeastern.edu/orthodox-church-pews-are-overflowing-with-converts/); [Boston Globe, 19 Nov 2025](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2025/11/19/lifestyle/orthodox-christianity-young-men-conservatives).
- Historical baseline: a 2008 Krindatch survey (Patriarch Athenagoras Orthodox Institute, about 1,000 members) found **29%** of Greek Orthodox and **51%** of OCA members were adult converts — [Christian Century, Dec 2008](https://www.christiancentury.org/article/2008-12/us-orthodox-churches-have-more-converts-expected-study-says).

### Inferences
- Evidence grade: **US plateau, strong** (large probability sample, replicated across Pew surveys). **UK Quiet Revival, discredited** for its headline figures pending a rerun. **US Orthodox convert wave, plausible but weakly measured**: Orthodoxy is under 1% of US adults, so Pew subsamples are small (the 64% male figure carries wide margins). Conversions were already common in 2008, and most quantitative claims come from advocates.
- The "young men returning" narrative in both countries rests partly on opt-in online samples, which are the method most exposed to fraudulent respondents, as the YouGov case showed.

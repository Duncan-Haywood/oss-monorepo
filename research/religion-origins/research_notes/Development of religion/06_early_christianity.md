## 1. Historical Jesus: the non-Christian sources, the criteria debate, consensus facts, the resurrection, and mythicism

### Takeaway
[Consensus] Nearly all specialists, whatever their religion, hold that Jesus of Nazareth existed. He was a Jewish teacher and eschatological prophet in Galilee who was crucified under Pontius Pilate (procurator/prefect 26–36 CE). Josephus' *Testimonium Flavianum* is usually judged partly authentic but edited by Christians. Tacitus *Annals* 15.44 is generally treated as independent and authentic. What happened after the crucifixion (the resurrection) is the point where historical method stops and the lenses split sharply.

### Cited Findings

**Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.63–64 (Testimonium Flavianum) and 20.200 (James)**
- [Consensus] "Since the late 20th century, the general consensus has held that the Testimonium is partially authentic," with its original scope still unclear — [Wikipedia, "Josephus on Jesus"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephus_on_Jesus) (tertiary, used only to describe the state of opinion). A scholarly abstract likewise reports that "almost all scholars today accept that the Testimonium is at least partially a Christian interpolation" — [ProQuest abstract](https://www.proquest.com/docview/220270817).
- [Consensus, historical data point] Louis Feldman's 1980 survey of the literature counted 4 scholars who held the TF entirely genuine, 6 mostly genuine, 20 genuine with some interpolations, 9 with several interpolations, and 13 a total interpolation — reported in [Peter Kirby, "The Testimonium Flavianum"](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/testimonium.html). The figures are 1980-era and come second-hand; they are not a current poll.
- [Minority, toward full authenticity] T. C. Schmidt, *Josephus and Jesus: New Evidence for the One Called Christ* (Oxford University Press, 2025), argues that the passage is "substantially authentic" — reviewed sympathetically by [Roger Pearse, May 2025](https://www.roger-pearse.com/weblog/2025/05/).
- [Minority, toward total interpolation] Some recent work argues that Josephus wrote none of the TF — see the article indexed at [Hrčak (Croatian scholarly journals portal)](https://hrcak.srce.hr/347774). Ken Olson (in several essays) and Richard Carrier are associated with this view *(bibliographic, not fetched)*.
- [Consensus] The brief notice in *Ant.* 20.200 of "James, the brother of Jesus who was called Christ," stoned in 62 CE under the high priest Ananus, is far more widely accepted as authentic than the TF. John P. Meier, *A Marginal Jew*, vol. 1 (Doubleday/Anchor Bible Reference Library, 1991), ch. 3, is the standard treatment of both passages *(bibliographic, not fetched)*. Alice Whealey, *Josephus on Jesus: The Testimonium Flavianum Controversy from Late Antiquity to Modern Times* (Peter Lang, 2003), traces the Arabic (Agapius) and Syriac (Michael the Syrian) variants. These are often cited as evidence for a more neutral original *(bibliographic, not fetched)*.

**Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44 (c. 116 CE)**
- [Primary source] "Christus, from whom the name had its origin, suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus, and a most mischievous superstition…" — Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44, trans. Church & Brodribb, [Perseus Digital Library](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.02.0078:book=15:chapter=44).
- [Consensus] The passage is generally held to be authentic, because its hostile tone ("superstition") is unlike Christian interpolation. Pilate's title (he was strictly a *praefectus*, as the 1961 Caesarea inscription shows) is an anachronism and does not count against authenticity. Whether Tacitus had independent (archival) information or was repeating what Christians said in his own day is debated. Meier, *A Marginal Jew* vol. 1, ch. 3 *(bibliographic, not fetched)*.

**Pliny the Younger, *Epistles* 10.96–97 (c. 111–113 CE)**
- [Primary source] Pliny, governor of Bithynia-Pontus, reports that Christians met "on a fixed day before dawn" and sang "a hymn to Christ as to a god" (*carmen Christo quasi deo dicere*). He also says the "contagion" had spread through towns and countryside, and he notes that Trajan replied in 10.97 *(primary text; standard Loeb/ccel editions; URL not fetched)*. This is the earliest pagan evidence for Christian worship of Christ, and it bears directly on §2 (early high Christology). It says nothing independent about the historical Jesus.
- Suetonius, *Claudius* 25.4 ("impulsore Chresto") is often added to the list. Whether "Chrestus" means Christ is disputed *(bibliographic, not fetched)*.

**The criteria debate**
- [Consensus, older method] Meier, *A Marginal Jew* vol. 1 (1991), ch. 6, sets out the "criteria of authenticity": embarrassment, discontinuity, multiple attestation, coherence, and rejection/execution *(bibliographic, not fetched)*.
- [Critique within the mainstream] Chris Keith and Anthony Le Donne (eds.), *Jesus, Criteria, and the Demise of Authenticity* (T&T Clark, 2012), and Dale C. Allison, *Constructing Jesus: Memory, Imagination, and History* (Baker Academic, 2010), argue that the criteria cannot reliably isolate individual sayings. They shift attention to social memory and to recurring "gist" patterns. James D. G. Dunn, *Jesus Remembered* (Eerdmans, 2003), argues from oral tradition and the "impact" Jesus made on his followers *(all bibliographic, not fetched)*.

**Consensus "facts" lists**
- [Consensus] E. P. Sanders, *Jesus and Judaism* (Fortress/SCM, 1985), gives a widely cited list of "almost indisputable facts": Jesus was baptized by John; he was a Galilean who preached and healed; he called disciples and spoke of twelve; he confined his activity to Israel; he engaged in a controversy about the Temple; he was crucified outside Jerusalem by the Roman authorities; his followers continued as an identifiable movement after his death; and at least some Jews persecuted at least parts of the new movement. Sanders, *The Historical Figure of Jesus* (Penguin, 1993), restates the list *(bibliographic, not fetched; the list is reproduced in many textbooks)*. Bart D. Ehrman, *Jesus: Apocalyptic Prophet of the New Millennium* (Oxford UP, 1999), defends the apocalyptic-prophet model *(bibliographic, not fetched)*.

**Resurrection**
- [Consensus on what can be said historically] Most historians affirm only that, soon after the crucifixion, some followers (and Paul) sincerely believed they had seen Jesus alive. 1 Cor 15:3–8 is the earliest list, a pre-Pauline tradition usually dated to within a few years of the crucifixion. Whether that belief had a supernatural cause is outside historical method. This is Ehrman's stated position in *How Jesus Became God* (HarperOne, 2014) *(bibliographic, not fetched)*.
- [Catholic/Orthodox-congenial, Anglican author] N. T. Wright, *The Resurrection of the Son of God* (Fortress/SPCK, 2003), argues that the empty tomb combined with the appearances is the best historical explanation of the rise of early Christian belief. He also argues that "resurrection" in Second Temple Judaism meant bodily life after death, not spiritual survival *(bibliographic, not fetched)*.
- [Mediating/agnostic] Dale C. Allison Jr., *The Resurrection of Jesus: Apologetics, Polemics, History* (T&T Clark, 2021). It starts with historical-critical analysis of the empty tomb and the early confessions. It then compares the resurrection with other traditions (including Tibetan "rainbow body" accounts) and weighs apologetic and skeptical views. Allison concludes that the evidence fits more than one interpretation. See the [publisher summary as reproduced by Princeton Theological Seminary](https://ptsem.edu/?p=1196) and Henry Wansbrough's review in the [Church Times, 23 April 2021](https://www.churchtimes.co.uk/articles/2021/23-april/books-arts/book-reviews/the-resurrection-of-jesus-apologetics-polemics-history-by-dale-c-allison-jr), which calls the argument rigorous and fair. A critical blog review notes that the book has no bibliography and argues that Allison's sympathies pull him in several directions — [K. R. Harriman](https://krharriman.substack.com/p/review-of-the-resurrection-of-jesus-de8). Academic review: David Schweitzer, *Religious Studies and Theology* 41.1 (2022): 124–126 — [Equinox](https://journal.equinoxpub.com/RST/article/view/21207).
- [Skeptical] Gerd Lüdemann, *The Resurrection of Jesus* (Fortress, 1994), explains the appearances as visions arising from grief and guilt *(bibliographic, not fetched)*.

**Mythicism (the claim that Jesus never existed)**
- [Consensus] Ehrman, *Did Jesus Exist? The Historical Argument for Jesus of Nazareth* (HarperOne, 2012), argues that virtually no credentialed specialist doubts that Jesus existed, and treats Robert M. Price as the partial exception. Summary at [The Tech (MIT), 6 April 2012](https://thetech.com/2012/04/06/ehrman-v132-n16). Ehrman's main grounds are: Paul's letters (written within about 20–25 years) refer to Jesus' brother James (Gal 1:19) and to Peter, whom Paul knew personally; there are multiple independent Gospel traditions; and a crucified messiah is an embarrassing, unexpected claim that nobody would have invented *(argument summarized from Ehrman 2012; bibliographic)*.
- [Minority/Skeptical] Richard Carrier, *On the Historicity of Jesus: Why We Might Have Reason for Doubt* (Sheffield Phoenix Press, 2014), applies Bayesian reasoning and argues that Jesus began as a celestial figure who was later historicized *(bibliographic, not fetched)*. Carrier's hostile review of Ehrman's book is summarized at [Vridar, 20 April 2012](https://vridar.org/2012/04/20/richard-carriers-on-bart-ehrmans-did-jesus-exist/). A historicist response is Michael S. Heiser's [blog post](https://drmsh.com/bart-ehrman-smacks-jesus-mythicism/). These sources are partisan on both sides.

### Inferences
- The independent pagan evidence (Tacitus, plus the probable core of Josephus) confirms only the outline: Jesus existed, was executed under Pilate, and gave rise to a movement. Almost all the detail comes from Christian sources. The case for historicity rests mainly on Paul and on the multiple, independent strands of the Gospel traditions.
- On the resurrection, a Catholic/Orthodox-sympathetic author can fairly say three things: the *belief* is historically secure and very early; Wright and Allison are serious scholars who take the empty tomb seriously; and the inference to a miracle is a philosophical or theological step, not a historical-critical result.

### Gaps
- I did not open Schmidt (2025) or recent reviews of it in journals such as JSHJ or JTS, so I cannot say how specialists have received it.
- I did not fetch Pliny 10.96 or Suetonius online. The quotations follow the standard Latin.
- I found no independent academic review of Carrier (2014) in this session.

## 2. Early high Christology: Hurtado, Bauckham and the "Early High Christology Club" versus Ehrman and Bousset

### Takeaway
[Consensus trend since about 1990] Many specialists now accept that Jesus received cultic devotion (prayer, hymns, invocation of his name, baptism in his name) within the first two decades, in Jewish-Christian circles. That cuts against Wilhelm Bousset's model, in which a "Kyrios cult" arose on Gentile, Hellenistic soil. The live debate is over *what kind* of divinity this devotion implies and *how* it arose.

### Cited Findings
- [Older evolutionary model] Wilhelm Bousset, *Kyrios Christos* (1913; ET Abingdon, 1970), argued that worship of Jesus as "Lord" arose in Hellenistic Gentile congregations, under the influence of the mystery cults, and not in the Palestinian church *(bibliographic, not fetched)*.
- [Early-high model] Larry W. Hurtado, *Lord Jesus Christ: Devotion to Jesus in Earliest Christianity* (Eerdmans, 2003), argues that worshipping Jesus alongside the God of Israel was a major "mutation" in Second Temple monotheism. He holds that it was generated by visions of the risen Christ receiving heavenly worship, and expressed in prophetic oracles, songs and charismatic exegesis. Jewish intermediary figures (angels, personified Wisdom) offer partial precedent, but none of them became the object of a cult. Summarized by [Kok, "Critical Questions for the Early High Christology Club," Bible & Interpretation, 2015](https://bibleinterp.arizona.edu/opeds/2015/07/kok398030).
- [Early-high model] Richard Bauckham, *Jesus and the God of Israel* (Eerdmans, 2008), argues that early Christians placed Jesus within the unique "divine identity" (Creator and sovereign ruler). He builds this especially from Psalm 110 and Isaiah 40–55, and he denies that intermediary figures were real precedents — [Kok 2015](https://bibleinterp.arizona.edu/opeds/2015/07/kok398030). Martin Hengel is the third name usually associated with the "club" — ibid.
- [Gradualist/evolutionary model] Bart D. Ehrman, *How Jesus Became God: The Exaltation of a Jewish Preacher from Galilee* (HarperOne, 2014), argues that the earliest Christology was "exaltation" Christology (Jesus made Son of God at the resurrection, e.g. Rom 1:3–4). It developed into "incarnation" Christology (Paul, John), and Jesus was not at first considered God in the later sense. Contrasted with the EHCC's "big bang" model in [Kok 2015](https://bibleinterp.arizona.edu/opeds/2015/07/kok398030). Ehrman places Paul's Christ as an angel-like divine being (Gal 4:14) on a spectrum of Jewish divine beings.
- [Response to Ehrman] Michael F. Bird, Craig A. Evans, Simon Gathercole, Charles E. Hill and Chris Tilling, *How God Became Jesus: The Real Origins of Belief in Jesus' Divine Nature* (Zondervan, 2014). Bird objects that Ehrman's question assumes an evolutionary model — [Zondervan Academic excerpt](https://zondervanacademic.com/blog/how-god-became-jesus-bart-ehrman-high-christology-excerpt/); [Books at a Glance summary](https://www.booksataglance.com/book-summaries/how-god-became-jesus-the-real-origins-of-belief-in-jesus-divine-nature-a-response-to-bart-ehrman-michael-bird-ed/).
- [Critique of the EHCC from within the academy] Kok (2015) asks whether "divine identity" is a modern category, and whether Hurtado's appeal to religious experience can be tested — [Bible & Interpretation](https://bibleinterp.arizona.edu/opeds/2015/07/kok398030). Other critics include James McGrath (*The Only True God*, U Illinois Press, 2009) and James Dunn (*Did the First Christians Worship Jesus?*, WJK/SPCK, 2010). Dunn distinguishes reverence for Jesus from full cultic worship *(both bibliographic, not fetched)*.
- [Pagan corroboration of very early cultic devotion] Pliny, *Ep.* 10.96 ("a hymn to Christ as to a god"), c. 112 CE (see §1).
- [Primary NT evidence used by all sides] Phil 2:6–11 (often taken as a pre-Pauline hymn); 1 Cor 8:6 (the Shema reworked to include "one Lord, Jesus Christ"); the Aramaic *maranatha* in 1 Cor 16:22 (pointing to Aramaic-speaking invocation of Jesus, contrary to Bousset) *(primary texts; standard)*.

### Inferences
- [Catholic/Orthodox reading] The EHCC findings are welcome to a traditional reader. They imply that Nicaea (325) *articulated* a devotional pattern already present in the 30s–50s CE rather than inventing a new one. The cautious historical claim is narrower: the *practice* of worship was early, while the *ontological* clarification (homoousios) took three centuries and was contested.
- Ehrman and the EHCC agree more than the popular framing suggests. Both accept that Jesus was regarded as divine in *some* sense very early. They differ on what Jewish categories of "divinity" allowed and on the order of development.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve any 2016–2026 survey articles (for example in JSNT or NTS) that measure where the field now stands.

## 3. Growth rates: Stark, Hopkins, epigraphy, Constantine

### Takeaway
[Consensus, with caveats] No ancient source counted Christians. All figures are modelled. Stark's roughly 40%-per-decade model, from about 1,000 Christians in 40 CE to about 6 million (about 10% of the empire) by 300, is widely cited as *plausible arithmetic*. Its inputs and its assumption of steady growth are contested. Constantine's conversion (312) and patronage came *before* Christians were a majority, not after.

### Cited Findings
- [Stark] Rodney Stark, *The Rise of Christianity: A Sociologist Reconsiders History* (Princeton University Press, 1996). Starting from about 1,000 Christians in 40 CE, reaching about 6 million by 300 requires about 40% growth per decade (about 3.4% a year). Stark compares this with the early growth of the Latter-day Saints and attributes the growth to social networks, high fertility, better care during epidemics, and the status of women — summarized in [Ian Paul, Psephizo, "How fast should the church grow?"](https://www.psephizo.com/life-ministry/how-fast-should-the-church-grow/) and [Ehrman Blog, "The Rate of Christian Growth"](https://ehrmanblog.org/the-rate-of-christian-growth/).
- [Hopkins] Keith Hopkins, "Christian Number and Its Implications," *Journal of Early Christian Studies* 6.2 (1998): 185–226. Hopkins accepted a roughly 3.4%-per-year rate as plausible. He drew out what it implies: Christians were statistically tiny for the first two centuries (on the order of 7,000 around 100 CE and about 200,000 around 200 CE on this curve), which says something about how few literate leaders there were and how communities communicated — [Ehrman Blog, "Were Christians Statistically Insignificant in the First 200 Years?"](https://the-bart-ehrman-blog.castos.com/episodes/were-christians-statistically-insignificant-in-the-first-200-years); [Ehrman Blog](https://ehrmanblog.org/?p=11431). (The volume and page numbers are the standard citation, not checked against the journal page in this session.) Hopkins also asked who should count as a "Christian" at all — [Ehrman, "If You're Counting Christians, Who Counts as a Christian?"](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/25056750-if-you-re-counting-christians-who-counts-as-a-christian).
- [Critique] Ehrman (*The Triumph of Christianity*, Simon & Schuster, 2018) uses the same compound-growth framing. He stresses that the starting figure of 1,000 is a guess, that growth would not have been steady, and that the rate is only a long-run average — [Ehrman Blog](https://ehrmanblog.org/the-rate-of-christian-growth/); [Ehrman, "Exaggerating the Numbers of Early Christians"](https://the-bart-ehrman-blog.castos.com/episodes/exaggerating-the-numbers-of-early-christians). Robin Lane Fox, *Pagans and Christians* (Knopf/Viking, 1986), gave a lower estimate for the mid-third century (about 2%) *(bibliographic, not fetched)*.
- [Epigraphic data] Roger Bagnall's work on Egyptian papyri (personal names) and studies of the third-century Phrygian "Christians for Christians" inscriptions are the main material proxies. They suggest slow growth before about 250 and rapid growth in the fourth century. Stark (1996), ch. 1, used Bagnall's onomastic data as a check on his curve *(bibliographic, not fetched)*.
- [Constantine] Constantine's victory at the Milvian Bridge (312) and the so-called Edict of Milan (313, the Licinius–Constantine agreement), after Galerius' edict of toleration (311), came when Christians were a minority (on most models about 10%). Imperial patronage then sped up conversion. The standard account is Timothy D. Barnes, *Constantine and Eusebius* (Harvard UP, 1981) *(bibliographic, not fetched)*.

### Inferences
- [Lens split] Stark's sociological model naturalizes the growth; it requires no miracle, only ordinary compounding. A traditional reader can accept the arithmetic while still reading the steady growth under persecution as providential (compare Tertullian, *Apol.* 50: "the blood of Christians is seed"). Those are two levels of explanation, not competing data.
- Most of the empire became Christian *after* 312, which supports reading the late-antique Church as substantially shaped by the post-Constantinian settlement. That is a theme in Protestant and in some skeptical historiography.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch any post-2010 quantitative studies, such as statistical work on Christian onomastics or archaeological church-building counts, to update Stark and Hopkins.

## 4. Development of doctrine: Newman, Pelikan, Florovsky, Harnack

### Takeaway
How later doctrine relates to the apostolic deposit gets three very different answers. [Liberal Protestant/Harnack] It was *corrupted* by Greek metaphysics. [Catholic/Newman] It *developed* organically, the way a seed grows. [Orthodox/Florovsky] The Church receives the mind of the Fathers *in living continuity*, and the language of "development" is treated with caution. Jaroslav Pelikan's five-volume history is the standard descriptive account, and is written sympathetically to all three.

### Cited Findings
- [Newman, Catholic] John Henry Newman, *An Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine* (1845; revised 1878). In ch. 5 he gives seven "Notes" that distinguish genuine development from corruption: (1) Preservation of Type; (2) Continuity of Principles; (3) Power of Assimilation; (4) Logical Sequence; (5) Anticipation of Its Future; (6) Conservative Action upon Its Past; (7) Chronic Vigour. Ch. 5 §6: "a corruption is a development in that very stage in which it ceases to illustrate, and begins to disturb" the earlier acquisitions — [Newman Reader, ch. 5](https://www.newmanreader.org/works/development/chapter5.html). (In the 1845 edition the Notes were called "Tests"; the 1878 edition renamed them.)
- [Reception, Catholic] Newman's theory was viewed with suspicion at first. Some Catholic theologians, notably Orestes Brownson in America, thought it compromised immutability. It was later broadly absorbed, and Vatican II's *Dei Verbum* §8 speaks of tradition that "develops in the Church" and understanding that "grows" *(standard; vatican.va text not fetched)*. Owen Chadwick, *From Bossuet to Newman* (Cambridge UP, 1957; 2nd ed. 1987), is the classic reception history *(bibliographic, not fetched)*.
- [Pelikan] Jaroslav Pelikan, *The Christian Tradition: A History of the Development of Doctrine*, 5 vols. (University of Chicago Press, 1971–1989). Vol. 1, *The Emergence of the Catholic Tradition (100–600)*; vol. 2, *The Spirit of Eastern Christendom (600–1700)*. Pelikan, a Lutheran who became Orthodox in 1998, defines doctrine as "what the church believes, teaches and confesses" *(bibliographic, not fetched)*. In *Christianity and Classical Culture* (Yale UP, 1993) he argues against the strong Hellenization thesis, holding that the Cappadocians *transformed* Greek metaphysics as much as they adopted it *(bibliographic, not fetched)*.
- [Harnack, liberal Protestant] Adolf von Harnack, *Lehrbuch der Dogmengeschichte* (1886–90; ET *History of Dogma*), and *Das Wesen des Christentums* (1900; ET *What Is Christianity?*), describe dogma as "a work of the Greek spirit on the soil of the Gospel" (vol. 1 Introduction). They contrast it with Jesus' simple message of the Fatherhood of God and the infinite value of the soul *(bibliographic, not fetched)*.
- [Critiques of Harnack] Paul L. Gavrilyuk, *The Suffering of the Impassible God* (Oxford UP, 2004), argues that the "theology's fall into Hellenistic philosophy" narrative misreads patristic divine impassibility, which functioned as a qualifier and not as imported Platonism. Pelikan (1993) argues similarly, as do Hengel's studies of Hellenism *within* Second Temple Judaism (*Judaism and Hellenism*, Fortress, 1974), which undercut any sharp line between "Jewish" and "Greek" *(all bibliographic, not fetched)*. Benedict XVI's Regensburg Lecture (12 September 2006) criticized "de-Hellenization" programmes, Harnack's included *(vatican.va; not fetched)*.
- [Orthodox] Georges Florovsky launched the "neo-patristic synthesis" (a "return to the Fathers") at the First Congress of Orthodox Theology, Athens, 1936. He described it as a creative re-reception of the Fathers' insights addressed to the present age, not repetition — [Zizioulas Foundation glossary, "Neopatristic synthesis"](https://www.zizioulas.org/library/glossary/neopatristic-synthesis); [*Ortodoksia* (journal.fi), "Neopatristic Synthesis between Unity and Identity in Orthodox Theology"](https://journal.fi/ortodoksia/article/view/147725). Florovsky never defined the method rigorously, and critics have called its legacy anti-modern — ibid.; see also Paul Gavrilyuk, *Georges Florovsky and the Russian Religious Renaissance* (Oxford UP, 2013), and a related Gavrilyuk lecture text at [ecclesiagreece.gr (2024)](https://ecclesiagreece.gr/greek/press/theologia/material/2024_1_13_gavriluyk.pdf).
- [Orthodox vs Newmanian development] Orthodox writers (Florovsky, Vladimir Lossky in "Tradition and Traditions," in *In the Image and Likeness of God*, SVS Press, 1974; Kallistos Ware, *The Orthodox Church*, Penguin, 1963/rev. 1993) usually present Tradition as the life of the Holy Spirit in the Church. On this view doctrine is *expressed* or *defined* against heresy but not "developed" in content. Many apply this against Catholic dogmas such as papal infallibility (1870) and the Immaculate Conception (1854), which they see as developments failing Newman's own first note *(bibliographic, not fetched)*. One institutional source holds that Newman's patristic emphasis influenced Florovsky's ecumenical programme — [Eighth Day Institute, "Florovsky-Newman Week"](https://eighthdayinstitute.org/florovsky-week). This is an advocacy claim, not a scholarly finding.

### Inferences
- The Newman–Florovsky contrast is partly about vocabulary. Both reject Harnack's "corruption" model and both appeal to the Fathers. The substantive difference is over *criteria* (does the Roman see guarantee development?) and over specific post-1054 dogmas.
- Harnack's thesis in its strong form is now a minority position among patristic scholars. In softer forms (doctrine as a Greek-philosophical *translation*) it remains common in introductory historiography.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve a peer-reviewed article that directly compares Newman and Florovsky. The Eighth Day page is the only direct link found.

## 5. Canon, apostolic succession, episcopacy, the papacy's early history, and the seven ecumenical councils

### Takeaway
[Consensus] The four-Gospel core and Paul's letters were widely accepted by the late 2nd century (Irenaeus). The 27-book NT list first appears exactly in Athanasius' 39th Festal Letter (367). The monepiscopate (one bishop per city) is clearly attested in Asia Minor by Ignatius (c. 110) but probably arrived later in Rome (mid-2nd century). Roman primacy of honour and appellate weight is early. Universal *jurisdiction* in the later Western sense is a later development, and that is the central Orthodox–Catholic disagreement.

### Cited Findings

**Canon**
- [Primary source] Athanasius, *Festal Letter* 39 (367): §5 lists the four Gospels, Acts, seven Catholic Epistles, fourteen letters of Paul, and Revelation. §3 refers to "the books included in the Canon, and handed down, and accredited as Divine." §7 lists books "not indeed included in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read," among them "the Teaching of the Apostles" (generally identified with the Didache) and "the Shepherd" — [New Advent, NPNF² vol. 4](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2806039.htm).
- [Muratorian fragment, consensus or plurality] The traditional late-2nd-century Roman date rests on the fragment's statement that Hermas wrote "quite lately, in our times" while his brother Pius was bishop of Rome (c. 140–155). Everett Ferguson defended this date against Sundberg ("Canon Muratori: Date and Provenance," *Studia Patristica* 17, 1982), and Bruce Metzger judged that Ferguson had "sufficiently refuted (not to say demolished)" Sundberg — [C. E. Hill, "The Debate over the Muratorian Fragment and the Development of the Canon," *Westminster Theological Journal* 57.2 (1995)](https://gospelstudies.org.uk/earlychurch/pdf/fragment_hill.pdf); [Galaxie listing](https://www.galaxie.com/article/wtj57-2-09).
- [Minority, fourth-century date] Albert C. Sundberg Jr., "Canon Muratori: A Fourth-Century List," *Harvard Theological Review* 66 (1973), and Geoffrey Mark Hahneman, *The Muratorian Fragment and the Development of the Canon* (Oxford Theological Monographs, Clarendon, 1992), argue for an Eastern date around 375. Hahneman's book won support from Robert M. Grant, J. K. Elliott and others — [Hill 1995](https://gospelstudies.org.uk/earlychurch/pdf/fragment_hill.pdf); [Themelios review](https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/themelios/review/the-muratorian-fragment-and-the-development-of-the-canon-oxford-theological-monographs/); [Project MUSE review](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/244841/pdf). Jonathan J. Armstrong, *Vigiliae Christianae* 62 (2008), proposed Victorinus of Pettau (d. c. 304) as the author — [Hypotyposeis](https://hypotyposeis.org/weblog/who-wrote-the-muratorian-canon). Conclusion: the date is genuinely disputed. Do not present either date as settled.
- [Catholic/Orthodox reading] Canon is the Church *recognizing* inspired books through liturgical use and episcopal tradition. Catholics add the Councils of Rome (382, "Decretum Gelasianum," itself of disputed date), Hippo (393) and Carthage (397), and later Trent (1546). Orthodoxy never closed the Old Testament canon with a single conciliar act. Bruce Metzger, *The Canon of the New Testament* (Clarendon, 1987), is the standard mainstream survey *(bibliographic, not fetched)*.

**Apostolic succession and episcopacy**
- [Primary sources] *1 Clement* 42–44 (Rome to Corinth, conventionally c. 96) says the apostles appointed "bishops and deacons" and arranged for others to succeed them. In it, "bishops" (*episkopoi*) and "presbyters" are used more or less interchangeably. Ignatius of Antioch (*Smyrn.* 8; *Magn.* 6; *Trall.* 3) insists on one bishop with presbyters and deacons ("where the bishop is, there let the people be"). Irenaeus, *Adv. Haer.* 3.3.3 (c. 180), lists the Roman succession from Linus to Eleutherius, the twelfth from the apostles. Hegesippus (in Eusebius, *HE* 4.22) also compiled succession lists *(primary texts on newadvent.org / ccel.org; not fetched in this session)*.
- [Consensus] Ignatius' letter *to the Romans* names no bishop of Rome. Together with Hermas and 1 Clement, this suggests Rome was led by a college of presbyters until the mid-2nd century. Peter Lampe, *From Paul to Valentinus: Christians at Rome in the First Two Centuries* (Fortress, 2003; German 1987), argues for "fractionated" house-churches in Rome, with monepiscopacy emerging c. 150 (Anicetus). Allen Brent, *Hippolytus and the Roman Church in the Third Century* (Brill, 1995), argues similarly *(bibliographic, not fetched)*. Eamon Duffy (Catholic), *Saints and Sinners: A History of the Popes* (Yale UP, 1997; 4th ed. 2014), ch. 1, accepts that there was "no single bishop" in Rome for perhaps a century after the apostles, while defending the antiquity of Roman pre-eminence and the strength of the Petrine tradition at Rome *(bibliographic, not fetched; this is a widely reported reading of Duffy)*.
- [Minority/Skeptical] Walter Bauer, *Orthodoxy and Heresy in Earliest Christianity* (German 1934; ET Fortress, 1971), argued that in several regions "heresy" was earlier than "orthodoxy" and that Rome exported its form of Christianity. The thesis is widely criticized in detail (for example by H. E. W. Turner, *The Pattern of Christian Truth*, Mowbray, 1954) but remains influential (Ehrman, *Lost Christianities*, Oxford UP, 2003) *(bibliographic, not fetched)*.
- [Catholic/Orthodox reading] Both traditions read 1 Clement, Ignatius and Irenaeus as continuous with apostolic appointment, and the collegial-presbyterate stage as compatible with succession. Orthodox and Catholics agree on the sacramental episcopate. They disagree on the Roman see.

**Papacy and Orthodox critiques**
- [Orthodox] Nicholas Afanasiev, "The Church Which Presides in Love," in John Meyendorff (ed.), *The Primacy of Peter* (Faith Press, 1963; new ed. St Vladimir's Seminary Press, 1992). Afanasiev argues from "eucharistic ecclesiology": each local Eucharist is the whole Church, and Rome's early priority was one of "presiding in love" (Ignatius, *Rom.* inscr.), not jurisdiction — [OrthodoxWiki, Nicholas Afanasiev](https://en.orthodoxwiki.org/Nicholas_Afanasiev); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Afanasiev); [Edinburgh thesis on Afanasiev's ecclesiology](https://era.ed.ac.uk/handle/1842/30580). (I confirmed the 1992 SVS edition; the 1963 first edition is from standard bibliographies.) Afanasiev influenced Schmemann, Meyendorff and Zizioulas, and Vatican II observers. Catholic critics fault his rejection of "universal ecclesiology" — ibid.; [Catholic Books Review](https://catholicbooksreview.org/2013/afanasiev.html).
- [Orthodox] John Meyendorff, *Byzantine Theology* (Fordham UP, 1974), and *Imperial Unity and Christian Divisions* (SVS Press, 1989), argue that East and West shared a "primacy" which the West came to treat as divinely instituted jurisdiction, while the East saw it as conciliar and historically grounded (cf. Chalcedon canon 28, which ranks Constantinople after Rome "because it is the imperial city") *(bibliographic, not fetched)*.
- [Catholic] The traditional defence rests on Matt 16:18; Irenaeus *AH* 3.3.2 ("with this Church, because of its superior origin [*potentiorem principalitatem*], all Churches must agree"); Leo I's Tome and Chalcedon's acclamation "Peter has spoken through Leo"; and Pope Agatho's letter at Constantinople III. A. Edward Siecienski, *The Papacy and the Orthodox: Sources and History of a Debate* (Oxford UP, 2017), is the best even-handed survey *(bibliographic, not fetched)*.

**The seven ecumenical councils (recognized by both Catholic and Orthodox Churches)**
- Nicaea I (325: Arius, homoousios, date of Easter); Constantinople I (381: divinity of the Spirit, the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed, canon 3 on Constantinople's rank); Ephesus (431: Theotokos, against Nestorius); Chalcedon (451: two natures in one person; canon 28); Constantinople II (553: the Three Chapters); Constantinople III (680–81: two wills, against monothelitism, including the condemnation of Pope Honorius); Nicaea II (787: veneration of icons). Text and canons: Norman P. Tanner (ed.), *Decrees of the Ecumenical Councils* (Sheed & Ward / Georgetown UP, 1990); NPNF² vol. 14 on newadvent.org *(bibliographic, not fetched)*.
- [Lens note] The Oriental Orthodox (Coptic, Armenian, Syriac and others) accept only the first three. The Church of the East accepts only the first two. "Seven" is therefore a Chalcedonian (Byzantine–Latin) count. The condemnation of Honorius at Constantinople III is a standard Orthodox and Protestant argument against papal infallibility. Catholic responses (for example at Vatican I) argue that Honorius erred in negligence, not in an *ex cathedra* definition *(standard; not fetched)*.

### Inferences
- What the historical record can establish: Rome was early and unique in its prestige (the tombs of Peter and Paul, the imperial capital, its doctrinal steadiness), and other churches appealed to it. Whether this amounted to jurisdiction *iure divino* is a theological judgement the sources do not settle. Duffy (Catholic) and Meyendorff (Orthodox) agree on much of the first-millennium data and differ on its normative meaning.

## 6. The Schism: causes, 1054, 1204, Florence (1439), 1965, Ravenna (2007)

### Takeaway
[Consensus] Historians now treat "1054" as a symbolic marker within a gradual estrangement (c. 800–1204), not a single break. The causes ran together: the filioque, papal claims, azymes (unleavened bread), and linguistic and political drift after the Carolingians. The 1204 sack of Constantinople by the Fourth Crusade is widely seen as the event that turned estrangement into popular, lasting schism. Florence (1439) produced a union that the Eastern faithful rejected. The 1965 declaration and later dialogue (Ravenna 2007) removed the 1054 sentences but explicitly did not end the division.

### Cited Findings
- [Standard accounts] Henry Chadwick, *East and West: The Making of a Rift in the Church: From Apostolic Times until the Council of Florence* (Oxford UP, 2003). A. Edward Siecienski, *The Filioque: History of a Doctrinal Controversy* (Oxford UP, 2010). Tia M. Kolbaba, *The Byzantine Lists: Errors of the Latins* (University of Illinois Press, 2000), shows that Byzantine polemic often stressed *practice* (azymes, fasting, clerical beards and marriage) over doctrine. Steven Runciman, *The Eastern Schism* (Clarendon, 1955), introduced the "gradual estrangement" view *(all bibliographic, not fetched)*.
- [Causes] The filioque ("and the Son") was added to the Creed in Spain (Toledo III, 589), spread in Francia (Charlemagne, Aachen 809), and was resisted by Pope Leo III, who had the Creed without it inscribed on silver shields. It was adopted at Rome c. 1014. Photius' *Mystagogy of the Holy Spirit* and the Photian schism (863–867) form the Eastern case *(Siecienski 2010; bibliographic)*.
- [1054] Cardinal Humbert of Silva Candida laid a bull of excommunication on the altar of Hagia Sophia on 16 July 1054, against Patriarch Michael Cerularius. Cerularius' synod anathematized the legates days later. Pope Leo IX had died in April 1054, so the legates' authority is debated, and the sentences struck persons, not churches *(Chadwick 2003; bibliographic)*.
- [1204] The Fourth Crusade sacked Constantinople in April 1204 and set up a Latin patriarch. Jonathan Phillips, *The Fourth Crusade and the Sack of Constantinople* (Viking/Jonathan Cape, 2004) *(bibliographic, not fetched)*. In Athens on 4 May 2001 Pope John Paul II asked forgiveness for Catholic sins against the Orthodox and referred to the sack. Ecumenical Patriarch Bartholomew accepted the apology in 2004, on the 800th anniversary *(widely reported; not fetched in this session)*.
- [Florence] The Council of Ferrara-Florence (1438–39). The bull of union *Laetentur Caeli* (6 July 1439) affirmed that the Spirit proceeds from the Father *and* the Son "as from one principle," and declared the Greek formula "through the Son" compatible. It accepted either leavened or unleavened bread and affirmed the Roman pontiff's primacy "saving all the privileges and rights" of the Eastern patriarchs. Mark of Ephesus refused to sign. The union was repudiated in the East and formally rejected at a synod in Constantinople (1484). Joseph Gill, *The Council of Florence* (Cambridge UP, 1959); text in Tanner, *Decrees* vol. 1 *(bibliographic, not fetched)*.
- [1965, primary] The Common Declaration of Pope Paul VI and Ecumenical Patriarch Athenagoras I, 7 December 1965, was read simultaneously in Rome (at Vatican II) and in Istanbul. It regrets "the offensive words, the reproaches without foundation, and the reprehensible gestures" of 1054 (§4A). It removes the excommunications "from memory and from the midst of the Church," committing them "to oblivion" (§4B). It states that the gesture "is not sufficient to end both old and more recent differences" (§5) — [Vatican, Common Declaration](https://www.vatican.va/content/paul-vi/en/speeches/1965/documents/hf_p-vi_spe_19651207_common-declaration.html). The two men had met in Jerusalem in January 1964. On the 60th anniversary Cardinal Koch said the declaration ushered in an "ecclesiology of sister Churches" — [Vatican News, January 2026](https://www.vaticannews.va/en/vatican-city/news/2026-01/lifting-anathemas-60-years-paul-vi-anathagoras-koch-getcha.html).
- [Ravenna 2007, primary] The Joint International Commission for Theological Dialogue between the Catholic Church and the Orthodox Church adopted "Ecclesiological and Canonical Consequences of the Sacramental Nature of the Church: Ecclesial Communion, Conciliarity and Authority" at Ravenna on 13 October 2007. It was the commission's first agreed text since Balamand (1993). It says conciliarity and authority operate at the local, regional and universal levels, and it records agreement that Rome held the first place (*taxis*) among the patriarchal sees in the first millennium. It leaves open how the bishop of Rome's primacy should be understood and exercised. Rome's text notes that it is a commission document and "not an official declaration" of either Church's teaching — [Dicastery for Promoting Christian Unity, English text (PDF)](https://www.christianunity.va/content/unitacristiani/en/dialoghi/sezione-orientale/chiese-ortodosse-di-tradizione-bizantina/commissione-mista-internazionale-per-il-dialogo-teologico-tra-la/documenti-di-dialogo/testo-in-inglese.pdf); [USCCB](https://usccb.org/news/2008/08-084.cfm). The Moscow Patriarchate's delegation left the Ravenna meeting over a dispute about the Estonian Apostolic Orthodox Church and did not sign *(widely reported; not fetched)*. The North American Orthodox–Catholic Theological Consultation issued a response in October 2009 that took exception to the document's single footnote — [USCCB, response](https://www.usccb.org/resources/ravenna-response.pdf); [USCCB news 2009](https://usccb.org/news/2009/09-225.cfm). The commission's later Chieti document (2016), on synodality and primacy in the first millennium, continues this work *(not fetched)*.

### Inferences
- [Catholic reading] The schism is a wound inside one Church, and Florence shows that union is possible on the basis of the first millennium. [Orthodox reading] Rome departed from conciliar ecclesiology; Florence was imposed under military duress (the Ottoman threat) and rightly rejected. [Historical-critical] Politics (the Carolingian and Ottonian empires against Byzantium, the Normans in southern Italy, the Crusades) mattered as much as dogma, and lay identity in the East hardened after 1204.

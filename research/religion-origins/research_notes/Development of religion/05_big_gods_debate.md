# The quantitative "Big Gods" / moralizing-gods debate and its datasets

Scope: the empirical literature testing whether moralizing high gods (MHG) and supernatural punishment caused, or followed, the rise of large and complex societies. Covers theory, field experiments, phylogenetic tests, the Seshat dispute and retraction, critiques of the coding, and a dataset inventory for reanalysis. Research date: 2026-10-24.

Verification legend: **[verified]** means the bibliographic record (title, authors, venue, volume/pages, DOI) was confirmed against Crossref, OpenAlex or Europe PMC metadata in this session, or the repository/file was opened directly. **[secondary]** means the claim comes from a press or blog summary and not the primary text. **[unverified]** means I could not confirm it and the writer should check it before publishing. Nature, ScienceDirect and Taylor & Francis full texts returned 403 or a login redirect through the proxy, so numbers from those papers come from abstracts, open-access mirrors or the authors' own GitHub repositories, as marked.

## 1. Norenzayan's "Big Gods" (2013) and the 2016 BBS target article

### Takeaway
The Big Gods thesis is a cultural-group-selection account. Religious beliefs arose first as cognitive by-products. Later, cultural variants with increasingly powerful, moralizing, watchful and punishing gods, plus credible displays of commitment, were selected because they raised cooperation with co-religionist strangers and fertility, and so helped groups win intergroup competition. The thesis is explicitly co-evolutionary ("mutually energizing"). Strong one-way readings, "gods first, then complexity", are a simplification that later tests often attacked.

### Cited Findings
- Norenzayan, A., Shariff, A. F., Gervais, W. M., Willard, A. K., McNamara, R. A., Slingerland, E., & Henrich, J. (2016). "The cultural evolution of prosocial religions." *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 39, e1. DOI 10.1017/S0140525X14001356 [verified]. Abstract: a package of "increasingly potent, moralizing, supernatural agents, credible displays of faith, and other psychologically active elements conducive to social solidarity promoted high fertility rates and large-scale cooperation with co-religionists, often contributing to success in intergroup competition and conflict". The two developments (large-scale cooperation and the spread of prosocial religions over the last 10–12 millennia) "were importantly linked and mutually energizing". Religious beliefs "originally arose as nonadaptive by-products of innate cognitive functions" before particular variants were culturally selected. — [Europe PMC record, PMID 26785995](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26785995); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X14001356)
- The BBS article is a target article with open peer commentary. Commentaries and an authors' response were published with it in the same issue (vol. 39) [the count and names of commentaries are unverified, see Gaps]. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X14001356)
- Norenzayan, A. (2013). *Big Gods: How Religion Transformed Cooperation and Conflict*. Princeton University Press. This is the book-length statement of the thesis [title and publisher from my own knowledge; I did not fetch a publisher page this session, so the URL is unverified]. — [Princeton UP (unverified URL)](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691169736/big-gods)

### Inferences
- The BBS synthesis allows reciprocal causation. A finding that "complexity precedes MHGs" does not refute it outright, but it does refute the strong reading that MHGs were a prerequisite for large societies. Later critics (Lightner et al. 2023, below) argue that the relevant variable is moralistic supernatural punishment in general, not "high gods".

### Gaps
- Number and authors of the BBS commentaries, and the title of the authors' response: not retrieved, because the Cambridge Core page was not fetched. Check at the DOI.

## 2. Purzycki et al. 2016 *Nature*: random allocation game in 8 societies, and its follow-ups

### Takeaway
Across 591 people in 8 field sites, higher ratings of a moralistic god's punitiveness and knowledge predicted allocating more coins to distant co-religionist strangers in an anonymous, cheatable die-roll game. The effect held relative to both self and local co-religionists. The effect is correlational, and allocations stayed below the fair 50:50 split even at maximum punitiveness. The 15-site extension (Lang et al. 2019) found "small but robust" support. A preregistered 2023 reanalysis found that a god's explicitly moral concerns, as free-listed, do not reliably predict cooperation.

### Cited Findings
- Purzycki, B. G., Apicella, C., Atkinson, Q. D., Cohen, E., McNamara, R. A., Willard, A. K., Xygalatas, D., Norenzayan, A., & Henrich, J. (2016). "Moralistic gods, supernatural punishment and the expansion of human sociality." *Nature* 530(7590): 327–330. DOI 10.1038/nature16980 [verified]. — [Europe PMC, PMID 26863190](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26863190)
  - Sample: n = 591 participants, 35,400 observations. Eight communities: inland Tanna (Vanuatu), coastal Tanna (Vanuatu), Yasawa (Fiji), Lovu (Fiji), Pesqueiro (Brazil), Pointe aux Piments (Mauritius), Tyva Republic (Siberia, Russia), Hadzaland (Tanzania). Traditions included Christianity, Hinduism, Buddhism, animism and ancestor worship. — [abstract via Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26863190)
  - Result (abstract): "Holding a range of relevant variables constant, the higher participants rated their moralistic gods as punitive and knowledgeable about human thoughts and actions, the more coins they allocated to geographically distant co-religionist strangers relative to both themselves and local co-religionists." — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26863190)
  - Design: in the Random Allocation Game (RAG), each player got 30 coins and two cups. For each coin the player mentally picked a cup, then rolled a die with three faces of each of two colours. One colour meant "put the coin in the chosen cup", the other "put it in the other cup". Honest play gives roughly 50:50, so deviations measure favouritism or cheating. Players were alone and unobserved. Two versions were played: SELF vs DISTANT co-religionist, and LOCAL vs DISTANT co-religionist. — [Coyne 2016 summary, secondary](https://whyevolutionistrue.com/2016/06/08/belief-in-moralistic-gods-makes-people-generous-towards-coreligionists/); the design is confirmed in Bendixen et al. 2023 §2 (30 coins, fair die, cups) — [Bendixen et al. 2023 PDF](https://doi.org/10.1017/ehs.2023.15)
  - Effect sizes as relayed by a secondary summary: going from lowest to highest god punitiveness, allocations to DISTANT relative to the other cup rose by a factor of 4.8 in the SELF game and 5.3 in the LOCAL game. That is about two more coins for a "very punitive" god than for "don't know". Even at maximum punitiveness, mean allocations stayed below 15 of 30 coins, though the CI included 15. Error bars were bootstrapped 95% CIs (1,000 replications). — [Coyne 2016, secondary](https://whyevolutionistrue.com/2016/06/08/belief-in-moralistic-gods-makes-people-generous-towards-coreligionists/) [verify the 4.8 and 5.3 figures against the Nature text]
  - HRAF's Explaining Human Culture summary: knowledge beliefs were significant vs self (p < 0.01) and vs local (p < 0.05). Punishment beliefs were significant vs both (p < 0.001). Controls included material insecurity and number of children. Belief in divine reward did not account for the effect. — [HRAF EHC hypothesis 3488](https://hraf.yale.edu/hypotheses/3488)
  - Contemporary critiques (blog-level, not peer-reviewed): the design has no out-group (non-co-religionist) condition, so it cannot speak to religion's spread. External validity of a short game is unclear. A statistician commenter (Ed Kroc) argued that overlapping error bars and the "factor of 4.8/5.3" framing understate the uncertainty, and that the evidence rests on the final models. A Nature News & Views commentary noted that an unmeasured variable could explain the correlation. — [Coyne 2016 and comments](https://whyevolutionistrue.com/2016/06/08/belief-in-moralistic-gods-makes-people-generous-towards-coreligionists/); [search summary of N&V, secondary](https://henrich.fas.harvard.edu/resource/feature-nature-news-views)
- Data descriptor: Purzycki et al. (2016). "Cross-cultural dataset for the evolution of religion and morality project." *Scientific Data* 3: 160099. DOI 10.1038/sdata.2016.99 [verified]. It describes behavioural economic games plus surveys of demographics, religious beliefs, material security and intergroup perceptions, and "notes inconsistencies across sites". — [OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1038/sdata.2016.99); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/sdata.2016.99)
- Extension: Lang, M., Purzycki, B. G., Apicella, C. L., Atkinson, Q. D., Bolyanatz, A., Cohen, E., … Henrich, J. (2019). "Moralizing gods, impartiality and religious parochialism across 15 societies." *Proc. R. Soc. B* 286(1898): 20190202. DOI 10.1098/rspb.2019.0202 [verified]. N = 2,228 participants, 15 populations, RAG and Dictator Game (DG). "Higher ratings of gods as monitoring and punishing predicted decreased local favouritism (RAGs) and increased resource-sharing with distant co-religionists (DGs)." Out-group effects varied between sites. — [OpenAlex abstract](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1098/rspb.2019.0202). Note: the project GitHub README cites this paper under a different title ("Moralistic gods, extended prosociality, and religious parochialism…"), but the publisher metadata gives the title above. — [ERM repo README](https://github.com/bgpurzycki/Evolution-of-Religion-and-Morality)
- Preregistered null on moral concern: Bendixen, T., Lightner, A. D., Apicella, C., Atkinson, Q., Bolyanatz, A., Cohen, E., … Lang, M., & Purzycki, B. G. (2023). "Gods are watching and so what? Moralistic supernatural punishment across 15 cultures." *Evolutionary Human Sciences* 5: e18. DOI 10.1017/ehs.2023.15 [verified; open access, CC]. Game-specific N: RAG SELF 1,033; RAG LOCAL 1,028; DG SELF 1,077; DG LOCAL 1,066. Moral concern was measured by free-listing what angers or pleases the deity, modelled with Bayesian multilevel models (Stan via cmdstanr). Result: "gods' moral concerns do not play a direct, cross-culturally reliable role in motivating cooperative behaviour". There is support at some sites but not overall. Data and code: https://github.com/tbendixen/moral-freelist-econ. — [Europe PMC / PMC10426076](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/37587943); [PDF](https://doi.org/10.1017/ehs.2023.15)

### Inferences
- The individual-level evidence supports "punitive and monitoring gods" better than "moralizing gods" as such. Bendixen et al. (2023) separate the two and find the moral-content component does not carry the effect. This mirrors the macro-level shift from "MHG" to "broad supernatural punishment" (Watts et al. 2015).
- None of the game studies is experimental with respect to belief; all are cross-sectional correlations within sites.

### Gaps
- Exact odds ratios and coefficients from Purzycki et al. 2016 (Extended Data tables) could not be read because Nature full text was blocked. The 4.8/5.3 factors are second-hand.
- Lang et al. 2019 effect sizes: abstract only.

## 3. Watts et al. 2015 *Proc R Soc B*: Austronesian phylogenetic test (Pulotu)

### Takeaway
Using 96 Austronesian cultures and Bayesian phylogenetic models of correlated evolution, the authors found broad supernatural punishment (BSP) tends to precede and facilitate the rise of political complexity. Moralizing high gods tend to follow it. The MHG result rests on only 6 cultures with MHGs, and the Bayes factors for direction are modest ("substantial" at best against independence, below 3 against the unconstrained dependent model).

### Cited Findings
- Watts, J., Greenhill, S. J., Atkinson, Q. D., Currie, T. E., Bulbulia, J., & Gray, R. D. (2015). "Broad supernatural punishment but not moralizing high gods precede the evolution of political complexity in Austronesia." *Proc. R. Soc. B* 282(1804): 20142556. DOI 10.1098/rspb.2014.2556; open access, PMC4375858 [verified]. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4375858/)
  - Abstract: earlier cross-cultural support for the MHG hypothesis relied "on correlational analyses only and do not correct for the statistical non-independence of sampled cultures". The authors find "broad supernatural punishment drives political complexity, whereas MHGs follow political complexity", and suggest MHGs "diffused as part of a suite of traits arising from cultural exchange between complex societies". — [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25740888)
  - Coding (binary): political complexity is high if there are ≥2 jurisdictional levels beyond the local community (complex chiefdoms, states). An MHG is present if a god created or governs the cosmos, is active in human affairs and specifically supports human morality. BSP is present if a supernatural agent or process reliably monitors and punishes selfish acts, the belief is widely held, and it covers a broad range of selfish behaviours and community members. Cultures were coded from the earliest ethnographic records, and known Abrahamic borrowing was excluded. — [PMC4375858](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4375858/)
  - Method: RJMCMC in BayesTraits across 4,000 Austronesian language trees, comparing dependent with independent models of trait evolution by Bayes factor. Kass and Raftery thresholds: 3–10 substantial, >10 very strong. — [PMC4375858](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4375858/)
  - Counts: 22 high-complexity cultures, 6 with MHGs, 37 with BSP. — [PMC4375858](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4375858/)
  - Bayes factors: MHG dependent vs independent 3.60. MHG "complexity must precede MHG" vs independent 4.52, vs unconstrained dependent 0.92. BSP dependent vs independent 3.24. BSP "BSP must precede complexity" vs independent 5.02, vs unconstrained dependent 1.78. — [PMC4375858](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4375858/)
  - Authors' interpretation: BSP facilitates the rise of complexity but does not sustain it once established. Ancestral spirits may be a major source of BSP. — [PMC4375858](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4375858/)
- Related: in the Pulotu database, v7 "Belief in supernatural punishment for impiety" and v8 "Supernatural punishment for selfishness" are coded present/absent/missing. v8 requires the agent to have greater monitoring ability than humans and to reliably punish selfish individuals or their kin, and the belief must be widespread. — [Pulotu CLDF dist README](https://github.com/D-PLACE/dplace-dataset-pulotu/blob/master/dist/README.md)

### Inferences
- Read the direction claims as suggestive. None of the directional Bayes factors exceeds about 5 against independence, and against the unconstrained dependent model they are all below 2. The 6 MHG cases are too few for a robust test, which the authors acknowledge.
- The paper reframes the debate, moving the action from "high gods" to "supernatural punishment" from any agent. Lightner et al. 2023 and the Seshat RBB special issue both take up that reframing.

### Gaps
- I did not verify whether the Watts 2015 analysis files are separately archived (e.g. Dryad).

## 4. Botero et al. 2014 *PNAS*: the ecology of moralizing high gods

### Takeaway
With 583 Ethnographic Atlas societies, belief in MHGs (EA v34 = 4) was more likely in poorer, less productive environments and in politically complex societies with animal husbandry. Spatial proximity (neighbours' beliefs) was the strongest predictor, which points to diffusion. The "91% accuracy" is an out-of-sample AUC, not percent correctly classified.

### Cited Findings
- Botero, C. A., Gardner, B., Kirby, K. R., Bulbulia, J., Gavin, M. C., & Gray, R. D. (2014). "The ecology of religious beliefs." *PNAS* 111(47): 16784–16789. DOI 10.1073/pnas.1408701111; PMC4250141 [verified]. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4250141/)
  - Outcome: EA variable 34 dichotomised as MHG (category 4) vs not (1–3). Of 1,267 EA societies, 775 had religion data and 583 had complete covariates. Models were fit on a random two-thirds (389) and tested on 194. — [PMC4250141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4250141/)
  - Relative variable importance (Table 3): spatial proximity 1.00 (β = 5.867); political complexity 1.00 (β = 0.652); resource abundance 0.73 (β = −0.333); climate stability 0.48; animal husbandry 0.40 (β = 0.988); agriculture 0.33 (β = −0.716, weak); abundance × stability 0.25; language family 0.26. — [PMC4250141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4250141/)
  - "Predicts the global distribution of beliefs in moralizing high gods with an accuracy of 91%". The full text clarifies this is AUC = 0.91 on held-out societies. — [PMC4250141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4250141/)
  - Caveats the authors note: correlational; the language family random effect is a coarse proxy for phylogeny; climate data are from 1901–1950 and do not match ethnographic focal dates; agriculture and husbandry are collinear. — [PMC4250141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4250141/)

### Inferences
- The positive association with political complexity here is exactly the kind of EA/SCCS-based association that Lightner, Bendixen & Purzycki (2023) argue is inflated by the "high god" coding criterion.

### Gaps
- None material.

## 5. Whitehouse et al. 2019 *Nature* (Seshat), the Beheim et al. critique, the 2021 retraction and the 2022 "retake"

### Takeaway
The 2019 Letter claimed moralizing gods appear only after "megasocieties" of about 1 million people. Critics showed that 61% of the moralizing-gods data points were missing and had been recoded as "absent". Analysing only known data or imputing reverses the "forward bias". Nature published the critique as a Matters Arising (Nature 595, E29–E34, 2021). The authors retracted the Letter on 7 July 2021 (Nature 595: 320) while maintaining their conclusions were "still strongly supported". They republished a corrected, expanded analysis in *Religion, Brain & Behavior* 13(2) (online 29 June 2022, printed in the 2023 volume), alongside a new target article (Turchin et al.) and commentaries. Historians affiliated with the DRH separately attacked the quality of Seshat's coding and its expert vetting.

### Cited Findings

**The original Letter**
- Whitehouse, H., François, P., Savage, P. E., Currie, T. E., Feeney, K. C., Cioni, E., Purcell, R., Ross, R. M., Larson, J., Baines, J., ter Haar, B., Covey, A., & Turchin, P. (2019). "Complex societies precede moralizing gods throughout world history." *Nature* 568(7751): 226–229. DOI 10.1038/s41586-019-1043-4 [verified; now RETRACTED]. — [Europe PMC, PMID 30894750](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30894750)
  - Data: "414 societies that span the past 10,000 years from 30 regions", using "51 measures of social complexity and 4 measures of supernatural enforcement of morality". — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30894750)
  - Claims: moralizing gods "follow—rather than precede—large increases in social complexity"; "powerful moralizing 'big gods' and prosocial supernatural punishment tend to appear only after the emergence of 'megasocieties' with populations of more than around one million people"; and "rituals that facilitate the standardization of religious traditions … generally precede the appearance of moralizing gods". — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30894750)
  - Analysis code: https://github.com/pesavage/moralizing-gods (R; CC BY-NC-SA 4.0). It uses an `exportdat.csv` scraped from Seshat on 10 Jan 2018, 20 multiple imputations of complexity predictors, PCA to a single complexity score (PC1), and t-tests of complexity before vs after MG appearance plus logistic regression. The README states: "A version of this exportdat.csv file correcting values initially mislabeled as 'unknown' was uploaded and a correction notice submitted to Nature in February 2020." — [pesavage/moralizing-gods README](https://github.com/pesavage/moralizing-gods) [repo opened directly]

**The critique (Beheim et al.)**
- Preprint: Beheim, B., Atkinson, Q., Bulbulia, J., Gervais, W., Gray, R. D., Henrich, J., Lang, M., Monroe, M. W., Muthukrishna, M., Norenzayan, A., Purzycki, B. G., Shariff, A., Slingerland, E., Spicer, R., & Willard, A. K. (2019). "Treatment of missing data determines conclusions regarding moralizing gods." PsyArXiv, posted 2 May 2019, DOI 10.31234/osf.io/jwa2n [verified via OSF API]. Abstract: "61% of Seshat data points on moralizing gods are missing values, mostly from smaller populations below one million people, and during the analysis the authors re-coded these data points to signify the absence of moralizing gods beliefs. When we confine the analysis only to the extant data or use various standard imputation methods, the reported finding is reversed: moralizing gods precede increases in social complexity." The "megasociety threshold" is "solely a consequence of the decision to re-code nearly two-thirds of Seshat data from unknown values to known absences". — [OSF/PsyArXiv](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/jwa2n). An earlier circulated title was "Corrected analyses show that moralizing gods precede complex societies but serious data concerns remain". — [babeheim repo description](https://github.com/babeheim/moralizing-gods-reanalysis); [pesavage re-reanalysis README](https://github.com/pesavage/moralizing-gods-re-reanalysis)
- Published Matters Arising: Beheim, B., Atkinson, Q. D., Bulbulia, J., Gervais, W., Gray, R. D., Henrich, J., Lang, M., Monroe, M. W., Muthukrishna, M., Norenzayan, A., Purzycki, B. G., Shariff, A., Slingerland, E., Spicer, R., & Willard, A. K. (2021). "Treatment of missing data determined conclusions regarding moralizing gods." *Nature* 595(7866): E29–E34. DOI 10.1038/s41586-021-03655-4 [verified]. Note the change from "determines" (preprint) to "determined" (published). — [Europe PMC, PMID 34234331](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/34234331)
- Specifics from the reanalysis code repository (https://github.com/babeheim/moralizing-gods-reanalysis; R + Stan; CC BY-NC-SA 4.0; derivative of Savage/Turchin code, extended by Beheim, Lang and Spicer) [repo opened directly]:
  - The regression design matrix has 801 observations. 490 missing ("NA") outcome points were set to 0 (known absence), out of 502 zeros in total.
  - Only 1 of 30 regions (Middle Yellow River Valley) had a known MG absence preceding a presence, and the critics dispute that coding using DRH expert data. MGs generally appear in Seshat simultaneously with or after writing.
  - Estimating the "forward bias" from known observations only places the predicted first emergence of MGs about 958 years (SE 210) before their first observation in Seshat.
  - The roughly 40% jump in complexity just before MG appearance partly reflects conquered regions being assigned the conquerors' properties (Deccan, Kachi Plain, Sogdiana) or MGs arriving by mission (Kansai, Niger Inland Delta, Orkhon Valley). The remaining 6 NGAs show steady rises.
  - The social complexity score correlates with log population at Pearson r = 0.94 (n = 864 observations).
  - In observed societies below the median SC score of 0.42, the model predicts MGs appear in only about 2% (SE 1%) of cases per century.
  - — [babeheim/moralizing-gods-reanalysis README](https://github.com/babeheim/moralizing-gods-reanalysis)
- Savage's counter-reanalysis code: https://github.com/pesavage/moralizing-gods-re-reanalysis, described as "Reanalysis code for Savage et al.'s (2019) modification of Beheim et al. (2019)". It modifies 4 Beheim scripts and 1 Whitehouse script. — [repo README](https://github.com/pesavage/moralizing-gods-re-reanalysis) [opened directly]
- Timeline per Retraction Watch: Beheim's group responded immediately after publication and posted the preprint in early May 2019. Nature accepted the Matters Arising on 18 Feb 2021, about 21 months later. The Letter had 49 Web of Science citations at retraction. Beheim: the objection "wasn't so much their conclusion, but that they weren't transparent about the amount of missing data involved." — [Retraction Watch, 7 Jul 2021](https://retractionwatch.com/2021/07/07/critique-topples-nature-paper-on-belief-in-gods/) [secondary]

### Inferences
- The robust part of the dispute is methodological. With Seshat's coverage, the answer to "which came first" depends almost entirely on how missing moralizing-god codes are treated, since 61% of outcome points were missing and only one region had a known absence before a presence. Neither side's original causal conclusion is strongly identified from the 2019 data. The 2022 retake rests on an expanded, recoded dataset, which a reanalysis should use instead of the 2018 scrape.
- A shared confound appears in both camps' framings. MGs first appear in Seshat with writing, so earlier MGs in non-literate societies are structurally invisible. That biases any Seshat test toward "complexity first".

## 6. Lightner, Bendixen & Purzycki 2023: is moralistic punishment really tied to complexity? (venue verified: *Evolution and Human Behavior*, not *Nature Human Behaviour*)

### Takeaway
The positive association between "moralizing gods" and social complexity in SCCS/EA-based work is probably an artefact of the "high god" coding criterion, which requires a creator or director of the universe. That criterion produces false negatives for moralistic, punitive gods in small-scale societies, and does so more often at low complexity. The ethnographic record supports moralizing gods being prevalent in small-scale societies.

### Cited Findings
- Lightner, A. D., Bendixen, T., & Purzycki, B. G. (2023). "Moralistic supernatural punishment is probably not associated with social complexity." *Evolution and Human Behavior* 44(6): 555–565 (online 2022). DOI 10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2022.10.006 [verified; open access]. — [OpenAlex abstract](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2022.10.006); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2022.10.006)
  - Argument: the SCCS high-god variable "is defined by whether or not a god is the creator or director of the universe, regardless of power or omniscience". It has "led researchers to underestimate the presence of moralizing gods by systematically producing false negatives". Using datasets that code both moralizing gods and moralizing high gods, false negatives are inferred "more frequently among lower levels of social complexity", likely producing "a spurious positive association". "Ethnographic evidence supports the prevalence of moralizing gods among small-scale societies." — [abstract](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2022.10.006)
- Related open data and code: Purzycki & McKay (2023), "Morality, gods, and social complexity," in Purzycki & Bendixen (eds.), *The Minds of Gods*, Bloomsbury, pp. 122–132; code and data at https://github.com/bgpurzycki/moralgodschapter. Swanson's (1960) *The Birth of the Gods* data are digitised at https://github.com/bgpurzycki/Swanson_BirthoftheGods. — [repo READMEs](https://github.com/bgpurzycki/moralgodschapter) [opened directly]

### Inferences
- This critique applies directly to EA034 and Botero et al. 2014, and indirectly to any Seshat or Pulotu MHG variable that requires a "high" (cosmic creator or governor) god. A reanalysis should analyse "moralistic punishment by any agent" (Pulotu v8, Watts' BSP) separately from "high god" variables.

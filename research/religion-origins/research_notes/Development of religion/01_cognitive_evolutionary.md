## 1. Byproduct theories: agency detection, mentalizing, MCI concepts, teleology, dualism. What is the experimental evidence, and has it replicated?

### Takeaway
The byproduct ("standard model") claims come out unevenly under replication. The flagship causal experiment, "analytic thinking reduces belief" (Gervais & Norenzayan 2012), failed two high-powered replications. The "mentalizing deficit / autism → less belief in God" correlation failed a large replication (N > 67,000). Threat-driven hyperactive agency detection has had weak or null tests. Adult teleological bias (Kelemen) is the most robust finding. Belief in God tracks cultural exposure (CREDs) much more strongly than any single cognitive bias.

### Cited Findings

**Framing and classic sources**
- The byproduct view says religious beliefs are non-functional outputs of cognitive mechanisms that evolved for other jobs (agency detection, theory of mind, MCI memorability). It is described as the most popular view among CSR researchers: Pyysiäinen, I. & Hauser, M. (2010), "The origins of religion: evolved adaptation or by-product?", *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 14(3):104–109 — cited via [Wikipedia, Cognitive science of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_science_of_religion) (the TICS paper itself was not fetched).
- Founding books **[bibliographic, not fetched]**: Guthrie, S. (1993), *Faces in the Clouds: A New Theory of Religion*, Oxford UP (anthropomorphism / agency detection); Boyer, P. (2001), *Religion Explained*, Basic Books; Atran, S. (2002), *In Gods We Trust*, Oxford UP; Barrett, J. L. (2004), *Why Would Anyone Believe in God?*, AltaMira (listed at [CSCA](https://csca.ca/2015/11/16/csr/) and [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_science_of_religion)); Bloom, P. (2004), *Descartes' Baby*, Basic Books.

**Hyperactive agency detection (HADD)**
- Maij, D. L. R., van Schie, H. T., & van Elk, M. (2019), "The boundary conditions of the hypersensitive agency detection device: an empirical investigation of agency detection in threatening situations," *Religion, Brain & Behavior* 9(1):23–51, doi:10.1080/2153599X.2017.1362662. Six threat-induction experiments, total N = 233, testing whether ambiguous threat makes people falsely detect agents — [UvA repository](https://dare.uva.nl/id/aa4a7834-585c-47f6-b479-0d133bff8d6b); [PDF](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/32525276/2153599X.2017.pdf). (I did not read the results section in this session. My recollection is that the predicted threat effect did not appear, but treat that as unverified; see Gaps.)
- A 2024 virtual-reality study (N = 199) did not confirm that people falsely detect agents more often when they expect danger, which "casts doubt on HADD conceptualized as a threat-triggered mechanism." This is Szymanek et al. 2024, *Journal of Psychology of Religion and Spirituality* (per search snippet; full citation not verified) — [PDF](https://mcll.uj.edu.pl/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Szymanek-et-al_2024_j-psych-rel-spir.pdf).
- Correlational support: stronger supernatural beliefs go with a stronger bias toward illusory agency detection in perceptual decision tasks (van Elk 2013, 2015, as summarised in van Elk, M. (2022), "Proximate and ultimate causes of supernatural beliefs," *Frontiers in Psychology* 13:949131, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2022.949131) — [PMC9714272](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9714272/). The review gives no effect sizes.
- In Maij et al. 2017 (Study 4, US MTurk, N = 797), agency attribution measured with a Geometrical Figures Task, taken together with AQ and EQ, explained only R² = .03 of supernatural belief — [PMC5568287](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5568287/).

**Mentalizing / theory of mind / the autism finding**
- Original: Norenzayan, A., Gervais, W. M., & Trzesniewski, K. H. (2012), "Mentalizing deficits constrain belief in a personal God," *PLoS ONE* 7(5):e36880 — [PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0036880). Four studies:
  - Study 1: autistic (n = 12) vs neurotypical (n = 13) adolescents. Autistic adolescents were less likely to strongly endorse God (OR = .11, p = .046).
  - Study 2: Canadian students, N = 327. Autism-spectrum score predicted lower belief (β = −.13), mediated by Empathy Quotient (indirect b = −.07, 95% CI [−.14, −.01]).
  - Study 3: MTurk, N = 706 (OR = .80/SD; mentalizing indirect b = −.20).
  - Study 4: online panel, N = 452 (OR = .66/SD; EQ and Reading-the-Mind-in-the-Eyes both mediated).
  - Mentalizing also mediated the gender gap in belief (men lower), Studies 2–4.
  - Note the small clinical sample in Study 1 and the modest effects throughout.
- Large replication: Maij, D. L. R., van Harreveld, F., Gervais, W., Schrag, Y., Mohr, C., & van Elk, M. (2017), "Mentalizing skills do not differentiate believers from non-believers, but credibility enhancing displays do," *PLoS ONE* 12(8):e0182764, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0182764 — [PMC5568287](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5568287/). Five studies, total N > 67,000. Note that Gervais, a co-author of the 2012 original, is a co-author here:
  - Study 1 (Netherlands, N = 65,561): AQ did not add to demographics (OR 0.96, p = .103; Nagelkerke R² < .001).
  - Study 2 (NL, N = 588): AQ and EQ non-significant (model R² = .002).
  - Study 3 (Switzerland, N = 603): AQ non-significant.
  - Study 4 (US, N = 797; framed as a direct replication of Norenzayan et al. 2012): mentalizing measures together R² = .03, plus an exploratory curvilinear AQ effect that did not replicate in other samples.
  - Study 5 (62 Dutch adolescents, 33 with ASD): no difference in religiosity (d = .06).
  - By contrast, CREDs predicted belief strongly (OR ≈ 1.5 per scale unit; R² = .10 alone).
- **Discrepancy to flag:** van Elk's own 2022 review describes Maij et al. (2017) as having "replicated" a positive mentalizing–belief association that is small but "robust" — [PMC9714272](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9714272/). That conflicts with the 2017 paper's own title, abstract and reported statistics — [PMC5568287](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5568287/). Follow the primary paper.
- Jack, Friedman, Boyatzis & Taylor (2016, *PLoS ONE*, "Why do you believe in God?…") found that EQ did not predict supernatural belief once analytic thinking and moral concern were controlled, and that the Eyes test was not significant. This is as summarised in Maij et al. 2017 — [PMC5568287](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5568287/). The Jack paper itself was not fetched.
- Reddish, Tok & Kundt (2016) found very few differences between people with and without ASD on seven measures of religious belief and behaviour (as summarised in Maij et al. 2017 — [PMC5568287](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5568287/)).
- Schaap-Jonker et al. (2013) and Visuri (2020): people high on autism-quotient measures can still endorse supernatural beliefs and report supernatural experiences (as summarised in van Elk 2022 — [PMC9714272](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9714272/)).
- Neural evidence: personal prayer activates mentalizing-related brain regions (Schjoedt et al. 2009, as summarised in van Elk 2022 — [PMC9714272](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9714272/)). Reddish et al. caution that activity in theory-of-mind regions does not necessarily mean mentalizing is taking place (per [PMC5568287](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5568287/)).

**The integrated path model (Willard & Norenzayan 2013) and its replication**
- Willard, A. K. & Norenzayan, A. (2013), "Cognitive biases explain religious belief, paranormal belief, and belief in life's purpose," *Cognition* (vol. 129, pp. 379–391, **[pages not verified]**). The data fit best a path model in which mentalizing → dualism and teleology → religious, paranormal and life's-purpose beliefs (as summarised in the Willard et al. 2020 abstract) — [Brunel repository](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/handle/2438/17715).
- Replication: Willard, A. K., Cingl, L., & Norenzayan, A. (2020), "Cognitive biases and religious belief: A path model replication in the Czech Republic and Slovakia with a focus on anthropomorphism," *Social Psychological and Personality Science* 11(1):97–106. Nationally representative samples, total N = 2,022. Anthropomorphism was unrelated to belief in God overall but consistently related to paranormal belief. It predicted God-belief among the unaffiliated, and that relationship disappeared or reversed among Christian-affiliated believers — [Brunel repository](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/handle/2438/17715). (The session described this as a partial, culture-moderated replication.)

**Analytic thinking → disbelief (the "analytic atheism" hypothesis)**
- Original: Gervais, W. M. & Norenzayan, A. (2012), "Analytic thinking promotes religious disbelief," *Science* 336:493–496, doi:10.1126/science.1215647 (**[title/pages from memory; DOI confirmed via FLoRA page]**). Four experiments in which analytic-thinking primes (including viewing Rodin's *The Thinker*) reduced self-reported belief — [Sanchez et al. 2017, PMC5325262](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5325262/).
- Failed direct replication: Sanchez, C., Sundermeier, B., Gray, K., & Calin-Jageman, R. J. (2017), "Direct replication of Gervais & Norenzayan (2012): No evidence that analytic thinking decreases religious belief," *PLoS ONE* 12(2):e0172636. Preregistered, N = 941 (the OSF page says the true analysed N was 952). Result: d = 0.07 in the wrong direction, 95% CI [−0.12, 0.25] — [PMC5325262](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5325262/); [OSF erratum note](https://osf.io/qc6rh/wiki/home). Caveat from a commentary: it replicated only Study 3 (the *Thinker* manipulation) — [Mind & Culture](https://mindandculture.org/looking-back-on-social-foundations-hypothesis/).
- Failed in the Social Sciences Replication Project: Camerer, C. F. et al. (2018), "Evaluating the replicability of social science experiments in *Nature* and *Science* between 2010 and 2015," *Nature Human Behaviour* 2:637–644, doi:10.1038/s41562-018-0399-z. 13 of 21 studies replicated (62%), and replication effects averaged about 50% of the originals. FLoRA records the Gervais & Norenzayan replication as "failed" despite high power and feedback from the original authors — [FLoRA](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1126/science.1215647/); [Caltech record](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/91063/).
- Mixed: Finley et al. (2015, *PLoS ONE*). The negative relationship became non-significant when religious belief was measured before analytic thinking — [FLoRA](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1126/science.1215647/).
- Cross-cultural correlational test: Gervais, W. M., van Elk, M., Xygalatas, D., McKay, R. T., et al. (2018), "Analytic atheism: A cross-culturally weak and fickle phenomenon?", *Judgment and Decision Making* 13(3):268–274. N = 3,461 across 13 countries. The CRT–disbelief link "appear[ed] robustly only in aggregate analyses and in three individual countries" — [JDM](https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/journal/18/18228/jdm18228.html); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/453e4cc7f5194a47b701ff509761a39c). A 2019 *JDM* follow-up reports the UK as the only country with a significant positive CRT–belief correlation — [JDM 14(2)](https://www.sas.upenn.edu/~baron/journal/18/181017/jdm181017.html).
- Neural null results: Hoogeveen et al. (2020, preregistered fMRI) found no negative relation between religiosity and neural conflict responses; Farias et al. (2017) report similar nulls (as summarised in van Elk 2022 — [PMC9714272](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9714272/)).

**Minimally counterintuitive (MCI) concepts**
- Boyer, P. & Ramble, C. (2001), "Cognitive templates for religious concepts: Cross-cultural evidence for recall of counter-intuitive representations," *Cognitive Science* 25:535–564 (**[pages not verified]**). Free-recall experiments in France, Gabon and Nepal showed better recall for domain-level violations, with similar sensitivity across cultures — [WUSTL profile](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/cognitive-templates-for-religious-concepts-cross-cultural-evidenc/).
- Barrett & Nyhof (2001, *Journal of Cognition and Culture*): after three retellings, counterintuitive concepts were recalled better than bizarre ones, and bizarre better than intuitive — [Wikipedia, Minimal counterintuitiveness effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_counterintuitiveness_effect).
- Norenzayan, A., Atran, S., Faulkner, J., & Schaller, M. (2006), "Memory and mystery: The cultural selection of minimally counterintuitive narratives," *Cognitive Science* 30:531–553 — [PDF](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~schaller/Norenzayan2006CogSci.pdf).
- Definitional instability: Atran first treated two-expectation violations as maximally counterintuitive. Barrett (2008, *Method and Theory in the Study of Religion* 20:308–338) redefined 1–2 violations as MCI and 3+ as MXCI. Upal (2010) argues counterintuitiveness depends on context and fades with exposure — [Wikipedia, CSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_science_of_religion).
- Critique target article: Purzycki, B. G. & Willard, A. K., "MCI theory: a critical discussion," *Religion, Brain & Behavior* (2015/2016, **[year and volume not verified]**). They argue the theory has serious conceptual and empirical problems (the deep vs shallow inference distinction) and propose an alternative — [PDF](https://www.gwern.net/doc/philosophy/religion/2015-purzycki.pdf).
- A Rutgers dissertation describes the MCI literature as "mired in empirical contradictions and methodological shortcomings." Another study suggests elaborative processing, not domain violation as such, may drive the effect — [Rutgers](https://rucore.libraries.rutgers.edu/rutgers-lib/65324/); [Wikipedia MCI effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_counterintuitiveness_effect).

**Promiscuous teleology (Kelemen)**
- Kelemen, D. (2004), "Are children 'intuitive theists'? Reasoning about purpose and design in nature," *Psychological Science* 15(5):295–301. A review concluding that by about age 5 the "intuitive theist" description has explanatory value, and that children prefer purpose-based explanations of natural phenomena — [BU PDF](https://www.bu.edu/cdl/files/2013/08/2004_Kelemen_IntuitiveTheist.pdf).
- Kelemen, D., Rottman, J., & Seston, R. (2013), "Professional physical scientists display tenacious teleological tendencies: Purpose-based reasoning as a cognitive default," *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 142(4):1074–1083, doi:10.1037/a0030399 — [PubMed 23067062](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23067062/).
  - Under speeded judgement (about 3 s per item), physical scientists from top US universities accepted more unwarranted teleological explanations.
  - They endorsed fewer than comparison groups overall.
  - They did not differ from humanities scholars in Study 2.
  - Kelemen told press she does not regard the tendency as "innate or hardwired" — [BU news](https://www.bu.edu/cas/even-scientists-look-for-purpose-in-nature-bu-psychologist-concludes).

**Intuitive dualism (Bloom)**
- Bloom (2007, *Developmental Science*, "Religion is natural") proposes that dualism is natural. Bering & Bjorklund (2004) and Bering et al. (2005) reported early-emerging afterlife and dualist reasoning in children (as summarised in van Elk 2022 — [PMC9714272](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9714272/)).
- Counter-evidence: a 24-country study (> 10,000 participants; "submitted" at the time of van Elk 2022) found mental states judged more likely than physical states to continue after death. But the modal response for both was cessation, which fits "intuitive materialism" (Barrett et al. 2021) better than universal dualism — [PMC9714272](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9714272/).

### Inferences
- What has fallen is mostly the *causal-experimental* layer: priming analytic thinking, threat-induced agent detection, and individual-difference mentalizing effects. The descriptive claims (adults and children readily reach for teleological and agentive explanations) are better supported. A blog series should keep "humans find agentive/teleological explanations easy" apart from "these biases explain who believes."
- The repeated finding that CREDs swamp cognitive-bias predictors (Maij et al. 2017; Willard et al. 2020; van Elk 2022) suggests cognitive biases at most shape *what kinds* of religious concepts spread. *Whether* a given person believes depends mainly on cultural input. That matches Gervais & Norenzayan's own later "dual inheritance" framing and Norenzayan's co-authorship on the replication work.

### Gaps
- I did not read the results of Maij, van Schie & van Elk (2019) on threat and HADD. The claim that it found null effects is from memory and is unverified.
- I did not verify a large preregistered multi-site MCI recall replication; I don't know whether one exists.
- The exact citations (pages, DOI) of Bloom 2007, Bering & Bjorklund 2004, Jack et al. 2016 and Willard & Norenzayan 2013 were not fetched. The authors and years are corroborated by the reviews cited above.
- I did not check whether Many Labs 1–5 included any religion items. I believe none tested CSR effects directly, but this is unverified.

## 2. Adaptationist theories: costly signalling, ritual and cohesion, religious priming and cooperation, group selection

### Takeaway
The best-known observational and field findings are suggestive but small-N or correlational: Sosis & Bressler's communes, Xygalatas's kavadi and fire-walking studies, and Purzycki et al.'s Big Gods games. The flagship *experimental* claim, that implicit religious primes increase generosity (Shariff & Norenzayan 2007), has not survived preregistered replication. Explicit primes may work, but only among the already religious. The Seshat "Big Gods came after complexity" *Nature* paper was retracted.

### Cited Findings

**Costly signalling**
- Sosis, R. & Bressler, E. R. (2003), "Cooperation and commune longevity: A test of the costly signaling theory of religion," *Cross-Cultural Research* 37(2):211–239, doi:10.1177/1069397103037002003 — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1069397103037002003).
  - Historical data on 83 nineteenth-century US communes; the results "support aspects of the costly signaling theory."
  - A secondary summary gives 30 religious and 53 secular communes. More costly requirements predicted longer survival for religious communes but not secular ones — [ScienceBlogs](https://scienceblogs.com/cortex/2008/03/27/religion-and-tribal-cooperatio).
  - The widely quoted "39% of religious vs 6% of secular communes survived 20 years" figure comes via Haidt's *The Righteous Mind*. I have not verified it against the paper itself.
- Wikipedia also lists Steadman & Palmer's costly-signalling account and Slone's mating-strategy account — [Wikipedia, CSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_science_of_religion).

**Ritual, synchrony, cohesion (Durkheim's "collective effervescence")**
- Konvalinka, I., Xygalatas, D., Bulbulia, J., Schjødt, U., Jegindø, E.-M., Wallot, S., Van Orden, G., & Roepstorff, A. (2011), "Synchronized arousal between performers and related spectators in a fire-walking ritual," *PNAS* 108(20):8514–8519 (**[volume/pages from memory]**) — [PMC3100954](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3100954). Heart-rate recordings from N = 38 in a Spanish fire-walk. Heart rates synchronised between fire-walkers and *related* spectators, not unrelated ones, during the 30-min ritual — [Masaryk Univ. record](https://www.ics.muni.cz/en/research/publications/975514). The companion paper "Quantifying collective effervescence" (*Communicative & Integrative Biology* 2011) frames this as a test of Durkheim — [PDF](https://pure.au.dk/ws/files/48691093/Communicative_Integrative_Biology_2011_Xygalatas.pdf).
- Xygalatas, D., Mitkidis, P., Fischer, R., Reddish, P., Skewes, J., Geertz, A. W., Roepstorff, A., & Bulbulia, J. (2013), "Extreme rituals promote prosociality," *Psychological Science* 24(8):1602–1605, doi:10.1177/0956797612472910 (**[author list beyond Xygalatas from memory]**). Field study at Thaipusam, Mauritius, with 86 men (19 performing the high-ordeal kavadi). Performers *and* observers of the kavadi donated more to the temple than low-ordeal (collective prayer) participants, and perceived pain predicted larger donations — [Aarhus IMC news](https://interactingminds.au.dk/news/enkelt/artikel/imc-researchers-published-in-psychological-science); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/painful-and-extreme-rituals-enhance-social-cohesion-and-charity/). Note the small N and the quasi-experimental (non-random) assignment.
- Whitehouse's "modes of religiosity" (2004, *Modes of Religiosity*, AltaMira **[bibliographic, not fetched]**) and "identity fusion" (Whitehouse & Lanman 2014, *Current Anthropology*, "The ties that bind us" **[not fetched]**) are the main theoretical frames here.

**Religious priming and prosociality**
- Original: Shariff, A. F. & Norenzayan, A. (2007), "God is watching you: Priming God concepts increases prosocial behavior in an anonymous economic game," *Psychological Science* 18(9), doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01983.x — [FLoRA](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01983.x/).
- Meta-analysis: Shariff, A. F., Willard, A. K., Andersen, T., & Norenzayan, A. (2016; online 2015), "Religious priming: A meta-analysis with a focus on prosociality," *Personality and Social Psychology Review* 20(1):27–48, doi:10.1177/1088868314568811. 92 studies. The most optimistic overall estimate is g = 0.40 (uncorrected, all DVs); the bias-corrected estimate for prosocial behaviour is g ≈ 0.18. Effects held for religious participants, not non-religious ones — [van Elk et al. 2015, PMC4569810](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4569810/); [Frontiers 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01149/pdf).
- Sceptical reanalysis: van Elk, M., Matzke, D., Gronau, Q. F., Guan, M., Vandekerckhove, J., & Wagenmakers, E.-J. (2015), "Meta-analyses are no substitute for registered replications: a skeptical perspective on religious priming," *Frontiers in Psychology* 6:1365, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01365 (**[co-author list from memory]**) — [PMC4569810](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4569810/).
  - PET-PEESE bias-corrected estimates: full set (k = 92) −0.002; prosocial subset (k = 25) −0.169, with significant small-study slopes.
  - Bayesian bias correction still found an effect (posterior near 0.3).
  - They concluded a registered replication was needed.
- Failed preregistered replication: Gomes, C. M. & McCullough, M. E. (2015), "The effects of implicit religious primes on dictator game allocations: A preregistered replication experiment," *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 144(6):e94–e104, doi:10.1037/xge0000027. N = 650; no significant priming effect. A small meta-analysis of dictator-game priming suggested a mean effect not different from zero, with small-study effects — [PDF](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~memccullough/Papers/Religious%20Priming_GomesMcCullough_FinalProof.pdf); [FLoRA](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1037/xge0000027/). A Japanese replication (Miyatake & Higuchi 2017) is also recorded as failed — [FLoRA](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01983.x/).
- Billingsley, J., Gomes, C. M., & McCullough, M. E. (2018), "Implicit and explicit influences of religious cognition on Dictator Game transfers," *Royal Society Open Science* 5(8):170238, doi:10.1098/rsos.170238. Bayesian analyses supported the null for *implicit* priming, even among the religious. There was a small but reliable *explicit* priming effect among religious subjects only. Religious subjects gave more on average. Viewing God as punishing did not moderate the effect — [PMC6124061](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6124061/).

**Big Gods / moralising gods (cross-cultural and historical)**
- Purzycki, B. G., Apicella, C., Atkinson, Q. D., Cohen, E., McNamara, R. A., Willard, A. K., Xygalatas, D., Norenzayan, A., & Henrich, J. (2016), "Moralistic gods, supernatural punishment and the expansion of human sociality," *Nature* 530:327–330 (**[vol/pages from memory]**) — [author PDF](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~ara/Manuscripts/Purzycki%20et%20al%202016%20Moralistic%20Gods%20Supernatural%20Punishment%20and%20the%20Expansion%20of%20Human%20Sociality.pdf).
  - Eight field sites (Tanna ×2, Yasawa, Lovu, Pesqueiro, Mauritius, Tyva, Hadza), n = 591; random allocation game.
  - The more participants rated their god as moralistic, knowing and punishing, the more coins they allotted to distant co-religionists.
  - The study is correlational. The lead author warned against reading it as "religious people are nicer" — [Oxford press release](https://www.ox.ac.uk/node/188385); [RNS](https://religionnews.com/2016/02/21/christianity-islam-gods/).
- Norenzayan, A., Shariff, A. F., Gervais, W. M., Willard, A. K., McNamara, R. A., Slingerland, E., & Henrich, J. (2016), "The cultural evolution of prosocial religions," *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 39:e1. Target article with open peer commentary, arguing that cultural group selection explains the co-spread of large-scale cooperation and prosocial "Big God" religions over 10–12 millennia — [author PDF](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~ara/Manuscripts/Norenzayan%202016%20cultural%20evolution.pdf); [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/6A1CEFA11F5B46308242D4D47854D0D8). Example commentary: White, Sousa & Prochownik, "Explaining the success of karmic religions" (BBS 39:42–43), which argues the theory does not explain karmic, non-agentive religions — [QUB](https://pure.qub.ac.uk/en/publications/explaining-the-success-of-karmic-religions/).
- Seshat retraction: Whitehouse, H., François, P., Savage, P. E., et al. (2019), "Complex societies precede moralizing gods throughout world history," *Nature*, was **retracted July 2021**.
  - Beheim et al.'s Matters Arising showed that 61% of moralising-god observations were coded "unknown" and treated as absent. A minimal correction moved first appearances back by a century and reversed the main result — [Retraction Watch](https://retractionwatch.com/2021/07/07/critique-topples-nature-paper-on-belief-in-gods/); [HRAF record](https://hraf.yale.edu/ehc/documents/1225).
  - The authors' revised analysis, "Testing the Big Gods hypothesis with global historical data: a review and 'retake'," *Religion, Brain & Behavior* (online 2021/2022; vol. 13(2)), maintains that sociopolitical complexity precedes Big Gods — [Turchin](https://peterturchin.com/publications/testing-the-big-gods-hypothesis-with-global-historical-data-a-review-and-retake/); [CSH](https://csh.ac.at/publication/testing-the-big-gods-hypothesis-with-global-historical-data-a-review-and-retake/); [LSE](https://www.lse.ac.uk/news/latest-news-from-lse/g-july-21/a-fresh-look-at-the-origins-of-moralising-religions).

**Group selection (D. S. Wilson)**
- Wilson, D. S. (2002), *Darwin's Cathedral: Evolution, Religion, and the Nature of Society*, University of Chicago Press. Religion as a group-level adaptation spread largely by cultural group selection; the central case study is Calvin's Geneva. Wikipedia describes it as generally seen as outside mainstream CSR — [Christian Century review](https://www.christiancentury.org/node/28981); [Wikipedia, CSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_science_of_religion).
- Critiques: Pinker, S. (2012), "The false allure of group selection," *Edge* (with replies from Wilson and others in the Reality Club) — [Edge](https://edge.org/node/21475). Jerry Coyne's *TLS* review of *Darwin's Cathedral* is critical of cultural group selection as an explanation of religion's spread — [Coyne blog](https://whyevolutionistrue.com/2014/12/07/my-tls-review-of-darwins-cathedral/).

### Inferences
- The adaptationist evidence is strongest where it is *ethnographic or historical and correlational*: communes, field games, ritual physiology. It is weakest where it was meant to be *causal and experimental*: priming. The field shifted from "religion causes prosociality in individuals" to "certain culturally evolved religious packages stabilise cooperation among strangers," which is a group-level, historical claim and harder to test.
- The Seshat retraction shows that the historical tests depend heavily on how missing data are coded. Both pro- and anti-Big-Gods conclusions from cross-cultural databases deserve caution.

### Gaps
- I did not obtain Sosis & Bressler's exact survival statistics from the full text.
- I found no large preregistered replication of Xygalatas et al. 2013 or Konvalinka et al. 2011.
- Galen (2012, *Psychological Bulletin*, "Does religious belief promote prosociality? A critical examination") is a major critique that I did not fetch.
- The Whitehouse et al. 2023 *Nature* paper on Seshat and ritual/Big Gods could not be verified in this session.

## 3. Cultural evolution: credibility-enhancing displays (CREDs) and dual-inheritance accounts

### Takeaway
CREDs (Henrich 2009) began as a formal model and are now among the best-supported predictors of individual belief. They consistently outperform cognitive-bias measures in the same samples, though the evidence is mostly retrospective self-report and correlational. Dual-inheritance theory (Atran & Henrich 2010; Norenzayan et al. 2016) folds byproduct cognition into a cultural-selection story, and this is now the field's dominant synthesis.

### Cited Findings
- Henrich, J. (2009), "The evolution of costly displays, cooperation and religion: credibility enhancing displays and their implications for cultural evolution," *Evolution and Human Behavior* 30:244–260 — [author PDF](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~henrich/pdfs/evolution%20of%20costly%20displays%20_henrich%202009.pdf). Learners guard against manipulation by preferentially adopting beliefs from models whose displays would be costly if insincere. The model yields a stable equilibrium of costly display and links it to ritual, within-group cooperation and between-group competition. A later agent-based study (JASSS 14(3)) finds this equilibrium coexists with a no-cost equilibrium depending on initial conditions, and calls the whole-population result "quite unrealistic" as a description — [JASSS](https://www.jasss.org/14/3/6.html).
- Lanman, J. A. & Buhrmester, M. D. (2017), "Religious actions speak louder than words: Exposure to credibility-enhancing displays predicts theism," *Religion, Brain & Behavior* 7(1):3–16, doi:10.1080/2153599X.2015.1117011. N = 316 US adults. Retrospective CRED exposure predicted certainty in God's existence; low exposure predicted certainty in non-existence (per press coverage; full text not read) — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/fascinating-concept-help-us-better-understand-belief-god-widespread/); [QUB](https://pure.qub.ac.uk/en/clippings/new-york-magazine-religion-spreads-through-actions-more-than-word/).
- Maij et al. 2017 (above): CREDs gave OR ≈ 1.5 per unit and explained R² ≈ .10, against R² ≤ .03 for mentalizing — [PMC5568287](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5568287/). van Elk (2022) calls parental CREDs the strongest predictor of supernatural belief, stronger than thinking style, agency detection or mentalizing — [PMC9714272](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9714272/).
- Follow-up on CRUDs (credibility-undermining displays, e.g. clergy scandals): Turpin, Andersen & Lanman, "CREDs, CRUDs and Catholic scandals: experimentally examining the effects…" (QUB record). They note there is "currently little causal evidence" that religious authorities' behaviour shifts pre-existing belief — [QUB](https://pure.qub.ac.uk/en/publications/creds-cruds-and-catholic-scandals-experimentally-examining-the-ef/).
- Dual inheritance: Atran, S. & Henrich, J. (2010), "The evolution of religion: how cognitive by-products, adaptive learning heuristics, ritual displays, and group competition generate deep commitments to prosocial religions," *Biological Theory* 5(1):18–30 — [author PDF](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~henrich/pdfs/atran_henrich_evolutionofreligion.pdf). Wikipedia's date for this is inconsistent (2010 vs 2015) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_science_of_religion).
- Gervais, Willard, Norenzayan & Henrich (2011), "The cultural transmission of faith," *Religion* — [author PDF](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~ara/Manuscripts/Gervais%20Willard%20Norenzayan%20Henrich%202011%20RELIGION.pdf).

### Inferences
- The CRED literature is where the major CSR figures themselves (Norenzayan, Gervais, van Elk) moved after the cognitive-bias replication problems. For a historical series, CREDs offer a testable mechanism for why martyrdom, asceticism and costly ritual recur in successful traditions (e.g. early Christian martyr narratives). Direct historical tests of that mechanism remain thin.

### Gaps
- I did not find a preregistered, longitudinal or experimental CRED study that establishes causation in childhood.

## 4. Heritability of religiosity: twin estimates and age effects

### Takeaway
Twin studies consistently find moderate heritability of *religiousness*: roughly 30–50% in adulthood, with shared environment small in adults. *Religious affiliation* is mostly culturally transmitted. Heritability rises from adolescence (when shared family environment dominates) to adulthood.

### Cited Findings
- Waller, N. G., Kojetin, B. A., Bouchard, T. J., Lykken, D. T., & Tellegen, A. (1990), "Genetic and environmental influences on religious interests, attitudes, and values: A study of twins reared apart and together," *Psychological Science* 1:138–142. 53 MZA and 31 DZA pairs from the Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart, plus reared-together twins. Reared-apart MZ twins were more similar than DZ twins on all religiosity scales. (A secondary claim of "about 50% of variance" is unverified against the paper) — [UMN record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/genetic-and-environmental-influences-on-religious-interests-attit/); [PDF](https://www.gwern.net/doc/genetics/heritable/1990-waller.pdf).
- Koenig, L. B., McGue, M., Krueger, R. F., & Bouchard, T. J. (2005), "Genetic and environmental influences on religiousness: Findings for retrospective and current religiousness ratings," *Journal of Personality* 73(2):471–488 (**[pages from memory]**). Adult male twins (169 MZ, 104 DZ pairs, mean age 33).
  - Heritability: 12% for retrospective adolescent religiousness vs 44% for current adult religiousness.
  - Shared environment: 56% vs 18%.
  - Current MZ r = .62, DZ r = .42.
  - Limitations: retrospective adolescent reports; male-only sample.
  - [UMN record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/genetic-and-environmental-influences-on-religiousness-findings-fo/).
- Koenig et al. (2008) extended this to stability and change in emerging adulthood (including female twins) — [PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/philosophy/religion/2008-koenig.pdf) (not read in detail).
- Vance, T., Maes, H. H., & Kendler, K. S. (2010), "Genetic and environmental influences on multiple dimensions of religiosity: A twin study," *Journal of Nervous and Mental Disease* 198(10):755–761. Seven religiosity factors from 78 items in adult male and female twins. Variance was mostly additive genetic plus unique environment, with little common environment; a single common genetic factor influenced all seven — [PDF](https://www.gwern.net/doc/genetics/heritable/correlation/2010-vance.pdf); [PMC listing](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/pmid/20161346). Vance et al. (2014, same journal, vol. 202) found religiosity factors negatively correlated with alcohol dependence through shared genetic effects — [PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/genetics/heritable/correlation/2014-vance.pdf).
- Bouchard et al. (1999), "Intrinsic and extrinsic religiousness: genetic and environmental influences and personality correlates," *Twin Research* — [UMN record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/intrinsic-and-extrinsic-religiousness-genetic-and-environmental-i/) (not read).

### Inferences
- The age pattern (family shapes adolescent religiosity; adults drift towards a genetically influenced set point) fits CREDs and cultural learning shaping early belief while temperament shapes how far adults keep it up. Heritability is population- and era-specific (mostly US/Minnesota/Virginia samples) and says nothing about *which* religion people hold.

### Gaps
- I did not locate a recent meta-analysis of religiosity twin studies or GWAS/polygenic-score results; I don't know whether any are robust.
- I did not verify Kendler, Gardner & Prescott (1997, *American Journal of Psychiatry*) on "personal devotion" heritability (~29% from memory).

## 5. Developmental evidence: are children "born believers"?

### Takeaway
There is a real dispute. Barrett and Kelemen argue that children's default agency, teleology and mind-concepts make them receptive to gods ("intuitive theists," *Born Believers*). Bloom, Banerjee, Harris and Corriveau argue that children show biases *receptive* to religious ideas but not spontaneous theism, and that the content of belief depends on testimony and culture.

### Cited Findings
- Barrett, J. L., Richert, R. A., & Driesenga, A. (2001), "God's beliefs versus mother's: The development of nonhuman agent concepts," *Child Development* 72(1):50–65 — [citation confirmed via Cambridge listing](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/science-of-childrens-religious-and-spiritual-development/EC5AD95722154A742516779AB50D5940). (Findings not read. From memory: in a false-belief paradigm, older preschoolers attributed false beliefs to mother but not to God. **Unverified.**)
- Knight, Sousa, Barrett & Atran (2004), "Children's attributions of beliefs to humans and God," *Cognitive Science* 28(1):117–126 (listed by [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_science_of_religion); not read).
- Barrett, J. L. (2012), *Born Believers: The Science of Children's Religious Belief*, Free Press — [Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9781439196540).
- Kelemen (2004), "intuitive theists" — see section 1 — [BU PDF](https://www.bu.edu/cdl/files/2013/08/2004_Kelemen_IntuitiveTheist.pdf).
- Banerjee, K. & Bloom, P. (2013), "Would Tarzan believe in God? Conditions for the emergence of religious belief," *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 17(1):7–8. They argue children lack spontaneous theistic views and that "the emergence of religion is crucially dependent on culture." If universal biases produced religion in each mind, spontaneous cases should appear, and such cases are "as best we know, non-existent" — [summary with quotes](https://mindblog.dericbownds.net/2013/03/would-tarzan-believe-in-god.html); [Coyne commentary](https://whyevolutionistrue.com/2013/03/19/is-religion-hardwired/). Coyne notes the article gives no citations for its main empirical claim.
- Corriveau, K. H., Chen, E. E., & Harris, P. L. (2015), "Judgments about fact and fiction by children from religious and nonreligious backgrounds," *Cognitive Science* 39(2):353–382 (**[pages from memory]**) — [BU PDF](https://www.bu.edu/learninglab/files/2015/10/Corriveau-Chen-Harris-2015.pdf).
  - 5–6-year-olds all judged characters in realistic stories real.
  - Children with church or parochial-school exposure judged protagonists of religious (miracle) stories real; secular children judged them fictional.
  - Secular children were also more likely to judge fantastical stories fictional.
  - This is evidence that testimony and exposure drive the content. Critics objected to press framing and possible confounds (e.g. biblical names) — [Christian Post](https://assets.christianpost.com/news/did-study-really-show-that-christian-kids-are-more-gullible.html); [Synapses](https://www.synapses.co.za/children-religion-distinguishing-fact-fiction/).
- Shook, J. (2017), "Are people born to be believers, or are gods born to be believed?", *Method & Theory in the Study of Religion*. He argues innate god-belief has not been empirically confirmed; a reply in the same journal defends the innateness hypothesis — [relbib](https://www.relbib.de/Record/1566785294); [reply](https://www.relbib.de/Record/1566785316).
- Legare & Gelman (2008, *Cognitive Science* 32(4):607–642): natural and supernatural explanations *coexist* and can increase with age (listed via [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_science_of_religion); not read).

### Inferences
- Both camps agree children have early agentive and teleological biases. The dispute is whether "belief" is the right word without cultural input. The cross-cultural "intuitive materialism" afterlife data (van Elk 2022) and Corriveau et al. weaken a strong "born believer" claim. A weaker "born receptive" claim remains widely accepted.

### Gaps
- I did not read Barrett's direct reply to Banerjee & Bloom; I don't know whether one exists.
- I did not verify findings of Lane, Wellman & Evans (2010, *Child Development*) on children's God-concepts and theory of mind.

## 6. Major critiques of CSR as a field, and of the byproduct/adaptation dichotomy

### Takeaway
The main internal critiques concern: (a) a replication crisis that hit CSR's signature experiments; (b) WEIRD sampling (analytic atheism varied by country); (c) a loose theoretical core; and (d) a false dichotomy, since most current theorists (Atran & Henrich; Norenzayan et al.) combine byproduct cognition with cultural (not genetic) adaptation.

### Cited Findings
- Replication record: see sections 1–2 (Sanchez et al. 2017; Camerer et al. 2018; Gomes & McCullough 2015; Billingsley et al. 2018; Maij et al. 2017; van Elk et al. 2015).
- Cross-cultural fickleness: Gervais et al. 2018 (13 countries) — [JDM](https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/journal/18/18228/jdm18228.html). Anthropomorphism–God belief reversed among Christian-affiliated Czechs and Slovaks — [Willard et al. 2020](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/handle/2438/17715).
- Field cohesion: CSR "remains somewhat loosely defined." Cohesion comes from a shared willingness to view religion in cognitive and evolutionary terms rather than shared detailed theory (Van Eyghen, *Religion Compass*, cited on [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_science_of_religion); the date given there could not be checked).
- Adaptationist critics of the byproduct consensus: Johnson, D. (2016), *God Is Watching You*, Oxford UP; Szocik, K. & Van Eyghen, H. (2021), *Revising Cognitive and Evolutionary Science of Religion: Religion as an Adaptation*, Springer — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_science_of_religion) (books not read).
- Synthesis dissolving the dichotomy: Atran & Henrich (2010) — [PDF](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~henrich/pdfs/atran_henrich_evolutionofreligion.pdf); Norenzayan et al. (2016, BBS) — [PDF](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~ara/Manuscripts/Norenzayan%202016%20cultural%20evolution.pdf).
- van Elk (2022) concludes that socio-cultural factors (CREDs, source heuristics) are more central than cognitive mechanisms — [PMC9714272](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9714272/).
- Data-quality critique of the historical tests: Beheim et al.'s critique and the Seshat retraction — [Retraction Watch](https://retractionwatch.com/2021/07/07/critique-topples-nature-paper-on-belief-in-gods/).
- Theoretical critique of MCI: Purzycki & Willard — [PDF](https://www.gwern.net/doc/philosophy/religion/2015-purzycki.pdf).
- Karmic and non-theistic religions are underexplained by Big-Gods theory: White, Sousa & Prochownik (BBS commentary) — [QUB](https://pure.qub.ac.uk/en/publications/explaining-the-success-of-karmic-religions/).

### Inferences
- In a decade the field's centre of gravity moved from "cognitive biases make religion natural" to "cultural learning (CREDs, testimony) and group-level cultural evolution do most of the work; cognitive biases constrain content." Even the critics are mostly CSR insiders, which suggests a self-correcting field rather than a discredited one.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch humanities-side critiques, e.g. from religious-studies scholars who object to treating "religion" as a natural kind (Martin & Wiebe; Watts & Turner eds. 2014, *Evolution, Religion, and Cognitive Science: Critical and Constructive Essays*, OUP). These exist to my knowledge but are **unverified** in this session.
- I did not verify Bellah (2011) *Religion in Human Evolution* (Harvard UP) as a critique or alternative.

## 7. Do these accounts bear on the truth of religious claims? (genetic fallacy, debunking arguments, Barrett and others)

### Takeaway
Philosophers on both sides largely agree that a causal-evolutionary explanation of a belief does not by itself show the belief is false; inferring that would be the genetic fallacy. The live question is whether a *modified* debunking argument works: that the mechanisms are "off-track," unreliable or falsehood-prone. Theist CSR researchers (notably Justin Barrett, a Christian) and Reformed epistemologists argue that CSR is compatible with theism, or even fits a "natural knowledge of God." Others argue it undercuts justification. This section reports the positions without adjudicating them.

### Cited Findings
- Jong, J. & Visala, A. (2014), "Evolutionary debunking arguments against theism, reconsidered," *International Journal for Philosophy of Religion* 76(3):243–258. Prima facie, debunking arguments "commit the genetic fallacy, conflating the context of discovery and the context of justification." The paper then tests whether modified versions (e.g. positing falsehood-tracking mechanisms) escape that charge, and whether religious belief lacks the escape from general evolutionary scepticism that commonsense and scientific beliefs have — [Coventry repository](https://pureportal.coventry.ac.uk/en/publications/evolutionary-debunking-arguments-against-theism-reconsidered/); [PhilPapers](https://api.philpapers.org/rec/JONEDA).
- Clark, K. J. & Barrett, J. L. (2011), "Reidian religious epistemology and the cognitive science of religion," *Journal of the American Academy of Religion* 79(3):639–675 — [ISIS record](https://data.isiscb.org/isis/authority/CBA000019243/); [catalogue](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/asn,65092658). The companion paper, Clark & Barrett (2010), "Reformed epistemology and the cognitive science of religion," *Faith and Philosophy* 27, argues the two fields "have remarkably converged on belief in God" — [relbib](https://www.relbib.de/Record/164683447X?lng=en). (Abstracts only; the argument that natural, non-inferential god-belief can be properly basic, after Reid and Plantinga, is described here from secondary snippets.)
- Barrett, J. L. (2011), *Cognitive Science, Religion, and Theology: From Human Minds to Divine Minds*, Templeton Press. It reviews CSR findings and their implications for theology. An endorsement by David G. Myers praises it for explaining why a natural cognitive basis for a belief need not discount it (endorser's wording, not Barrett's) — [UBC Press](https://www.ubcpress.ca/cognitive-science-religion-and-theology). Barrett was at Fuller Theological Seminary — [CSCA](https://csca.ca/2015/11/16/csr/).
- Survey of the debate: Wikipedia's "Evolutionary debunking" entry lists the main positions — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_debunking). A 2023 *International Journal for Philosophy of Religion* article lists Goldberg & Murray (2009) and Murray (2009) among general discussions of debunking — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11153-023-09882-1).
- Natural theology angle: De Cruz, H. & De Smedt, J. (2015), *A Natural History of Natural Theology*, MIT Press (listed at [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_science_of_religion); not read).
- Schloss, J. & Murray, M. (eds.) (2009), *The Believing Primate: Scientific, Philosophical, and Theological Reflections on the Origin of Religion*, Oxford UP. A dedicated volume pairing CSR scientists with philosophers and theologians — [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/book/12844).

### Inferences
- A fair framing for the series: (1) CSR describes *how* belief arises and spreads; (2) the logical step from that to "therefore false" is widely judged fallacious on its own; (3) the debate turns on whether the specific mechanisms (HADD, teleology) are unreliable in the relevant domain. The empirical weakening of HADD and the dominance of CREDs/testimony arguably shift the question from evolutionary debunking towards the epistemology of testimony, which is a different and well-developed philosophical literature. That last point is my inference, not a cited claim.
- Debunking-leaning authors (e.g. Dennett 2006, *Breaking the Spell*; Bering 2011, *The Belief Instinct*) are widely cited as treating CSR as deflationary. I did not verify their texts in this session; see Gaps.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm the exact title of Murray & Goldberg's chapter in *The Believing Primate* ("Evolutionary accounts of religion: Explaining and explaining away" is from memory) or its page range.
- I did not fetch Barrett's own text stating that CSR does not undermine theism. Plantinga's, Dennett's, Bering's and Wilkins & Griffiths' (2013, "Evolutionary debunking arguments in three domains") positions are from memory and **unverified** here.
- I did not verify Paul Bloom's (2012, *Annual Review of Psychology*, "Religion, morality, evolution") remarks on truth-implications.

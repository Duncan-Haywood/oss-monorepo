# Religion in the First States and Archaic Civilisations (c. 3500 BCE – 600 BCE)

**How to read these notes (citation status).** Every claim has a citation. Two kinds are used:
- **[web-verified]**: I fetched or searched the source in this session (Oct 2026) and the details were confirmed.
- **[bibliographic]**: a standard scholarly work cited from established bibliographic knowledge. I did *not* fetch it this session, and many are paywalled monographs. Author, year, title and publisher are standard and well known, but **page-level claims should be checked against the book before publication**. Where a claim is my paraphrase of a scholar's widely reported thesis, I say so.

Dates are approximate. For the third millennium BCE, Mesopotamian and Egyptian absolute chronologies carry uncertainties of decades or more, and "Middle Chronology" dates are used for Mesopotamia.

## 1. Mesopotamia: temple economies, city gods, Enuma Elish, Gilgamesh and Genesis, Jacobsen's phases

### Takeaway
In southern Mesopotamia the state grew up around the temple. Late Uruk Eanna (c. 3400–3100 BCE) produced the earliest writing, and most of it is economic bookkeeping for institutional households. Each city belonged to a patron god whose house was the temple. Kingship was presented as granted by the gods, and only a few kings (Naram-Sin and Shulgi, c. 2250–2050 BCE) were deified. The flood stories (Atrahasis, Gilgamesh XI) parallel Genesis 6–9 closely enough that nearly all scholars posit literary dependence or a shared tradition. The *direction* and *mechanism* of that relationship are still debated.

### Cited Findings
- **The Uruk Eanna precinct and early writing.** The Eanna district at Uruk (Level IV, c. 3400–3200 BCE) holds monumental tripartite buildings and yielded the earliest proto-cuneiform tablets. About 85–90% of these tablets are administrative records: rations, livestock, labour and land. Lexical lists make up the rest. — [bibliographic] Nissen, H. J., Damerow, P. & Englund, R. K. (1993), *Archaic Bookkeeping: Early Writing and Techniques of Economic Administration in the Ancient Near East*, University of Chicago Press; Englund, R. K. (1998), "Texts from the Late Uruk Period," in Bauer, Englund & Krebernik, *Mesopotamien: Späturuk-Zeit und Frühdynastische Zeit* (OBO 160/1), Fribourg/Göttingen. Open data: Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative, https://cdli.mpiwg-berlin.mpg.de/
- **The "temple-state" hypothesis and its revision.** Anton Deimel (1920s–1931) and Anna Schneider (1920) read the Girsu archives (c. 2400 BCE) as showing that the temple owned all land. Scholars now reject that "Sumerian temple-city" model. The temple was one large household (*é*) among several, alongside palace and private holdings, and the Girsu "temple" archive was tied to the ruler's wife's household. — [bibliographic] Foster, B. R. (1981), "A New Look at the Sumerian Temple State," *Journal of the Economic and Social History of the Orient* 24(3): 225–241, https://doi.org/10.1163/156852081X00031 ; Maekawa and others summarised in Van De Mieroop, M. (2016), *A History of the Ancient Near East ca. 3000–323 BC*, 3rd ed., Wiley-Blackwell.
- **City gods.** Each city had a tutelary god who "owned" it: Inanna/Eanna at Uruk (with An), Enlil at Nippur, Nanna at Ur, Enki at Eridu, Ningirsu at Girsu/Lagash, and later Marduk at Babylon and Aššur at Assur. A city's political fortunes were described in terms of its god's. — [bibliographic] Jacobsen, T. (1976), *The Treasures of Darkness: A History of Mesopotamian Religion*, Yale University Press; Bottéro, J. (2001), *Religion in Ancient Mesopotamia*, trans. T. L. Fagan, University of Chicago Press.
- **Kingship as divine gift, and limited deification.** The Sumerian King List opens "When kingship was lowered from heaven." Naram-Sin of Akkad (c. 2254–2218 BCE) took the divine determinative, and Shulgi of Ur III (c. 2094–2047 BCE) was the object of a cult. Most later Mesopotamian kings were *not* gods. They were stewards (ensi) or shepherds of the god. — [bibliographic] Brisch, N. (ed.) (2008), *Religion and Power: Divine Kingship in the Ancient World and Beyond*, Oriental Institute Seminars 4, University of Chicago, open access: https://isac.uchicago.edu/research/publications/ois/ois-4-religion-and-power-divine-kingship-ancient-world-and-beyond
- **Enuma Elish.** This Babylonian creation epic dates probably to the late second millennium BCE. Many scholars place it under Nebuchadnezzar I (c. 1125–1104 BCE), when Marduk's statue came back from Elam, though some argue for an Old Babylonian date. It tells of Marduk killing Tiamat, building the cosmos from her body, and the gods naming him king. It was recited at the Babylonian *akītu* (New Year) festival. Jacobsen and others read it as political theology that raised Babylon's god to the head of the pantheon. — [bibliographic] Lambert, W. G. (2013), *Babylonian Creation Myths*, Eisenbrauns (the standard edition and dating discussion); Jacobsen (1976).
- **Gilgamesh and the flood.** The Standard Babylonian Gilgamesh (attributed to Sîn-lēqi-unninni, c. 1200 BCE) includes in Tablet XI a flood narrative borrowed from *Atrahasis* (Old Babylonian, c. 1700 BCE). Shared points with Genesis 6–9 include a divine decision to destroy humanity, a warned hero, a boat sealed with pitch, birds sent out to test for dry land, a mountain landing, and a sacrifice whose odour the deity/ies smell. George Smith announced the Tablet XI parallel in 1872. — [bibliographic] George, A. R. (2003), *The Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic: Introduction, Critical Edition and Cuneiform Texts*, 2 vols., Oxford University Press; Lambert, W. G. & Millard, A. R. (1969), *Atra-hasis: The Babylonian Story of the Flood*, Oxford: Clarendon.
- **The Genesis relationship is debated.** The mainstream critical view holds that the Genesis flood (often analysed as J and P strands) reworks Mesopotamian tradition, probably during contact in the first millennium BCE. A minority holds that the stories descend independently from a common older tradition. Theologically, Genesis is widely read as a monotheistic *polemic* or "demythologisation": one God acting for moral reasons replaces gods annoyed by human "noise" (Atrahasis). — [bibliographic] Heidel, A. (1949), *The Gilgamesh Epic and Old Testament Parallels*, 2nd ed., University of Chicago Press; Frymer-Kensky, T. (1977), "The Atrahasis Epic and Its Significance for Our Understanding of Genesis 1–9," *Biblical Archaeologist* 40(4): 147–155, https://doi.org/10.2307/3209526
- **Jacobsen's three phases (a scholarly model, not data).** Jacobsen argued that the divine was imagined in three successive dominant metaphors:
  1. **Fourth millennium:** gods as powers *immanent* in natural phenomena, tied to the economy, such as Dumuzi in the date palm and the grain.
  2. **Third millennium:** gods as **rulers**, the divine as king, reflecting the rise of kingship and war.
  3. **Second millennium:** gods as **parents**, a personal god who cares for the individual, seen in penitential psalms and personal religion.

  Jacobsen also coined "primitive democracy" for early Sumerian assemblies. — [bibliographic] Jacobsen, T. (1976), *The Treasures of Darkness*, Yale University Press; Jacobsen, T. (1943), "Primitive Democracy in Ancient Mesopotamia," *JNES* 2(3): 159–172, https://doi.org/10.1086/370672
- **Critiques of Jacobsen.** The schema has been criticised as too neat and evolutionary, and for leaning on intuitive "feeling" reconstructions of religious experience. Bottéro stressed the gods' consistently anthropomorphic, royal-court character and the central role of divination. — [bibliographic] Bottéro (2001); Rochberg, F. (2004), *The Heavenly Writing: Divination, Horoscopy, and Astronomy in Mesopotamian Culture*, Cambridge University Press.
- **Divination as state science.** Extispicy (reading sheep livers) and celestial omens (*Enūma Anu Enlil*, c. 70 tablets) were a royal intelligence apparatus. Neo-Assyrian kings (8th–7th c. BCE) received scholars' omen reports and letters by the hundreds. — [bibliographic] Rochberg (2004); Bottéro, J. (1992), *Mesopotamia: Writing, Reasoning, and the Gods*, University of Chicago Press. Published reports: State Archives of Assyria vols. 8 & 10, open via Oracc, http://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/saao/
- **Human sacrifice at Ur.** Leonard Woolley excavated the Royal Cemetery of Ur in the 1920s–30s (Early Dynastic III, c. 2600–2450 BCE). Its "death pits" held dozens of attendants. The PG 1237 "Great Death Pit" had 74. Baadsgaard et al. CT-scanned two skulls (Penn Museum) and found perimortem cranial trauma. The bodies had been heated and treated, possibly with mercury, and laid out in rows. This contradicts Woolley's peaceful "poison cup" theory. **Discrepancy:** the Antiquity abstract describes a "sharp instrument", while secondary accounts and the follow-up chapter speak of blunt force (bludgeoning). — [web-verified, via search abstracts] Baadsgaard, A., Monge, J., Cox, S. & Zettler, R. L. (2011), "Human Sacrifice and Intentional Corpse Preservation in the Royal Cemetery of Ur," *Antiquity* 85(327): 27–42, https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/E44F1731C922EF9FF43E0B5D2DF67AC1 ; Baadsgaard et al. (2012), "Bludgeoned, Burned, and Beautified: Reevaluating Mortuary Practices in the Royal Cemetery of Ur," in Porter & Schwartz (eds.), *Sacred Killing: The Archaeology of Sacrifice in the Ancient Near East*, Eisenbrauns, pp. 125–158, https://keibi.uni-tuebingen.de/Record/KEI00121575/Details ; overview: ANE Today, "Human Sacrifice in the Ancient Near East and Egypt," https://anetoday.org/human-sacrifice-near-east/

### Inferences
- In Mesopotamia, religion organised the state *materially* (temple households) before it did so ideologically (royal theology). Writing appears to have been invented to manage gods' estates. This is a strong point for the "religion and state co-evolve" view, though it is a point about institutions, not belief.
- The Genesis–Mesopotamia parallels support both a critical-historical reading (borrowing) and a confessional one (Genesis deliberately reworks shared material to make a moral-monotheist point). A fair blog treatment can present both, because they are not mutually exclusive.
- Mesopotamian human sacrifice was episodic and royal-funerary (Ur, c. 2500 BCE). It was not a sustained state institution like the Shang or Aztec cases.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch the full Baadsgaard 2011 paper to settle "sharp" vs "blunt" trauma.
- No precise single-source percentage for administrative vs lexical archaic texts was verified this session. The 85–90% figure is the commonly cited Englund estimate and should be checked.

## 2. Egypt: divine kingship, Ma'at, mortuary religion, Akhenaten, Assmann's "Mosaic distinction"

### Takeaway
Pharaonic kingship (from c. 3100 BCE) treated the living king as Horus and the dead king as Osiris. The state's job was to uphold *ma'at* (order, truth, justice) against *isfet* (chaos). Mortuary religion started as a royal monopoly (Pyramid Texts, c. 2350 BCE) and was progressively "democratised" through the Coffin Texts and the Book of the Dead. Some scholars now contest that "democratisation" model. Akhenaten's Aten cult (c. 1353–1336 BCE) was the most radical exclusive cult before Israel. Scholars still disagree on whether it was true monotheism, henotheism or a royal "monolatry", and on whether it bears any historical relation to Mosaic religion. Assmann says the link is a matter of memory, not history.

### Cited Findings
- **Divine kingship.** The king was the "son of Re" (from the 4th Dynasty) and the embodiment of Horus. He was the sole legitimate officiant of the cult in every temple, and priests acted as his deputies. Most Egyptologists now think the king's *office* was divine while the individual was human; the king was "deified" mainly in specific ritual contexts. — [bibliographic] Frankfort, H. (1948), *Kingship and the Gods: A Study of Ancient Near Eastern Religion as the Integration of Society and Nature*, University of Chicago Press (contrasts Egypt's divine king with Mesopotamia's god-chosen king); Posener, G. (1960), *De la divinité du Pharaon*, Paris (the classic argument that the king's divinity was limited); O'Connor, D. & Silverman, D. (eds.) (1995), *Ancient Egyptian Kingship*, Brill.
- **Ma'at.** Ma'at is both a goddess and a principle of cosmic, social and moral order. The king "offers ma'at" to the gods in temple reliefs. Assmann describes it as "connective justice", a principle binding vertical (king and gods) and horizontal (social) solidarity. — [bibliographic] Assmann, J. (1990), *Ma'at: Gerechtigkeit und Unsterblichkeit im Alten Ägypten*, Munich: C. H. Beck; Assmann, J. (2001), *The Search for God in Ancient Egypt*, Cornell University Press.
- **Mortuary religion.** The Pyramid Texts first appear in the pyramid of Unas (end of the 5th Dynasty, c. 2350 BCE). Coffin Texts (First Intermediate Period/Middle Kingdom, c. 2100–1700 BCE) put similar spells on non-royal coffins, and the Book of the Dead (New Kingdom, from c. 1550 BCE) placed them on papyri for elites. Spell 125 contains the weighing of the heart against ma'at and the "negative confession". The older "democratisation of the afterlife" thesis has been challenged, notably by Smith, who argues that non-royal people had Osirian afterlife hopes earlier than the textual record suggests. — [bibliographic] Allen, J. P. (2005), *The Ancient Egyptian Pyramid Texts*, SBL; Smith, M. (2017), *Following Osiris: Perspectives on the Osirian Afterlife from Four Millennia*, Oxford University Press; Taylor, J. H. (ed.) (2010), *Journey Through the Afterlife: Ancient Egyptian Book of the Dead*, British Museum Press.
- **Early royal retainer sacrifice.** First Dynasty royal tombs at Abydos (c. 3000–2900 BCE) are ringed by subsidiary graves. About 200 accompany the tomb of Djer. Many scholars interpret these as retainers killed at the king's burial, though direct evidence of the cause of death is limited. The practice disappears after the 1st Dynasty. — [web-verified as overview] ANE Today, "Human Sacrifice in the Ancient Near East and Egypt," https://anetoday.org/human-sacrifice-near-east/ ; [bibliographic] Morris, E. F. (2007), "Sacrifice for the State: First Dynasty Royal Funerals and the Rites at Macramallah's Rectangle," in Laneri (ed.), *Performing Death*, Oriental Institute Seminars 3, https://isac.uchicago.edu/research/publications/ois/ois-3-performing-death-social-analyses-funerary-traditions-ancient-near-east
- **The Amarna reform.** Amenhotep IV became Akhenaten around year 5 of his reign, c. 1349 BCE. He founded Akhetaten (Amarna) and promoted the sun-disc, the Aten, as the sole god. Later in the reign the names of Amun, and sometimes the plural "gods", were hacked out. Only the king (and Nefertiti) had access to the Aten, so ordinary people worshipped through the royal family. The *Great Hymn to the Aten* has often been compared with Psalm 104. After his death (c. 1336 BCE) the reform was reversed under Tutankhamun and Horemheb, and Akhenaten suffered a *damnatio memoriae*. — [bibliographic] Hornung, E. (1999), *Akhenaten and the Religion of Light*, Cornell University Press; Redford, D. B. (1984), *Akhenaten: The Heretic King*, Princeton University Press.
- **The "was it monotheism?" debate.**
  - Hornung calls it the first monotheism, in a qualified sense: a "religion of light" that denied other gods.
  - Redford stresses its coercive, royal-centred character.
  - Others call it monolatry or henotheism, because the king himself was a quasi-divine mediator and the reform was short-lived and elite.
  - Freud, in *Moses and Monotheism* (1939), speculated that Moses was an Atenist. No Egyptologist accepts that as history.

  — [bibliographic] Hornung (1999); Redford (1984); Freud, S. (1939), *Der Mann Moses und die monotheistische Religion*, Amsterdam: de Lange.
- **Assmann's "Mosaic distinction".** Assmann argues that Akhenaten and Moses (as a figure of memory) introduced a "counter-religion" built on the distinction between *true* and *false* religion. That distinction replaced the "cosmotheism" of the ancient world, in which gods were translatable across cultures (Amun = Zeus). Assmann treats Akhenaten as the historical but forgotten instance and Moses as the remembered one. He says the inquiry is "mnemohistory", not a claim of historical dependence. Critics include theologians who charge that the thesis links monotheism with intolerance and violence; Assmann partly qualified it in later work. — [bibliographic] Assmann, J. (1997), *Moses the Egyptian: The Memory of Egypt in Western Monotheism*, Harvard University Press; Assmann, J. (2010 [2003]), *The Price of Monotheism*, Stanford University Press (his response to critics).

### Inferences
- Egypt shows the most fully integrated religion-state of the period: king, cult, cosmic order and the afterlife form one system, and the temple economy was large (the New Kingdom Amun estate). The Amarna episode shows the system's limit. A king could impose a theology from the top, but it did not outlive him.
- For a Catholic/Orthodox-sympathetic writer: Assmann's thesis is a serious secular account of what was *new* about biblical monotheism, an exclusive truth-claim. It invites theological engagement rather than simple refutation, and Assmann's own later qualifications soften its "monotheism equals violence" reading.

### Gaps
- No page-level verification of the Hornung and Redford positions was done this session.
- The Abydos retainer numbers (c. 200+ for Djer; counts differ by source and grave category) need checking against Morris (2007) or Dreyer's excavation reports.

## 3. Shang oracle bones and ancestor worship; the Zhou "Mandate of Heaven"

### Takeaway
The late Shang state at Anyang (Yinxu, c. 1250–1046 BCE) was governed partly through **pyromantic divination** addressed to royal ancestors and to a high god, Di. Divination was a royal monopoly whose records are the earliest Chinese writing. Ancestor cult was tied to **large-scale human sacrifice**, often of Qiang captives: one estimate puts the total at more than 13,000 people over roughly 200 years. The Zhou conquest (c. 1046 BCE) introduced the theology of *Tianming* (Mandate of Heaven): Heaven grants rule to the virtuous and withdraws it from the corrupt. This was a moralised political theology, and it justified a dynastic change.

### Cited Findings
- **Oracle-bone divination.** Diviners applied heat to prepared turtle plastrons and cattle scapulae, read the cracks, and often incised the charge (the question), the prognostication and sometimes the verification. About 150,000+ inscribed fragments are known, mostly from Anyang, covering roughly the reigns of Wu Ding to Di Xin (c. 1250–1046 BCE). Topics include sacrifices, war, harvests, weather, childbirth, illness and dreams. — [bibliographic] Keightley, D. N. (1978), *Sources of Shang History: The Oracle-Bone Inscriptions of Bronze Age China*, University of California Press; Keightley, D. N. (2000), *The Ancestral Landscape: Time, Space, and Community in Late Shang China (ca. 1200–1045 B.C.)*, Berkeley: Institute of East Asian Studies. Popular summary: Sapiens, "The Chinese History That Is Written in Bone," https://www.sapiens.org/archaeology/chinese-oracle-bones-history/ (fetched only as a search result).
- **The ancestral pantheon and Di.** Keightley describes a hierarchy: the high god **Di** (remote, controls weather and war, not directly sacrificed to in the same way); nature powers (River, Mountain); "former lords"; and royal ancestors, who received a regular, scheduled sacrificial cycle and could cause harm (*huo*, "curse"). Keightley famously called the Shang religion a "bureaucratic" theology in which ancestors were ranked like officials and approached through proper procedure. He also argued that this "made" the Shang state's administrative mentality. — [bibliographic] Keightley, D. N. (1978), "The Religious Commitment: Shang Theology and the Genesis of Chinese Political Culture," *History of Religions* 17(3/4): 211–225, https://doi.org/10.1086/462792 ; Eno, R. (2009), "Shang State Religion and the Pantheon of the Oracle Texts," in Lagerwey & Kalinowski (eds.), *Early Chinese Religion, Part One: Shang through Han*, Brill, pp. 41–102.
- **Scholarly disagreement over Di.** Eno argues that "Di" may have been a collective or generic term for ancestral powers rather than a single supreme god. Others, such as Keightley, treat Di as a high god. — [bibliographic] Eno (2009).
- **Scale of human sacrifice.** Christina Cheung (2017) estimates that more than 13,000 humans were sacrificed at Yinxu over about 200 years, often 50 and sometimes up to 300 at once. The royal cemetery area holds more than 2,500 sacrificial pits. Oracle inscriptions name many victims as **Qiang** captives. — [web-verified] Sapiens / American Scientist (Cheung), https://www.sapiens.org/archaeology/chinese-oracle-bones-history/ ; Archaeology magazine report, https://www.archaeology.org/news/5664-170616-china-sacrificial-victims
- **The isotope study.** Cheung et al. measured δ13C, δ15N and δ34S in 68 sacrificial victims and 39 Yinxu residents. Victims' bones showed non-local diets earlier in life but local diet in the years before death. This suggests captives were held (perhaps as labourers) for years before being killed. The authors acknowledge there is no direct evidence of forced labour. — [web-verified, via search abstract and news] Cheung, C., Jing, Z., Tang, J., Weston, D. A. & Richards, M. P. (2017), "Diets, social roles, and geographical origins of sacrificial victims at the royal cemetery at Yinxu, Shang China: New evidence from stable carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur isotope analysis," *Journal of Anthropological Archaeology* 48: 28–45, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaa.2017.05.006 ; phys.org summary, https://m.phys.org/news/2017-06-skeletal-sacrificial-victims-shang-dynasty.html
- **Wider Shang captive sacrifice.** A 2022 strontium-isotope study of skulls from a ditch at Zhengzhou Shang City (earlier Shang) concluded they were likely non-local prisoners of war. — [web-verified, search result] https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9690788
- **The Mandate of Heaven.** Western Zhou bronze inscriptions (e.g., the Da Yu ding, early 10th c. BCE) and early chapters of the *Shangshu* (Book of Documents), such as "Shao gao" and "Kang gao", state that **Tian** (Heaven) withdrew its mandate from the Shang because of their drunkenness and cruelty. It then conferred the mandate on King Wen and King Wu. Dating the *Shangshu* chapters is disputed; some are much later. Early Zhou also largely ended mass human sacrifice, though not entirely: retainer burials continued sporadically into the Eastern Zhou. — [bibliographic] Shaughnessy, E. L. (1999), "Western Zhou History," in Loewe & Shaughnessy (eds.), *The Cambridge History of Ancient China*, Cambridge University Press, pp. 292–351; Pines, Y. (2012), *The Everlasting Empire*, Princeton University Press; Puett, M. J. (2002), *To Become a God: Cosmology, Sacrifice, and Self-Divinization in Early China*, Harvard University Asia Center.
- **Puett's revision.** Puett argues against a simple "Shang religion was magical/bureaucratic, Zhou was ethical" story. In his account, Shang and Zhou ritual alike aimed to transform capricious spirits into a manageable hierarchy, which is an agonistic rather than harmonious relationship. — [bibliographic] Puett (2002).

### Inferences
- The Shang are the clearest case in this survey of religion as **state information technology**: royal divination was simultaneously cult, decision procedure and archive.
- The Zhou mandate is a crucial conceptual step. It makes divine favour **conditional on moral conduct**, so rulers become answerable to a moral Heaven. That is a useful comparison point with biblical prophetic critique of kings, without implying any historical connection.
- The 13,000 figure is an extrapolated estimate and should be cited as such.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch Keightley's article texts. The number of oracle-bone fragments (150,000+; some say ~130,000–200,000) varies by source.
- The Western Zhou inscriptions were not checked against a primary edition this session.

## 4. Indus Valley: what can and cannot be said. Vedic religion: Rigveda dating, soma, yajña

### Takeaway
The **Indus (Harappan) civilisation** (Mature phase c. 2600–1900 BCE) left no decipherable texts. Its script is undeciphered, and some argue it is not even a full writing system. Religious interpretation therefore rests on iconography (seals, terracottas) and architecture (the Great Bath), and is highly speculative. Marshall's 1931 "proto-Shiva/Pashupati" reading of seal M-420 has been sharply criticised and is no longer a consensus. Notably, there are **no unambiguous temples or palaces**, which is unusual for an early state. **Vedic religion** is known from the Rigveda, orally composed probably c. 1500–1200 BCE (with uncertainty of a few centuries). It centres on fire sacrifice (*yajña*) to devas such as Agni, Indra and Soma, with the ritual drink *soma*, whose botanical identity is unknown.

### Cited Findings
- **The Pashupati seal.** The seal was excavated at Mohenjo-daro in 1928–29 (DK-G area, Mackay no. 420). Marshall (1931) identified the seated, possibly three-faced, horned figure surrounded by animals as a "proto-Shiva" (Pashupati, "lord of animals", in yogic posture). His view was widely accepted "for about two generations". — [web-verified] Wikipedia, "Pashupati seal" (with references to Marshall 1931 and Mackay 1937–38), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashupati_seal
- **Critics of the proto-Shiva reading.**
  - Sullivan (1964) read the figure as female.
  - Srinivasan (1975–76; 1997) argued the side "faces" are bovine ears and proposed a "buffalo-man".
  - Hiltebeitel (2011) linked it to the buffalo demon Mahiṣāsura.
  - Parpola linked the seal to a Varuna-like deity.
  - Witzel (2008) said a Vedic connection "cannot be established".
  - Doniger (2011) cautioned that Indus images cannot be shown to be the source of Hindu images.
  - Samuel (2017) said the competing readings "cannot all be right".
  - Kenoyer (2003) still accepted the ithyphallic reading.

  — [web-verified, secondary aggregator] Wikipedia, "Pashupati seal," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashupati_seal ; primary: Srinivasan, D. M. (1975–76), "The So-Called Proto-Śiva Seal from Mohenjo-Daro: An Iconological Assessment," *Archives of Asian Art* 29: 47–58 [bibliographic]; Samuel, G. (2008), *The Origins of Yoga and Tantra*, Cambridge University Press [bibliographic].
- **The script.** About 4,000–5,000 inscribed objects are known. Most inscriptions are very short, about 5 signs on average. Farmer, Sproat & Witzel (2004) argued the signs are a non-linguistic symbol system. Rao et al. (2009, *Science*) countered with conditional-entropy statistics consistent with language, and the dispute is ongoing. — [bibliographic] Farmer, S., Sproat, R. & Witzel, M. (2004), "The Collapse of the Indus-Script Thesis: The Myth of a Literate Harappan Civilization," *Electronic Journal of Vedic Studies* 11(2): 19–57; Rao, R. P. N. et al. (2009), "Entropic Evidence for Linguistic Structure in the Indus Script," *Science* 324(5931): 1165, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1170391
- **Archaeological religion with no clear temples.** The evidence includes the Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro, perhaps used for ritual purification, but this is an inference. It also includes "fire altars" at Kalibangan and Lothal (contested as ritual), female terracotta figurines (once read as a "mother goddess", now more cautiously), and "unicorn" seals. The absence of identifiable royal tombs, palaces or temples has fuelled debate about whether the Indus polity was a state with a ruling class at all. — [bibliographic] Possehl, G. L. (2002), *The Indus Civilization: A Contemporary Perspective*, AltaMira; Kenoyer, J. M. (1998), *Ancient Cities of the Indus Valley Civilization*, Oxford University Press.
- **Rigveda dating.** Most Indologists date the composition of the core "family books" (II–VII) to c. 1500–1200 BCE, with Book X later (c. 1200–1000 BCE). The main anchors are:
  - the Mitanni treaty of c. 1380 BCE (Hittite–Mitanni), which invokes Mitra, Varuṇa, Indra and the Nāsatyas;
  - linguistic closeness to the Avestan Gathas;
  - the absence of iron in the core books (iron appears c. 1200–1000 BCE in North India);
  - knowledge of the horse and chariot.

  A minority "Indigenous Aryans"/"Out of India" school argues for much earlier dates (3rd millennium or earlier). Mainstream philology and archaeogenetics reject this. Narasimhan et al. 2019 (*Science*) found Steppe-related ancestry entering South Asia in the 2nd millennium BCE, which they link to Indo-Aryan language spread. That link is itself an inference. — [bibliographic] Witzel, M. (1995), "Early Sanskritization: Origins and Development of the Kuru State," *EJVS* 1(4); Jamison, S. W. & Brereton, J. P. (2014), *The Rigveda: The Earliest Religious Poetry of India*, 3 vols., Oxford University Press (introduction on dating); Narasimhan, V. M. et al. (2019), "The formation of human populations in South and Central Asia," *Science* 365(6457): eaat7487, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aat7487 ; Bryant, E. (2001), *The Quest for the Origins of Vedic Culture: The Indo-Aryan Migration Debate*, Oxford University Press (fair survey of both sides).
- **Soma.** Book IX of the Rigveda (114 hymns) is devoted to Soma Pavamāna, the pressed and filtered plant drink, which is also a god. Its identity is unknown. Proposals include *Ephedra* (favoured by many, and backed by the Avestan *haoma* tradition and BMAC finds, which are themselves disputed), Wasson's *Amanita muscaria* (1968), and *Peganum harmala*. — [bibliographic] Wasson, R. G. (1968), *Soma: Divine Mushroom of Immortality*, Harcourt Brace; Houben, J. E. M. (2003), "The Soma-Haoma Problem," *EJVS* 9(1); Jamison & Brereton (2014).
- **Yajña.** Yajña is solemn sacrifice in the sacred fire (Agni as messenger to the gods) of milk products, grain, soma and animals. It was performed by hereditary priestly specialists: hotṛ (reciter), adhvaryu (ritual actor) and udgātṛ (chanter). The theology is reciprocal (*do ut des*), and later Brāhmaṇa texts make sacrifice the very engine of cosmic order (*ṛta*). The Puruṣa-sūkta (RV 10.90) depicts the cosmos, including the four varṇas, as made from the sacrifice of a primordial man. It is a late hymn and an early charter of the social hierarchy. — [bibliographic] Jamison & Brereton (2014); Heesterman, J. C. (1993), *The Broken World of Sacrifice: An Essay in Ancient Indian Ritual*, University of Chicago Press.
- **Vedic religion and political power.** Royal consecration and horse sacrifice (*rājasūya*, *aśvamedha*) emerged with the Kuru state (c. 1200–900 BCE). Witzel argues that ritual standardisation and canon formation (collecting the Rigveda) were deliberate state-building tools. — [bibliographic] Witzel (1995).

### Inferences
- Methodologically, the Indus is the cautionary case. Without texts, almost any religious reconstruction runs ahead of the evidence, so a blog should say plainly that "we do not know".
- The continuity claims ("Shiva worship is 5,000 years old") are largely unsupported and are politically charged in modern India.
- Vedic India shows a society where religious specialists (brahmins) and warriors (kṣatriyas) became distinct estates with formal ritual interdependence. That is a different religion–state pattern from Egyptian divine kingship.

### Gaps
- Narasimhan et al. 2019 and the Farmer/Rao debate were not fetched this session. Their citations are from established knowledge, and their findings should be spot-checked.
- No reliable evidence was found, or exists, to name an Indus deity.

## 5. Mesoamerica and the Andes: Olmec, Maya, Aztec sacrifice (Templo Mayor and Huei Tzompantli), Inca capacocha

### Takeaway
Mesoamerican religion combined sacred kingship, calendrical ritual, the ballgame, bloodletting and human sacrifice. These features run from the Olmec (c. 1200–400 BCE) to the Aztecs. The **Huei Tzompantli** skull rack at Tenochtitlan, excavated by INAH from 2015 on, has yielded more than 600 skulls (by 2020) from men, women and children. That confirms large-scale sacrifice, though not the largest Spanish figures. The Inca **capacocha** (15th–16th c. CE) involved children taken to high mountain shrines. Hair analysis of the Llullaillaco mummies shows escalating coca and alcohol intake in the months before death. *Note:* the Aztec and Inca cases fall outside the 3500–600 BCE window (they are 14th–16th c. CE). They are "archaic states" typologically, not chronologically.

### Cited Findings
- **The Olmec (c. 1400–400 BCE).** San Lorenzo and La Venta have colossal heads read as ruler portraits, plus "were-jaguar" iconography and offerings including buried serpentine mosaics. Infant remains at El Manatí (c. 1600–1200 BCE) are interpreted by some as child sacrifice, though that is debated. — [bibliographic] Diehl, R. A. (2004), *The Olmecs: America's First Civilization*, Thames & Hudson; Ortiz, P. & Rodríguez, M. del C. (1999), "Olmec Ritual Behavior at El Manatí," in Grove & Joyce (eds.), *Social Patterns in Pre-Classic Mesoamerica*, Dumbarton Oaks.
- **Maya sacred kingship.** Classic Maya kings (c. 250–900 CE) were *k'uhul ajaw* ("holy lord"). They performed autosacrifice (bloodletting; e.g., Yaxchilan Lintel 24, Lady K'abal Xook) and took captives for sacrifice. Since the decipherment of the 1970s–90s, the old view of the Maya as peaceful priest-astronomers has been abandoned. — [bibliographic] Schele, L. & Miller, M. E. (1986), *The Blood of Kings: Dynasty and Ritual in Maya Art*, Kimbell Art Museum; Houston, S., Stuart, D. & Taube, K. (2006), *The Memory of Bones: Body, Being, and Experience among the Classic Maya*, University of Texas Press.
- **The Huei Tzompantli of Tenochtitlan.** INAH's Urban Archaeology Program first found the structure in 2015, with 484 skulls identified at that stage. A December 2020 report announced 119 more on the eastern façade, bringing the total to more than 600. The skulls include men, women and at least three children, which overturns the assumption that they were all male warriors. The tower was built in three phases, probably between 1486 and 1502 (reign of Ahuitzotl). INAH archaeologist Raúl Barrera Rodríguez said archaeologists cannot determine how many were warriors, "perhaps some were captives destined for sacrificial ceremonies", and that they were "turned into gifts for the gods". — [web-verified] Smithsonian Magazine (14 Dec 2020), "New Find Brings Skulls Discovered in Aztec Tower to Over 600," https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/new-find-brings-skulls-discovered-aztec-tower-over-600-180976543/ ; Hyperallergic, https://hyperallergic.com/611595/119-human-skulls-unearthed-in-15th-century-aztec-shrine/
- **Chronicles vs archaeology.** Spanish conquistador Andrés de Tapia described "tens of thousands" of skulls (an often-quoted figure is 136,000). Archaeology has so far recovered hundreds, and only part of the structure is excavated. — [web-verified for the "tens of thousands" wording] Smithsonian (2020), above. The "136,000" figure was *not* verified this session.
- **Scale estimates are contested.** Figures such as 20,000 or 80,400 victims at the 1487 rededication of the Templo Mayor come from colonial sources. Most scholars consider them inflated. Revisionists who downplay sacrifice as Spanish propaganda have been answered by the bioarchaeology: tzompantli, child sacrifices in Templo Mayor offerings (e.g., Offering 48, more than 40 children associated with Tlaloc), and cut marks. — [bibliographic] López Luján, L. & Chávez Balderas, X. (2010), "Al pie del Templo Mayor: excavaciones en busca de los soberanos mexicas," in *Moctezuma II: Tiempo y destino de un gobernante*, INAH; Chávez Balderas, X. (2007), *Rituales funerarios en el Templo Mayor de Tenochtitlan*, INAH; Román Berrelleza, J. A. (1990), *Sacrificio de niños en el Templo Mayor*, INAH. For historiography, see Graulich, M. (2005), *Le sacrifice humain chez les Aztèques*, Fayard.
- **Aztec religious-political ideology.** In Aztec cosmology the Fifth Sun needed nourishment by blood and hearts (*tlaxcaltiliztli*, "nourishing"). War captives were the preferred victims, and "Flower Wars" (*xochiyaoyotl*) with Tlaxcala were said to supply them. Interpretations range from religious sincerity (Carrasco) to "political terror" (Harner's "protein" thesis, now widely rejected). — [bibliographic] Carrasco, D. (1999), *City of Sacrifice: The Aztec Empire and the Role of Violence in Civilization*, Beacon Press; Harner, M. (1977), "The Ecological Basis for Aztec Sacrifice," *American Ethnologist* 4(1): 117–135, https://doi.org/10.1525/ae.1977.4.1.02a00070 ; critique by Ortiz de Montellano, B. R. (1983), "Counting Skulls: Comment on the Aztec Cannibalism Theory of Harner-Harris," *American Anthropologist* 85(2): 403–406, https://doi.org/10.1525/aa.1983.85.2.02a00130
- **The Inca capacocha.** The Llullaillaco mummies were three children found in 1999 near the 6,739 m summit in Argentina, c. 500 years old. Segmental scalp-hair analysis showed coca and alcohol intake that escalated before death. The 13-year-old "Maiden" showed a coca increase about 12 months before death and peak alcohol in her final weeks. The authors read this alongside changes in status and treatment: a year-long ritual process. They raise questions of consent, coercion or compliance rather than settling them. — [web-verified] Wilson, A. S., Brown, E. L., Villa, C., Lynnerup, N., et al. (2013), "Archaeological, radiological, and biological evidence offer insight into Inca child sacrifice," *PNAS* 110(33): 13322–13327, https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1305117110 ; Bradford repository, https://bradscholars.brad.ac.uk/entities/publication/13d61491-ee76-468e-b557-a24a58e8447d ; Archaeology magazine (30 Jul 2013), https://archaeology.org/news/2013/07/30/130730-argentina-llullaillaco-maiden-coca-alcohol/
- **Capacocha and state power.** In Spanish chronicle accounts, the capacocha linked provinces to Cuzco. Children were taken to Cuzco and back to their provinces' sacred mountains (*huacas*), binding local elites to the Inca through the gift of their children. — [bibliographic] Reinhard, J. & Ceruti, M. C. (2010), *Inca Rituals and Sacred Mountains: A Study of the World's Highest Archaeological Sites*, Cotsen Institute of Archaeology, UCLA.
- **Earlier Andean sacrifice.** The Moche (c. 100–800 CE) sacrificed captives (Huaca de la Luna Plaza 3A). At Huanchaquito-Las Llamas (c. 1450 CE, Chimú) more than 140 children were sacrificed with llamas, the largest single child-sacrifice event known. — [bibliographic] Prieto, G. et al. (2019), "A mass sacrifice of children and camelids at the Huanchaquito-Las Llamas site, Moche Valley, Peru," *PLOS ONE* 14(3): e0211691, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0211691

### Inferences
- New World evidence independently confirms that early and archaic states in different hemispheres, with no contact, converged on sacred kingship, a monumental temple cult, calendrical priesthoods and state-administered human sacrifice. That convergence is one of the strongest data points for "religion–state co-evolution" theories.
- The bioarchaeology strongly supports the reality of Aztec sacrifice at scale, but not the largest colonial numbers. A fair presentation avoids both minimisation and sensationalism.

### Gaps
- INAH primary press bulletins were not fetched. The 2017 figure (reported as about 650+ skulls at that point) was not verified, so I report only the 2015 count (484) and the 2020 total (600+).
- The Templo Mayor Offering 48 child count and the Prieto 2019 numbers were not fetched this session.

## 6. Human sacrifice and social control: Watts et al. 2016 (Nature) findings and critiques

### Takeaway
Watts et al. (2016) applied Bayesian phylogenetic comparative methods to 93 Austronesian societies. They found that human sacrifice correlates with stratification. Their models support it helping societies *gain* high stratification and *not lose* it, which supports the "social control hypothesis". Critiques focus on modest Bayes-factor support, coding from sparse ethnographic sources, phylogenetic-model assumptions, and how far one language family can generalise.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation.** Watts, J., Sheehan, O., Atkinson, Q. D., Bulbulia, J. & Gray, R. D. (2016), "Ritual human sacrifice promoted and sustained the evolution of stratified societies," *Nature* 532(7598): 228–231, https://doi.org/10.1038/nature17159 , PMID 27042932. — [web-verified] PubMed, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27042932/
- **Key numbers.** Of 93 Austronesian cultures, 40 practised ritual human sacrifice. That includes 67% (18/27) of highly stratified, 37% (17/46) of moderately stratified and 25% (5/20) of egalitarian societies. Victims were typically low-status (e.g., slaves). Sacrificers were usually high-status (priests, chiefs). Occasions included punishing taboo violations, funerals and building canoes or houses. Methods included burning, drowning, strangulation, bludgeoning, burial alive, being crushed under a newly built canoe, and being rolled off a roof and decapitated. — [web-verified] Max Planck Institute for the Science of Human History press release, "The Dark Side of Religion" (Apr 2016), https://www.shh.mpg.de/166222/humansacrifices
- **Claimed causal direction.** Phylogenetic reconstruction found that sacrifice tended to precede, and to stabilise, high stratification. Gray: "human sacrifice provided a particularly effective means of social control". Watts: religious ritual had "a more sinister role in the evolution of modern societies". — [web-verified] MPI-SHH press release, above; HRAF summary, https://hraf.yale.edu/documents/1020
- **Critique 1: weak statistics.** Statistician Christian P. Robert ("Xi'an") noted that the reported Bayes factor for trait dependence is 3.78, "not overwhelming", and that alternative hypotheses produced Bayes factors in a similar range. He also called the models "quite simplistic" and flagged the use of maximum-likelihood estimates to inform priors. *This is a blog post, not a peer-reviewed reply.* — [web-verified] Xi'an's Og, republished on R-bloggers (16 May 2016), "Reversible chainsaw massacre," https://www.r-bloggers.com/2016/05/reversible-chainsaw-massacre/amp/
- **Critique 2: generalisation.** Press coverage noted the "danger of overgeneralization" from one region and language family. — [web-verified, search snippet] Scientific American, "How Human Sacrifice Propped Up the Social Order," https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/how-human-sacrifice-propped-up-the-social-order
- **Critique 3: wider methodological concerns** (my synthesis of the general literature on phylogenetic comparative methods, *not* a specific published reply to Watts). Ethnographic coding of sacrifice from missionary and colonial sources may be biased: missionaries might exaggerate or under-report. "Galton's problem" is addressed by the phylogeny but remains sensitive to tree uncertainty and horizontal transmission. Correlated evolution does not by itself establish mechanism. — I found **no formal published Nature "Matters Arising" reply** to Watts et al. in this session's searches.

### Inferences
- The paper fits a long tradition, from Girard to Bloch to Carrasco, linking sacrifice to the consolidation of power. The cross-period evidence surveyed here fits it qualitatively: Ur, Abydos, the Shang, the Aztecs and the Inca all show royal/elite-administered killing. But those are not data in the Austronesian model, and the pattern could reflect elites using *any* available violent ritual rather than sacrifice "causing" stratification.
- The Zhou abandonment of mass sacrifice while staying highly stratified, and the 1st-Dynasty Egyptian abandonment of retainer burial, suggest that once states had bureaucracies, sacrifice became dispensable as a control mechanism. That is consistent with Watts's claim that sacrifice matters most in the *transition* to stratification.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed rebuttal located. The Nature article's full text (paywalled) was not read, so the details of the "gain" and "loss" transition rates are not reported here.

## 7. Whitehouse's modes of religiosity (doctrinal vs imagistic): evidence from Seshat and Çatalhöyük

### Takeaway
Whitehouse's theory says religion clusters into two attractor modes:
- **imagistic:** rare, high-arousal, often traumatic rituals that bind small groups through episodic memory;
- **doctrinal:** frequent, low-arousal, routinised rituals transmitted by semantic memory, with specialist priesthoods, orthodoxy, and the capacity to unify large, anonymous populations.

The theory predicts a shift toward the doctrinal mode as societies scale up into states. Çatalhöyük was used as an early test case. Seshat global-history data have been used to argue that doctrinal ritual precedes, and moralising "big gods" follow, increases in social complexity. A 2019 Nature paper making the latter claim was **retracted** in 2021 after a critique of how it treated missing data. A corrected re-analysis says the main conclusion holds.

### Cited Findings
- **The theory.** — [bibliographic] Whitehouse, H. (2004), *Modes of Religiosity: A Cognitive Theory of Religious Transmission*, AltaMira Press; Whitehouse, H. (2000), *Arguments and Icons: Divergent Modes of Religiosity*, Oxford University Press. Short summary (ritual frequency and arousal versus group size): Faraday Institute profile of Harvey Whitehouse, https://www.faraday.cam.ac.uk/about/people/prof-harvey-whitehouse [web-verified via search snippet].
- **Çatalhöyük (Neolithic, c. 7100–5950 BCE; pre-state, included as a baseline).** Whitehouse and Hodder argued the sequence shows a shift from more imagistic practice (rare, dramatic events such as feasting and house-closing rituals with bull horns and plastered skulls) toward more routinised, frequent, house-based ritual over time. They framed this as a test case and stressed its partial and ambiguous fit. — [bibliographic] Whitehouse, H. & Hodder, I. (2010), "Modes of Religiosity at Çatalhöyük," in Hodder, I. (ed.), *Religion in the Emergence of Civilization: Çatalhöyük as a Case Study*, Cambridge University Press, pp. 122–145.
- **The 2019 Seshat Nature letter (retracted).** Whitehouse, H., François, P., Savage, P. E., et al. (2019), "Complex societies precede moralizing gods throughout world history," *Nature* 568: 226–229. The paper used 414 polities in 30 world regions and reported that moralising gods appear *after* "megasocieties" of about 1 million people, and that **doctrinal rituals tend to precede moralising gods by hundreds of years**. It was **retracted in 2021**. — [web-verified] retraction notice summary, HRAF, https://hraf.yale.edu/ehc/documents/1225 ; ScienceDaily (20 Mar 2019), https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2019/03/190320141116.htm (original press release, including the doctrinal-ritual claim); Inverse summary (414 societies; ritual over moralising gods), https://inverse.com/article/54229-did-religion-help-human-societies-advance . DOI [bibliographic]: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-019-1043-4
- **The Beheim et al. critique.** Beheim et al. argued that the 2019 analysis treated missing data on moralising gods as *absence*, which biased the result toward "complexity first". — [web-verified as described in CSH/HRAF summaries] https://csh.ac.at/publication/testing-the-big-gods-hypothesis-with-global-historical-data-a-review-and-retake/ ; [bibliographic] Beheim, B., Atkinson, Q. D., Bulbulia, J., Gervais, W., Gray, R. D., Henrich, J., Lang, M., Monroe, M. W., Muthukrishna, M., Norenzayan, A., Purzycki, B. G., Shariff, A., Slingerland, E., Spicer, R. & Willard, A. K. (2021), "Treatment of missing data determined conclusions regarding moralizing gods," *Nature* 595: E29–E34, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03655-4
- **The corrected "retake".** The retake, using an expanded codebook and a direct causal test, reports that "the largest increases in social complexity came before Big Gods" and that Big Gods did not drive complexity. — [web-verified] Whitehouse, H., François, P., Savage, P. E., Hoyer, D., Feeney, K. C., Cioni, E., Purcell, R., Larson, J., Baines, J., ter Haar, B., Covey, A. & Turchin, P. (2023; online 2022), "Testing the Big Gods hypothesis with global historical data: a review and 'retake'," *Religion, Brain & Behavior* 13(2): 124–166, https://doi.org/10.1080/2153599X.2022.2074085 ; listing at https://peterturchin.com/publications/testing-the-big-gods-hypothesis-with-global-historical-data-a-review-and-retake/ . *Note:* the Turchin page lists a 2021 date. The DOI indicates 2022 online, and the volume is 2023.
- **Book-length statement.** Whitehouse's *The Ritual Animal* (2021) includes a chapter on doctrinal ritual, identification and social complexity. — [web-verified listing] Whitehouse, H. (2021), *The Ritual Animal: Imitation and Cohesion in the Evolution of Social Complexity*, Oxford University Press, https://academic.oup.com/book/39035
- **The opposing position.** Norenzayan's "Big Gods" theory holds that belief in watchful, moralising gods enabled large-scale cooperation among strangers. The Beheim critics are largely aligned with this position, so the Seshat dispute is also a dispute between rival research programmes. — [bibliographic] Norenzayan, A. (2013), *Big Gods: How Religion Transformed Cooperation and Conflict*, Princeton University Press.

### Inferences
- The early states in this survey fit the "doctrinal" profile on the institutional side. They had full-time priesthoods, written liturgies (Pyramid Texts, Vedic samhitās, oracle archives) and calendrical routine. Yet their high gods were not clearly *moralising* in the later Axial sense, with partial exceptions in Egyptian ma'at/Spell 125 and the Zhou Tian. That fits "doctrinal ritual first, moralising gods later", but because of the retraction episode, any quantitative claim should be cited with care.
- For a blog, the 2019 to 2021 to 2023 story is itself a good illustration of how science corrects itself: claim, critique, retraction, re-analysis.

### Gaps
- I did not read the full text of the retake or Beheim et al., so the strength of the corrected doctrinal-ritual finding is not verified here.
- I did not verify any independent test of modes theory specifically on Bronze Age states.

## 8. Ugarit and Canaan: El, Baal, Asherah and early Israelite religion; Kuntillet Ajrud

### Takeaway
The Ugaritic tablets (Ras Shamra, Syria; mostly 14th–13th c. BCE, before the city's destruction c. 1185 BCE) give a Late Bronze Age Canaanite-type pantheon: **El** (aged father, head of the divine council), his consort **Athirat/Asherah**, the storm-god **Baal** (whose *Baal Cycle* includes battles with Yamm and Mot), and **Anat**. Mark S. Smith and others argue that early Israelite religion emerged *from within* this West Semitic milieu, through two processes:
- **convergence:** Yahweh absorbed El's and Baal's traits;
- **differentiation:** Baal and Asherah were later rejected.

On this view, strict monotheism came late (7th–6th c. BCE). The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions (c. 800 BCE) mentioning "Yahweh of Samaria/Teman and his asherah" are key, but contested, evidence that some Israelites associated Asherah, or an asherah cult object, with Yahweh.

### Cited Findings
- **The Ugaritic corpus.** Excavations from 1929 (Schaeffer) uncovered alphabetic cuneiform texts, including the *Baal Cycle* (KTU 1.1–1.6), *Aqhat* and *Kirta*. El is "father of years", "creator of creatures", with the epithet "Bull El". Baal is "rider of the clouds" (cf. Ps 68:5 [Eng 68:4], *rōkēb bāʿărābôt*). Athirat is "creatress of the gods". — [bibliographic] Smith, M. S. (2002), *The Early History of God: Yahweh and the Other Deities in Ancient Israel*, 2nd ed., Eerdmans / Dove Booksellers; Smith, M. S. (2001), *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism: Israel's Polytheistic Background and the Ugaritic Texts*, Oxford University Press; Pardee, D. (2002), *Ritual and Cult at Ugarit*, SBL Writings from the Ancient World 10; Day, J. (2000), *Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan*, JSOT Supplement 265, Sheffield Academic Press.
- **Smith's thesis.** Early Israel's religion was largely Canaanite. Yahweh, possibly originally a southern deity (cf. "Yahweh came from Seir/Teman", Deut 33:2; Judg 5:4; Hab 3:3), was identified with El (Gen 33:20, "El, the God of Israel"). Yahweh also took over Baal's storm and combat imagery (Leviathan in Isa 27:1 ≈ Ugaritic *ltn*). Smith reads Deut 32:8–9 (LXX/Qumran "sons of God") as preserving El ('Elyon) apportioning nations to the gods, with Yahweh receiving Israel. Exclusive monolatry and then monotheism developed in the monarchic and exilic periods (Deuteronomistic reform; Second Isaiah, c. 540 BCE). — [bibliographic] Smith (2002); Smith (2001).
- **Day's position.** Day agrees on the Canaanite background but stresses that Israel's Yahweh absorbed El early, while Baal and Asherah were treated as rivals. He emphasises biblical polemic against Baal as evidence of real conflict. — [bibliographic] Day (2000).
- **Kuntillet Ajrud.** This is a Sinai way-station excavated by Ze'ev Meshel in 1975–76, and fully published only in 2012. Radiocarbon dating points to around 800 BCE (Meshel, Carmi & Segal 1995), with alternative dates from the late 10th to the late 8th c. BCE. Pithos A reads *brkt ʾtkm lyhwh šmrn wlʾšrth*, "I bless you by Yahweh of Samaria and by his asherah". Pithos B has "Yahweh of Teman and his asherah". — [web-verified, aggregator] Wikipedia, "Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuntillet_Ajrud_inscriptions ; primary: Meshel, Z. et al. (2012), *Kuntillet ʿAjrud (Ḥorvat Teman): An Iron Age II Religious Site on the Judah–Sinai Border*, Israel Exploration Society; Meshel, Z., Carmi, I. & Segal, D. (1995), "14C Dating of an Israelite Biblical Site at Kuntillet Ajrud," *Radiocarbon* 37(2): 205–212 [bibliographic]. Dating context: Biblical Archaeology Society, https://library.biblicalarchaeology.org/?p=101202 ; intertextual.bible, https://intertextual.bible/read/kuntillet-ajrud-inscriptions
- **The interpretive debate.**
  1. **Goddess.** "Asherah" is the goddess as Yahweh's consort (Dever).
  2. **Cult object.** It is a cult object, the wooden pole (*ʾăšērâ*) of Deut 16:21, a "hypostasis" of the goddess or just a symbol. The main reason is that Hebrew normally does not put a possessive suffix on a personal name, so "his Asherah" suggests a common noun. Blum (2021) translates "the ashera of Yahweh".
  3. **Grammar.** Tropper argues the final *h* is not a possessive, giving "Yahweh and Asherah".

  The drawings on Pithos A, sometimes captioned as Yahweh and Asherah, are widely read as **Bes** figures and may not relate to the inscription at all. — [web-verified, aggregator] Wikipedia (citing Blum 2021, *Eretz-Israel*; Choi 2016; Krause 2017, *Vetus Testamentum*; Ahituv 2014; Puech 2014, *Revue Biblique*), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuntillet_Ajrud_inscriptions ; [bibliographic] Dever, W. G. (2005), *Did God Have a Wife? Archaeology and Folk Religion in Ancient Israel*, Eerdmans; Hadley, J. M. (2000), *The Cult of Asherah in Ancient Israel and Judah*, Cambridge University Press.
- **Khirbet el-Qom (near Hebron, 8th c. BCE).** A tomb inscription blesses a man "by Yahweh ... and by his asherah", which parallels Kuntillet Ajrud. — [web-verified mention, aggregator] Wikipedia (citing Zevit 1984, *BASOR*), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuntillet_Ajrud_inscriptions
- **Biblical cross-reference.** The Hebrew Bible itself reports Asherah poles and images in the Jerusalem temple and their removal by Josiah (2 Kgs 23:4–7, c. 622 BCE), and condemns the practice (Deut 16:21). Critical scholars read these texts as evidence that Asherah veneration was common before the Deuteronomistic reform. Traditional readings see them as apostasy from an original covenant faith. — [bibliographic] Smith (2002); Hadley (2000).
- **Ugaritic religion and kingship.** The Ugaritic king performed key rituals, and the *Kirta* epic depicts the king as "son of El", though not fully divine. — [bibliographic] Pardee (2002).

### Inferences
- The evidence firmly establishes that *some* Israelites and Judahites in the 9th–8th c. BCE linked Yahweh with an asherah, whether a goddess or a cult symbol, and worshipped him in local manifestations (Samaria, Teman).
- What this says about "original" Israelite religion is a matter of interpretation. The critical-evolutionary reading (Smith) holds that monotheism emerged gradually. The traditional-confessional reading holds that the epigraphy reflects popular syncretism of the kind the prophets condemned. Both readings fit the inscriptions. The biblical text itself presents a struggle between Yahweh-alone prophets and a syncretistic populace, which is congruent with the archaeology.
- For an Orthodox/Catholic-sympathetic writer, "the God of Israel took over the titles of El and the imagery of Baal" can be framed theologically as *appropriation* and *polemic* (the true God claims the attributes falsely ascribed to others). That fits patristic logic. It is a theological framing, though, not a historical finding.

## 9. Cross-cutting: religion and political power across the early states (synthesis)

### Takeaway
Across independent civilisations (Near East, Egypt, South Asia, China, the Americas), state formation coincided with the following:
1. full-time priesthoods and temple institutions that were also economic centres;
2. royal ideologies placing the ruler at the hinge of cosmos and society, ranging from fully divine (Egypt) to divinely chosen (Mesopotamia, Zhou) to holy lord (Maya);
3. state-controlled divination;
4. episodic or institutionalised human sacrifice, which typically declined as bureaucratic states matured, except in the Aztec and Inca cases;
5. written or memorised canonical ritual texts.

The main scholarly frameworks are functionalist/social control (Watts; Frankfort), cognitive-evolutionary (Whitehouse; Norenzayan) and history-of-religions (Jacobsen; Assmann; Smith). They are partly complementary.

### Cited Findings
- **Frankfort's comparison.** Frankfort framed the contrast between Egypt's god-king and Mesopotamia's servant-king as the basic dichotomy of Near Eastern political theology. — [bibliographic] Frankfort (1948), *Kingship and the Gods*.
- **The comparative standard.** Trigger, comparing seven early civilisations, concluded that all integrated religion and rule. In none did elites separate "religion" as a distinct sphere, and the ruler's role as chief mediator with the supernatural was universal. — [bibliographic] Trigger, B. G. (2003), *Understanding Early Civilizations: A Comparative Study*, Cambridge University Press.
- **The Seshat result.** Seshat-based work (above) reports that doctrinal ritual and high social complexity precede moralising "big gods". — [web-verified] Whitehouse et al. (2023), RBB, https://doi.org/10.1080/2153599X.2022.2074085
- **The sacrifice–stratification association.** The Austronesian analysis found an association between sacrifice and stratification. — [web-verified] Watts et al. (2016), https://doi.org/10.1038/nature17159

### Inferences
- A defensible thesis for the blog: the first states made religion *public, professional and political*. Gods became city-owners and king-makers, ritual became administration, and sacrifice became a display of sovereign power over life. The ethical universalism associated with the Axial Age (c. 800–200 BCE: prophets, Upanishads, Confucius, Buddha) can then be read in part as a reaction against, or transformation of, this archaic state religion. Early signs include the Zhou mandate's moral conditionality and Israel's prophetic critique of kings.
- Evidence and interpretation must be kept apart. Scale of sacrifice, dates and texts are well evidenced. Inner belief, especially in the Indus and the Olmec, often is not.

# Twin as control variate: estimating a controller's real-world cost when the twin is biased

*Stylised: scalar plant `x' = a x + b u + w`, `w ~ N(0,σ²)`, `a=0.9, q=1, r=0.1, σ²=1`, real gain `b=1`, controller `u=−kx` with `k=k*(b)=0.8233` (closed-loop coefficient `c=a−bk=0.0767`, real cost `J=1.074`). The digital twin has gain `b̂` (`ĉ=a−b̂k`). A rollout returns the `T`-step average cost `Y = T⁻¹ Σ (q+rk²) x_t²` (`x₀=0`). Pure Python; see `tests/` and `experiments/results.txt`.*

## Question
`twin-transfer` asks what a twin is worth for *training* a controller; `twin-upkeep` asks what it costs to keep it in sync. The remaining everyday use is *evaluation*: before deployment, how well can we estimate the real cost of a candidate controller when real rollouts are scarce and twin rollouts are cheap? A twin-only estimate is biased by the twin's error; a real-only estimate is noisy. A twin is not useless because it is wrong: used as a control variate it leaves the estimate unbiased, and what it is worth is set by the *correlation* between twin and real rollout costs, not by the twin's accuracy. The question is how that correlation depends on the twin's error and on how much of the real disturbance the twin can replay, and how to split a budget.

## Exact results
1. **Twin-real cost correlation has a closed form.** With the twin driven by the real disturbance (`λ=1`, replay of logged disturbances) the long-horizon correlation of cost sums is `ρ = 2C²(f+f̂−1)/√(v v̂)`, `C=1/(1−cĉ)`, `f=1/(1−c²)`, `v=2f²(2f−1)`, and equals the exact finite-`T` trace formula `tr(AÂ)/√(tr A² tr Â²)` to 0.02 at `T=400` (`ρ` agrees to 4 digits at `T=200`, e.g. `0.9408` vs `0.9411`) and Monte Carlo at `T=200` (6000 pairs: `0.9398`). Twin gain `b̂=1.15, 1.3, 1.6, 2.0` gives `ρ = 0.985, 0.941, 0.782, 0.472`, while the twin-only bias `J(b̂)−J` is `−0.004, +0.026, +0.219, +1.34`. Near the stability cliff (`b̂=2.3`, `ĉ=−0.994`, twin cost `84.9`) the correlation collapses to `0.07` (exact `T=200`; Monte Carlo `0.097`, heavy-tailed and noisy).
2. **Small-error law.** For a twin gain error `δ=b̂−b`, `1−ρ = (1+c⁴)/(1−c⁴)² · (kδ)² + O(δ⁴)`; the measured ratio `(1−ρ)/(kδ)²` is `1.0000, 0.9999, 0.9992, 0.9966` for `δ=0.01…0.10` (prediction `1.0001`). Correlation decays quadratically in the twin's error and the constant depends only on the closed-loop coefficient.
3. **Disturbance replay is the binding constraint.** If the twin is driven by a disturbance sharing fraction `λ` of the real one, the correlation is exactly `λ²ρ₀`: `0.941, 0.762, 0.602, 0.461, 0.235` for `λ=1, 0.9, 0.8, 0.7, 0.5` at `δ=0.3` (Monte Carlo at `λ=0.8`: `0.609` vs `0.602`), and `≈0` (`|ρ|<0.02`) for independent simulation. A perfect-gain twin with independent noise is worthless as a variance-reduction device, and a poor-gain twin fed the logged disturbances is not.
4. **Optimal split and break-even.** With a budget `B = n c_r + N c_t` for `n` real and `N ≥ n` twin rollouts (nested), the optimal-`β` control variate has variance `σ_r²[1/n − (1/n−1/N)ρ²]`, minimised at `N/n = √(c_r ρ² / (c_t(1−ρ²)))` to `σ_r²(√(c_r(1−ρ²))+√c_t|ρ|)²/B`. The twin helps iff `c_t/c_r < (1−√(1−ρ²))²/ρ²` (`0.024, 0.072, 0.167, 0.393, 0.753` for `ρ=0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 0.99`); at a price of `0.01` real rollouts the variance ratio to real-only is `0.97, 0.84, 0.61, 0.28, 0.058`. A correlation of 0.5 buys 16% at 1% price; a correlation of 0.99 buys 17×. This is the multifidelity Monte Carlo allocation of Peherstorfer et al. specialised to a twin whose `ρ` is now known in closed form.
5. **Simulation of the whole estimator.** `b̂=1.3`, `T=100`, twin rollout `0.02` of a real one, budget 20 real units: pilot `ρ=0.945` (exact `0.941`), `β*=0.90`; the optimal split is `n=14, N=283`, predicted variance ratio `0.225`. Over 2000 repetitions: real-only (`n=20`) sd `0.0339`, MSE `1.16·10⁻³`; twin-only (`N=283`) bias `+0.024`, MSE `6.6·10⁻⁴`; control variate bias `−0.0014` (sampling error of the mean, unbiased by construction), sd `0.0162`, MSE `2.6·10⁻⁴`. Empirical variance ratio to real-only `0.228` vs predicted `0.225`. Note the twin-only estimate already beats real-only here because its bias is small.
6. **Bias-variance budget map.** Twin-only beats real-only below `B* = (σ_r²c_r − σ_t²c_t)/bias²` real units: `1457` at `b̂=1.15`, `35` at `b̂=1.3`, `0.5` at `b̂=1.6` (closed form, `T=100`, `w=0.02`). The control variate is unbiased and its MSE is lowest in every cell of the table in `experiments/results.txt` except `b̂=1.15` at `B=20`, where the twin's bias (`0.004`) is so small that twin-only wins; at `B=2000` it is 11× lower than real-only and 15× lower than twin-only at `b̂=1.15`.

## Limitations
Scalar Gaussian LQ with a fixed linear controller; the twin differs only in `b`; the disturbance-sharing model `w'=λw+√(1−λ²)z` is an idealisation of disturbance reconstruction (a real reconstruction `ŵ=x⁺−ax−b̂u` carries the twin's own model error `(b−b̂)u`, which is not modelled). `β` is estimated from a 4000-pair pilot in the simulation; its estimation error and pilot cost are not accounted in the variance law. The variance law needs `N ≥ n` nested samples and Gaussian second moments; near the stability cliff costs are heavy-tailed and the closed forms are optimistic (results 1). Evaluation of one controller, not ranking of many; no claim about nonlinear systems or perception (where the twin and real disturbances are images, not scalars).

## Relevance
Simulation-to-real *evaluation* should be budgeted by correlation and price, not by whether the twin's mean matches. Practical consequences: (i) log the real disturbances (or the exogenous inputs) so the twin can replay them, since this alone sets `ρ ∝ λ²`; (ii) measure `ρ` on a pilot set of paired runs, not the twin's mean error; (iii) allocate `N/n = √(c_rρ²/(c_t(1−ρ²)))` twin runs per real run. Relates to work on sim-to-real predictivity (Kadian et al. measure how well simulated performance ranks real performance) and to work on digital twins of manipulation cells and field robots where real trials are expensive.

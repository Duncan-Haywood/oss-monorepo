# Model substitution in decentralised inference: an anytime-valid test, the cheater's square-root law, and the optimal audit rate

*Duncan Haywood. MIT licence. Code and experiments: `../src`, `../tests`, `../experiments`.*

## Abstract
A decentralised inference network pays providers to serve a claimed model `P`. A provider can pocket a saving `s` per query by serving a cheaper model `Q` instead. We study the cheater who substitutes on each query independently with probability `p`, against a verifier that re-scores a random fraction `f` of responses under `P`. The verifier runs a mixture e-process for `H0: tokens ~ P` against the family `(1−p)P + pQ` with `p` unknown, which is valid at any stopping time (Ville), so peeking is free. (i) Detection delay is `≈ ln(G/α) / (f·KL(mix_p‖P))`, which for small `p` is `2 ln(G/α)/(f p² χ²(Q‖P))`: cheating is detected in time inversely proportional to `p²`, not `p`. (ii) Therefore the cheater's best response is to substitute *rarely*: setting the delay equal to the horizon `N` gives `p* = √(2L/(f N χ²))` and undetected savings `s√(2LN/(fχ²))`, growing as `√N` rather than `N`. (iii) Inverting gives the audit rate that caps savings at `B`: `f = 2LNs²/(χ²B²)`, and (iv) with per-check cost `c` and harm `h` per unit of savings the total-cost-minimising rate is `f* = (hK/(2Nc))^{2/3}`, `K = s√(2LN/χ²)`. Simulation confirms the `p²` delay law (measured delay 0.5–0.8× the closed form), sublinear savings growth (×7.75 for ×64 more queries), and that false alarms stay below `α`. Substitutes with tiny `χ²` (near-quantisation-noise) are essentially unpoliceable by sampling. Stylised: the closed forms are small-`p` heuristics, not theorems with matched constants.

## 1. Model
Token distribution `P` (claimed) and `Q` (substitute) over a vocabulary; here a 30-token Zipf `P` and `Q ∝ P^β`. Each query is independently *cheated* with probability `p` (output drawn from `Q`, saving `s`) and independently *checked* with probability `f`. For a checked query the verifier, who can evaluate `P`, observes the output token `x` and the likelihood ratio `r = Q(x)/P(x)`. The ratio uses only the chosen token, so this stands in for a per-token logprob check from trusted re-execution. We ignore context dependence: a real deployment tests a sequence of conditional distributions, and `P`, `Q` here play the role of a per-position average.

**Test.** `E_n = (1/G) Σ_g Π_t (1 − p_g + p_g r_t)` over a log-spaced grid `p_g ∈ [0.02, 1]`, `G=16`, updated on checked queries only. Under `H0` each factor has mean `Σ_x P(x)(1−p+pQ(x)/P(x)) = 1` (tested), so `E` is a nonnegative martingale with mean 1 and `P(sup E ≥ 1/α) ≤ α`. Slash when `E ≥ 1/α`. This is the same device as in `sequential-slashing`, applied to a different alternative (a mixture family, not a shift of calibration).

## 2. Detection delay goes as `1/p²`
The correct grid component grows like `exp(n·KL(mix_p‖P))` per `n` checked queries and must overcome the `1/G` prior weight and the threshold `1/α`, so the delay in queries is `T(p) ≈ L/(f·KL(mix_p‖P))`, `L = ln(G/α)`. For small `p`, `KL(mix_p‖P) ≈ ½p²χ²(Q‖P)`, giving `T ≈ 2L/(f p² χ²)`. In the experiments (`β=0.5`, `χ²=0.390`, `KL=0.181`, `α=0.05`, `L=5.77`, `f=1`, 40 runs per row) the measured mean delays are 94, 210, 572, 1069, 2119, 4708, 6251, 16317 queries for `p = 0.5, 0.3, 0.2, 0.15, 0.1, 0.07, 0.05, 0.035`, versus the KL formula 126 to 24316: ratios 0.52–0.79 (E3). The formula is conservative (the check happens to fire early because the maximising component fluctuates upward); the scaling is right. Halving `p` roughly quadruples the delay. Every run was detected within the 120 000-query cap. Under an honest provider the empirical false-alarm rate over 1500 checked queries was 1.5% at `α=0.05` and 10% at `α=0.2` (400 runs, E2): below nominal, as Ville requires (the bound is loose because a mixture over a grid needs more than one lucky component).

## 3. The cheater's best response: substitute rarely
A cheater who cheats with fraction `p` for `N` queries saves `p·s·min(T(p), N)` before being caught (or `p s N` if never). The delay `T ∝ 1/p²` falls faster than `p` rises, so profit `p s T(p) ∝ 1/p` *increases* as `p` shrinks until `T(p)` hits the horizon. The maximum is at `T(p*) = N`:

`p* = √(2L/(f N χ²))`, savings `s·√(2LN/(f χ²))`.

Undetected theft is `O(√N)`, versus `N` for a naive full substitution, which is caught in about 100 queries (`p=1`). Measured (E4, best `p` on the grid, `f=1`): savings 49, 111, 221, 383 at `N = 250, 1000, 4000, 16000`, best `p` falling 0.3 → 0.15 → 0.07 → 0.035. That is ×7.75 for ×64 more queries (√64 = 8). The formula overstates the measurement by 1.6–1.8× at these sizes, consistent with §2's ratio (it is an upper envelope). At `N=64000` the optimum `p* ≈ 0.02` lies at the very edge of our test's grid, so that row (555) is truncated and is not evidence for or against the law.

*Implication.* A verifier tuned to catch the full-substitution attack is not safe: the profitable attack is a slow leak that hides below the detection scale. It is the sampling analogue of the low-and-slow attacks in `spot-check-slashing`; unlike there, the leak is limited by a `√N` law rather than a per-step bound.

## 4. Audit rate and cost
Savings scale as `f^{-1/2}`: quadrupling checks halves theft. Inverting, the rate needed to cap savings at `B` is `f = 2LNs²/(χ²B²)`. At `N=10⁶` queries and `B=10 000` (E5): `f = 0.155` for a crude `Q` (`β=0.3`, `χ²=0.74`), 0.296 (`β=0.5`), 0.82 (`β=0.7`, `χ²=0.14`) and 7.6 for a subtle `Q` (`β=0.9`, `χ²=0.015`): a rate above 1 means the budget is *unachievable by sampling*, since even checking every response leaves `√(2LN/χ²) s = 27 586` savings. Subtle substitutes (quantisation, small numerical differences) are undetectable in this sense; this is why bit-reproducible execution (`reproducible-refereed-training`) is the alternative for such cases.

With check cost `c` per query and harm `h` per unit of savings the total `f N c + h s√(2LN/(fχ²))` is minimised at `f* = (hK/(2Nc))^{2/3}`, capped at 1 (matches a grid search to 4 decimals, E6). At `N=10⁶`, `β=0.5`: `h=5, c=0.1` gives `f*=0.26` and total cost 79 337 versus 127 200 for checking everything; with `h=1, c=1` `f*=0.02` versus a full-check cost 17× higher. When checks are cheap relative to harm (`h=5, c=0.01`) `f*=1`.

## 5. Limitations
(1) The closed forms are heuristics using `KL ≈ ½p²χ²`, valid for small `p`; we validate numerically and they are 0.5–0.8× conservative, not tight. (2) One stylised i.i.d. token model with a single log-space test; real outputs are sequences and `Q` varies by context, the verifier's statistic may be a sequence logprob. (3) The verifier must be able to evaluate `P` on a sample (trusted re-execution, or a committed logit trace); we do not price that. (4) The cheater's decision is a fixed `p`; an adaptive cheater that learns the test's state, or checks only when un-audited, is out of scope, and the analysis needs checks to be unpredictable. (5) Our grid floor (`p ≥ 0.02`) matters at large `N`. (6) No stake or slashing model here: pair with `verification-game` for the stake side. (7) Not a claim about any deployed network. Contributions: the mixture e-process for substitution, the `1/p²` delay and `√N` theft law with measurements, and closed-form audit-rate rules.

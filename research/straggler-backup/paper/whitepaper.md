# Waiting for the fastest k of n: round-time laws, time-to-accuracy, and the bias of the speed filter

**Abstract.** Synchronous training over a decentralised pool is gated by its slowest worker. Dropping the slowest `n−k` ("backup workers") shortens rounds but discards their gradients. We give exact answers for three parts of this trade. (i) The round time is the `k`-th order statistic of the finishing times; for shifted-exponential workers `E X_(k) = s + (H_n − H_{n−k})/μ` and `Var X_(k) = Σ_{j≤k} 1/(μ(n−j+1))²`, and for Pareto-tailed workers `E X_(k) = x_m Π_{j≤k} (n−j+1)/(n−j+1−1/α)`, which is finite iff `n−k+1 > 1/α`: with `α ≤ 1` waiting for everyone has infinite expected round time, while skipping one worker does not (all checked against independent integrals and simulation). (ii) For SGD on a quadratic, expected wall-clock to accuracy is `t_k·E X_(k)` with an exact round count `t_k` that has a pole at a minimum `k` set by the noise floor; at `n=64` the optimal `k*` is 5–36 depending on the fixed cost `s`, beating wait-for-all by 34× (`s=0`) to 1.12× (`s=32`), and 34× under Pareto(1.5) tails. Choosing the step size jointly with `k` adds a further 1.1–6.9×. (iii) When device speed is correlated with data, the fastest-`k` filter is a biased sampler: the plain average estimates `Σ π_i θ_i / k`, not the mean (exact bias formula, verified), a bias that no number of rounds removes. Horvitz–Thompson reweighting removes it exactly, but at variance cost: with no coupling it is 7–40% worse in MSE; with strong coupling it is 21–24% better at `k=8–12` of 16, ties at `k=4` and is worse at `k=2`.

## 1. Model
`n` workers; worker `i` returns after `X_i = s + Exp(μ_i)` (a fixed compute/communication cost `s` plus an exponential tail), independently each round. The coordinator applies the average of the first `k` arrivals and ignores the rest; the round ends at `X_(k)`. Rounds are iid, so expected total time is (rounds) × `E X_(k)`. This is the backup-worker scheme of Chen et al. (2016) analysed exactly, in a decentralised setting where workers differ in speed and possibly in data.

## 2. Exact round time
Rényi's representation gives `X_(k) = s + Σ_{j=1..k} Z_j/(μ(n−j+1))` with `Z_j` iid `Exp(1)` (homogeneous rates), so the mean and variance above follow. Tests compare the mean with an independent integral `s + ∫ P(Bin(n,F(t)) ≤ k−1) dt` and mean and variance with simulation. E1 (`n=32, s=1, μ=1`, 20000 rounds): `E X_(k)` exact/sim is 1.1313/1.1315 (`k=4`), 1.6778/1.6773 (16), 2.3406/2.3371 (24), 2.9752/2.9752 (28), 5.0585/5.0526 (32); standard deviations agree to 1%. Waiting for all is 3.0× slower per round than waiting for half and 1.70× slower than skipping the four slowest, because the last few order statistics are `1/μ`, `1/2μ`, … apart in expectation: the harmonic tail `H_n` grows but only the last terms are expensive.

**Heavy tails.** With `X = x_m e^{E/α}` (Pareto), the same representation and the exponential MGF `E e^{tZ/r} = r/(r−t)` give the closed form in the abstract. E2 (`n=32`): for `α=2.5` exact/sim `E X_(k)` are 1.315/1.315 (`k=16`), 2.239/2.240 (28), 3.587/3.586 (31), 5.979/5.961 (32). For `α=1.5` it grows from 1.58 (`k=16`) to 27.1 (`k=32`); for `α=0.8` it is 2.39, 13.96, 92.8 and **infinite** at `k=32`. Backup workers are then not an optimisation but what makes the mean round time exist: the fewest workers to drop is `⌈1/α⌉−1`.

## 3. Time to accuracy
Take `f(x)=a x²/2`, step `η`, `ρ=1−ηa`, aggregate noise variance `σ²/k` (average of `k` independent worker gradients). Then `E x_t² = ρ^{2t}x_0² + (φ/k)(1−ρ^{2t})` with `φ = η²σ²/(1−ρ²)` (verified by 30000-run simulation). The target `E x_t² ≤ ε` is reachable iff `φ/k < ε`, i.e. `k > k_min = φ/ε`, and takes `t_k = ⌈ln((ε−φ/k)/(x_0²−φ/k))/(2 ln ρ)⌉` rounds (tested: first time below target, and infinite at or below `k_min`). So adding workers has two effects: it lowers `t_k` (diverging at `k_min`) and raises `E X_(k)`; the product is minimised at an interior `k*`.

E3 (`n=64, μ=1, a=1, η=0.2, σ²=2, x_0²=1, ε=0.05`, so `k_min=4.44`):

| fixed cost `s` | `k*` | rounds | time at `k*` | wait-all | `k=n/2` |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 5 | 12 | 1.0 | 33.2 (34.3×) | 5.5 (5.7×) |
| 0.5 | 10 | 8 | 5.3 | 36.7 (6.9×) | 9.5 (1.8×) |
| 2 | 10 | 8 | 17.3 | 47.2 (2.7×) | 21.5 (1.2×) |
| 8 | 36 | 7 | 61.7 | 89.2 (1.45×) | 69.5 (1.13×) |
| 32 | 36 | 7 | 229.7 | 257.2 (1.12×) | 261.5 (1.14×) |

`k*` is non-decreasing in `s` (tested); when the tail is negligible it is the smallest `k` that still attains the round count of waiting for all (rounds are integers, so `k*=36`, not 64, at `s=32`). Under Pareto(1.5) tails (E3b) `k*=10`, time 9.0 versus 300.6 for waiting for all (33.6×).

E4 lets the step size adapt to `k`. Because the noise floor scales as `η²σ²/(1−ρ²)`, more workers allow a larger step: at `s=2` the joint optimum is `k*=39, η*=0.92` with time 2.9 versus 17.3 at fixed `η=0.2` (5.9×; 6.9× at `s=8`), while at `s=0` the joint optimum is small (`k*=1, η*=0.04`, gain 1.09×). The `η` grid is 0.02 apart, so the large gains are partly the coarse quantisation of rounds at large steps. A fixed step therefore overstates the value of backup workers; the tuned comparison is the honest one.

## 4. The speed filter is a biased sampler
With unequal rates, worker `i` is in the fastest `k` with probability `π_i = ∫_0^1 P(#{j≠i faster} ≤ k−1 | u) du` (`u=e^{−μ_i t}`, a Poisson-binomial tail computed exactly; checked against `μ_1/(μ_1+μ_2)`, `Σπ_i=k`, and simulation to 0.01). If worker `i`'s local optimum is `θ_i`, the plain average of the selected set has expectation `Σ_i π_i θ_i/k`, so its bias is `Σ_i(π_i/k − 1/n)θ_i` (exact; verified by 40000-round simulation), independent of the number of rounds and of the noise. Slow workers have small `π_i` (E5: `π_min=0.037` at `k=2` of 16), so their data is nearly absent. The Horvitz–Thompson estimate `(1/n)Σ_{i∈S} g_i/π_i` is unbiased for any `k`; the self-normalised (Hájek) version divides by `Σ 1/π_i` instead.

E5 (`n=16`, log-rates `0.7 z_i`, `z` in [−1.5,1.5], unit noise, 6000 rounds, seed 5): with no coupling between speed and data (bias 0) plain is best (MSE 0.124 at `k=8` versus HT 0.151, Hájek 0.147; 0.245 versus 0.334 at `k=4`) because reweighting adds variance. With `θ_i = −z_i` (slow workers hold the large optima) the bias is −0.53 (`k=2`), −0.38 (`k=8`), −0.23 (`k=12`), 0 (`k=16`); MSE plain/HT/Hájek at `k=8` is 0.309/0.244/0.237 and at `k=12` 0.144/0.109/0.108; at `k=2` HT is worse (1.41 versus 1.11 plain) because weights are large. Round time from `k=12` to `k=16` rises 1.41 → 5.44: removing the bias by waiting costs 3.9× per round, reweighting costs nothing in time.

## 5. Implications
(i) Choose `k` from the data: `k_min` from the noise floor is a hard lower limit, the optimum lies above it and moves toward `n` as the fixed cost `s` grows. (ii) With heavy tails, backup workers are needed for a finite mean round time; drop at least `⌈1/α⌉−1`. (iii) Tune the step with `k`; a fixed step overstates the gain. (iv) If speed and data are correlated the speed filter needs a correction. Reweight when the bias is a large share of the MSE (Hájek is a safe default), and prefer plain averaging when heterogeneity is only in speed. (v) The inclusion probabilities `π_i` can be published, since a verifier can recompute them from committed speed profiles, which suits protocols that pay per accepted gradient (see `hetero-pipeline`, `local-sgd-bias`, `pipeline-replication`).

## 6. Limits
Independent exponential/Pareto finishing times, one shift `s` for all workers, quadratic objective with additive independent gradient noise, coordinator with instantaneous aggregation, no strategic slowing or speeding (a worker paid per accepted gradient would race; this is not modelled), rates known when computing `π_i`, and `θ_i` treated as fixed local optima (bias in the quadratic model is the exact analogue of the stationary bias of local-SGD-type schemes). The `η` grid in E4 is coarse. Simulation statistics are single-seed (seeds in `experiments/run.py`). Related: backup workers (Chen, Monga, Bengio, Jozefowicz 2016), order statistics (Rényi 1953), stragglers and coded computation (Lee et al. 2018; Dutta et al. 2016), Horvitz–Thompson estimation, and asynchronous/decentralised training over heterogeneous devices.

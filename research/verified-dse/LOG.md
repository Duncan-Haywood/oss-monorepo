## 2026-10-25 · Milestone 13: does a gentler cut than eta = 1.5, or filtering on R3 only, remove the halving floor? (`experiments/eta_floor/`)
- Why: milestone 12 chose eta = 1.5 in nearly every fold, the edge of its grid, and could not separate the cut from the ranking quality. This step extends the grid to {1.1, 1.25, 1.5, 2, 3} and adds two arms that do not cut after R2 and keep 1/2 or 1/3 after R3 ("f2", "f3"). Everything else is milestone 12's (cost model, pools, 400 paired seeds, leave-one-design-out choice, `sweep.py` copied and extended, about 30 s, no flow run).
- Disclosure: the predictions were not written before the run in this entry. The only plan on record is milestone 12's "Next", so read the outcome as exploratory, with 7 arms by 4 tables-clocks by 4 to 5 budgets compared and no multiplicity correction.

### Outcome
- **The relaxed-clock loss in the small designs is a property of the cut size.** Fixed eta = 1.5 gives 1.30 against random's 1.45 at 30 s and 0.24 against 0.55 at 45 s; eta = 1.25 gives 1.21 at 30 s and 4.90 against 5.69 at 10 s. Milestone 12 saw eta = 2 lose here (1.89, 1.27). Tight clock: eta = 1.5 gives 1.49 against 2.06 at 30 s and 0.42 against 0.94 at 45 s. In-sample fixed values, not held-out.
- **Held-out choice is not what delivers it.** The leave-one-design-out choice picks 1.25 or 1.5 in 9 of 10 folds but picks 2 for barrel16, where that is a bad choice, so the held-out mean is worse than fixed eta = 1.25 or 1.5 (relaxed 30 s: 2.17 against 1.21 to 1.30). One design drives that gap; barrel16 was also the design with the worst losses in milestones 3 and 11.
- **Larger designs: the floor is removed by a gentler cut.** Relaxed clock at 600 s, eta = 1.5 gives 0.00 and eta = 1.1 or 1.25 give 0.46 and 0.17 against random's 0.83, while eta = 3 stays at 5.03 and eta = 2 at 2.99. At 900 s eta = 1.1, 1.25 and 1.5 all reach 0.00 against random's 0.18. Tight clock, 900 s: 0.00 for 1.1 and 1.25.
- **Filtering on R3 only does about as well as a gentle cut.** f3 is the best fixed arm on the larger designs at the relaxed clock for 100 to 400 s (4.27, 1.71, 0.21 against 5.94, 2.53, 0.40 for eta = 1.5), and f2 is close to eta = 1.5 elsewhere. So most of the benefit comes from not discarding by R2 rank, and R3 is informative enough to cut on. At the tight clock on the larger designs eta = 3 and 2 stay best from 100 to 200 s (1.34, 0.33 against 2.00 and 0.83 for 1.5), so there a harder cut wins.
- Limits: 5 larger designs of which three are one family; in-sample fixed-eta numbers are selected after seeing the grid; the held-out column covers selection among 7 arms using 4 or 9 training designs; "f3" at 600 s and over keeps most of the pool through R3, so it approaches routing the survivors of an R3 sort; pool sizes of 50 to 60 cap how small a cut can matter. No new flow runs.
- Next: a paired significance test of fixed eta = 1.5 against random per design, a per-design-family fold (so the three-multiplier family leaves together), and the second corner.

## 2026-10-25 · Milestone 12: is eta = 2 for halving from R2 a real gain once eta is chosen on other designs? (planned before running)
- Why: milestone 11 found, after trying it as one of two variants, that SH from R2 with eta = 2 beat random R4 on 4 to 7 of 10 small designs at the tight clock and had the lowest floor on the larger designs. Five strategies by two tables by two clocks by four or five budgets was many comparisons, and eta = 2 was picked after seeing the numbers. This step tests it out of sample.
- Method (`experiments/eta_sweep/sweep.py`, no flow run): milestone 3 / 9 / 11 cost model, pools, 400 paired seeds, regret definition and both tables and clocks. SH from R2 with eta in {1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 4} (kept set at each rung = floor(n / eta), at least 1; the initial pool is the largest that fits the budget), against random R4. Leave-one-design-out: for each table, clock and budget, eta is chosen as the value with the lowest mean regret over the other designs, then scored on the held-out design. Report the mean held-out regret of the chosen eta, of fixed eta = 2, of fixed eta = 3, of the in-sample oracle best eta per design, and of random R4, with paired wins and losses of chosen-eta against random by design (95% CI over seeds).
- **H12-i:** at the tight clock on the small designs, the held-out chosen eta has lower mean regret than random R4 at 20, 30 and 45 s. Refuted if it is not lower at two of the three.
- **H12-ii:** at the relaxed clock on the small designs the chosen eta does not beat random R4 on mean regret at 30 and 45 s (milestone 11 saw 1.89 against 1.45 and 1.27 against 0.55 for fixed eta = 2). Refuted if it does.
- **H12-iii:** the chosen eta is 2 or smaller in most held-out folds on the larger designs at the tight clock (gentler cuts lower the bias floor). Refuted if the modal chosen eta there is 3 or 4.
- Caveats set in advance: 10 and 5 designs, so the selection folds have 9 and 4 training designs; three of the five larger designs are one family; 20 cells; the win/loss counts are descriptive.

### Outcome (`experiments/eta_sweep/`: `sweep.py` about 30 s, `results.txt`, `summary.json`; no flow run)
- **H12-i held, narrowly.** Tight clock, small designs, chosen eta against random R4: 3.15 against 3.33 at 20 s, 1.93 against 2.06 at 30 s, but 1.27 against 0.94 at 45 s. Two of three, as the refutation rule allowed; the margins at 20 and 30 s are 0.2 and 0.1 points, with paired wins/losses of 5/1 and 7/1 over designs. Fixed eta = 2 (0.57 at 45 s) does better than the held-out choice there, so selecting eta on other designs costs something.
- **H12-ii held.** Relaxed clock, small designs: chosen eta 2.15 against random 1.45 at 30 s and 1.39 against 0.55 at 45 s. Nothing here removes the relaxed-clock loss.
- **H12-iii held.** Larger designs, tight clock: the chosen eta is 2 or below in 16 of 25 folds (2 at 400 s; 1.5 at 600 and 900 s); at 100 s it is mostly 3 and at 200 s 2.5, so the "gentler cut" is a large-budget effect.
- **Unpredicted:** on the larger designs at the relaxed clock the chosen eta is 1.5 in nearly every fold and it beats random from 200 s: 2.53 against 4.85 at 200 s, 0.40 against 2.03 at 400 s, 0.00 against 0.83 at 600 s, with 4/0 wins. Fixed eta = 3 floors at 5.03 there. So the milestone 9 "bias floor" is a property of the aggressive cut, not of the R2 ranking, at least with this pool of five designs (three of one family). At 600 s and over, eta = 1.5 keeps most of the pool through R3, so it is close to filtering with R3 then routing; I have not separated that from the benefit of multi-fidelity ranking.
- Caveats: 1.5 is the edge of the grid and is chosen in nearly every small-design fold, so the optimum may lie lower and the grid, not the data, bounds the answer; with five larger designs, folds train on four; 20 cells and descriptive win/loss counts; a held-out design is scored with the same seeds as the pool it was drawn from (pool of 50 or 60 points, not fresh designs).
- Next: extend the grid below 1.5 (1.25, 1.1) and add an arm "filter on R3 only, then route", to separate the cut from ranking quality; the second corner and a non-arithmetic family still need new flow runs.

## 2026-10-25 · Milestone 11: can a two-arm hedge remove the bias floor of successive halving from R2? (planned before running)
- Why: milestone 9 found SH from R2 beats random search at 100 to 400 s on the larger designs but then floors (0.30% tight, 5.03% relaxed) because the R2 cut never lets the true optimum reach the routed rung; random search overtakes it from 600 s. Milestone 8's Hyperband hedged with equal brackets and lost the quarter of the budget it spent. A cheaper hedge: give SH from R2 a fraction f of the budget and spend the rest routing random points, answer = the best routed value of the two arms. Also a gentler cut (eta = 2) in place of eta = 3.
- Method (`experiments/hedge/replay.py`, no flow run): milestone 3 / 9 cost model, pool, seeds (400 paired) and regret definition, on both cached tables (10 small designs, budgets 10 to 45 s; 5 larger designs, budgets 100 to 900 s), both clocks. Strategies: random R4; SH from R2 (eta 3); SH from R2 with eta 2; hedge f = 0.5 and f = 0.75 (SH arm eta 3, random arm independent draw).
- **Predictions on record:** (1) On the larger designs at 600 s and over, hedge f = 0.75 has lower mean regret than SH from R2 alone at both clocks (it removes the floor) and is within 0.1 percentage points of random R4 at 900 s. (2) At 100 to 200 s on the larger designs, tight clock, hedge f = 0.75 keeps most of SH's head start (regret at 200 s below random's 1.85 and above SH's 0.33). (3) On the small designs the hedge does not beat random R4 at 10 to 30 s at either clock (consistent with milestones 3 and 8), and eta = 2 is no better than eta = 3 anywhere. If the hedge beats both random and SH from R2 at every budget on the larger designs, the bias floor is a fixable policy problem and the paper's H2 story becomes positive for the routed-expensive regime.

### Outcome (`experiments/hedge/`: `replay.py` about 60 s, `results.txt`, `summary.json`; no flow run)
- **Prediction (1) half held.** Hedge f = 0.75 has lower mean regret than SH from R2 at 600 and 900 s on the larger designs at both clocks (tight 0.23 and 0.21 against 0.30; relaxed 2.88 and 2.21 against 5.03), so the floor is partly removed. It is not within 0.1 points of random at 900 s (0.21 against 0.04 tight; 2.21 against 0.18 relaxed): the 25% of the budget given to the random arm is the price, and at the relaxed clock the SH arm still wastes its share. Hedge f = 0.5 does better at the relaxed clock (0.74 at 900 s) and is the better hedge there.
- **Prediction (2) held.** Larger designs, tight clock, 200 s: hedge f = 0.75 0.66%, between SH's 0.33 and random's 1.85.
- **Prediction (3) half held, half wrong.** Neither hedge beats random R4 on the small designs at 10 to 30 s at either clock (0 wins at the tight clock; 0 to 3 at the relaxed one). But **SH from R2 with eta = 2 is not worse than eta = 3: it is the best strategy tried in several cells.** Small designs, tight clock: 3.13 against random 3.33 at 20 s (4 wins, 2 losses), 1.92 against 2.06 at 30 s (7/2), 0.57 against 0.94 at 45 s (6/2; worst design 3.2 against 4.7). Relaxed clock: it beats random on 6 to 8 designs at every budget but its mean is not below random's beyond 10 s (worst-design regret 10 to 12% against 2 to 6%). Larger designs, tight: 0.11 at 400 s onward against random's 0.68 and 0.26 (floor 0.11, lower than eta = 3's 0.30), losing to random only from 900 s. Relaxed: worse than random from 200 s, floor 2.99.
- Reading: a gentler cut (eta = 2) lowers the bias floor at the tight clock, where the R2 ranking is good, and the plain random hedge costs more than it buys. This reverses milestone 3's reading that SH from R2 does not pay on the small designs: at the tight clock and 20 s or more, eta = 2 beats random on 4 to 7 of 10 designs. The relaxed clock remains the stress case, where R2's ranking misleads on a few designs and nothing here removes a 10 to 14% worst-design loss.
- Caveats: eta = 2 was added as one of two variants I tried and not tuned further, but five strategies by two tables by two clocks by four or five budgets is many comparisons, so the win/loss counts are descriptive, not a tested claim; same pools of 50, 10 and 5 designs (three of the five larger ones one family); costs from single-core runs; the seed is shared across strategies (paired), the bootstrap is over seeds within a design, not over designs.
- Next: an eta sweep (1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 4) with a held-out design split so eta is chosen on some designs and scored on others; the second corner and a non-arithmetic family still need new flow runs.

## 2026-10-25 · Milestone 8: Hyperband across the four rungs at equal wall-clock (planned before running)
- Why: PAPER.md lists Hyperband as a missing baseline. Plain SH from one rung commits to that rung's bias (milestone 3: R1 is a loss, R2 is a gamble); Hyperband hedges by running several SH brackets that start at different rungs. Replayed on the cached table with milestone 3's cost model, seeds and pool, so it is paired with random R4 and SH.
- Method (`experiments/hyperband/replay.py`, no flow run): four brackets, one per start rung (R1, R2, R3, R4), eta = 3, each given B/4 seconds and run as in milestone 3 (largest n0 that fits; an unaffordable bracket returns nothing and its share is lost, not redistributed); the answer is the best routed value over brackets; brackets draw from the whole pool independently (as Hyperband does). Same budgets (5 to 45 s), 400 paired seeds, 10 designs x 2 clocks. Hyperband-lite (R1 and R2 brackets only, B/2 each) as a variant. Comparators recomputed in-script: random R4, SH from R2.
- **Predictions on record:** (1) At 10 to 30 s Hyperband's mean regret is above random R4's at both clocks, because a quarter of the budget goes to R1 and R4 brackets that cannot pay for themselves in a 50-point pool. (2) It beats SH from R1 at every budget of 10 s or more (it never commits to R1 alone). (3) It does not beat SH from R2 at the relaxed clock for budgets up to 30 s (the R2 bracket gets a quarter of the budget). If Hyperband beats random at 20 s or more, hedging over rungs is worth its cost on this table and milestone 3's reading changes.

### Outcome (`experiments/hyperband/`: `replay.py` about 30 s, `results.txt`, `summary.json`; the SH-from-R2 and random columns reproduce milestone 3's numbers exactly, a check on the shared cost model)
- **Prediction (1) held.** Hyperband over four brackets (`hb_all`) has higher mean regret than random R4 at every budget of 10 to 45 s, both clocks, and beats random on 0 designs in every cell. Tight clock, % (random / SH from R2 / `hb_all`): 10 s 6.27 / 8.89 / 7.53; 20 s 3.33 / 5.85 / 4.71; 30 s 2.06 / 4.53 / 3.34; 45 s 0.94 / 2.87 / 2.51. Relaxed: 20 s 2.68 / 3.23 / 4.07; 45 s 0.55 / 1.68 / 1.64. At 5 s no bracket is affordable on a quarter of the budget (`hb_all` is undefined).
- **Prediction (2) held.** `hb_all` beats SH from R1 (8 to 10% at 10 s or more, milestone 3) at every budget of 10 s or more on both clocks.
- **Prediction (3) half held.** At the relaxed clock `hb_all` is worse than SH from R2 up to 30 s (6.82 vs 5.83, 4.07 vs 3.23, 2.60 vs 2.22) and ties it at 45 s (1.64 vs 1.68). At the tight clock I did not expect it to win and it does: better than SH from R2 at 10 to 45 s (7.53 vs 8.89 at 10 s, 2.51 vs 2.87 at 45 s). The two-bracket variant (R1, R2 only) is worse than `hb_all` from 20 s (6.8 vs 4.7 tight at 20 s, 5.6 vs 2.5 at 45 s): its R1 bracket spends half the budget on a rung that ranks like noise, and its R2 bracket is too small.
- **What hedging buys is a thinner tail, not a lower mean.** Worst-design regret at 45 s, relaxed: `hb_all` 4.6 against 11.8 for SH from R2 (random 1.8); tight: 11.1 against 13.9. The R4 bracket is a random-search insurance policy that SH from R2 lacks, and it caps the loss on the designs where R2 misleads (barrel16, ripple adder). It never makes up the quarter of the budget it costs in a pool of 50 points.
- Limits: brackets split the budget equally and unaffordable brackets lose their share (Hyperband proper allocates by a schedule over a much larger pool); the table is 50 points per design, so the R4 bracket nearly equals random search and all strategies converge to 0 by 60 s (not run here); eta = 3 only; one corner; the paired win/loss counts are over 10 designs.
- Next: BOHB or BO needs a bigger pool than 50 points to say anything (a model has nothing to fit); the larger-design set remains the blocker for H2. Coverage proof and the MLCAD template are still open.

## 2026-10-25 · Milestone 7c: does the top-vs-all bound survive smaller pilots, and does the hypergeometric fact explain when? (planned before running)
- Why: 7b's B statistic covered everywhere, but with a pilot of 16 from pools of about 50 (a third of the pool) it sees most of the pool's best few. Whether B is a pilot-checkable guarantee depends on the pilot missing them. Sweep the pilot size m in {6, 8, 12, 16, 24} and top in {1, 3, 5}, same 20 groups, both halving starts, 200 pilots x 10 tie-breaks per cell (`experiments/theory/sweep.py`, no flow run).
- Fact to check, not assume: P(the pool's single best point is in a pilot of m from n) = m / n; P(at least one of the pool's best j in the pilot) = 1 - C(n-j, m)/C(n, m) (hypergeometric). Compare, per m, the empirical share of pilots whose top-`top` contains a pool top-3 point with this formula (it should match up to sampling error: it is a counting fact, so a mismatch is a bug).
- **Predictions on record:** (1) coverage of B falls as m shrinks and top grows weaker: at m = 6 at least 8 of 40 group-start cells (pooled over top = 3) fall below 0.8; at m = 24 none do. (2) Coverage tracks the hypergeometric probability that the pilot contains one of the pool's best few: across cells, the cell-level coverage correlates positively (Spearman above 0.3) with that probability at m = 6 and 8. (3) Bound size shrinks with m (fewer pairs seen), so informative-bound cells rise while coverage drops: the trade-off is the result. If B stays above 0.8 nearly everywhere even at m = 6, the 7b result was not a large-pilot artefact and the guarantee can be stated conditionally on a pool-best-in-pilot event of probability m/n.

### Outcome (`experiments/theory/`: `sweep.py` about 3 s, `sweep_results.txt`, `sweep_summary.json`)
- **Prediction (1) half held.** Pilot-by-pilot coverage of the top-3 statistic falls with m but less than I predicted: cells under 0.8 (of 40) are 6 at m = 6, 3 at m = 8, 1 at m = 12, 0 at m = 16 and 24 (I predicted at least 8 at m = 6; none at m = 24 held). Mean coverage over the 23 cells with realised regret above 0 is 0.855 / 0.915 / 0.969 / 0.988 / 0.995 for m = 6 / 8 / 12 / 16 / 24. A weaker top (1) fails more: 15 of 40 cells under 0.8 at m = 6, 2 at m = 16 and 24. A wider top (5) is safer (6 at m = 6, 0 from m = 16) and its bound is only slightly larger.
- **Prediction (2) could not be tested as designed.** Both pools have 50 or 52 points, so the hypergeometric probability that a pilot holds one of the pool's best three is the same in every cell for a given m, and a per-cell correlation is undefined (my design error, found when the Spearman came out NaN; the correlation was dropped from the script). ## 2026-10-25 · Milestone 7a: a distribution-free pilot quantile of pair inversions, and how much of the halving bound it keeps (planned before running)

## 2026-10-24 · Milestone 6: a regret bound for successive halving from the largest rung inversion (theory), planned before running
- Why: milestones 3 and 4 explain *that* plain halving from a biased rung loses and that per-point conformal coverage does not transfer to the argmin, but give no bound. The proposal's Method asks for a regret bound for halving as a function of the bias and the number of rungs, with the place where it is vacuous. This step states and proves one, then checks it against the cached table.
- Definition: for a rung r with score s_r and routed value y, the **inversion** iota_r = max over pairs (x, x') with s_r(x) <= s_r(x') of log y(x) - log y(x') (clipped at 0): the worst the rung can do by ranking x ahead of or level with x' when x is in fact worse by that factor. It needs no calibration and no model of the bias; it is invariant to monotone rescaling of the rung.
- **Proposition (to prove in `theory/BOUND.md`):** successive halving that keeps the top k of n entrants by s_r at each cut, and answers with the best routed value among the final survivors, returns y with log y - log y* <= sum of iota_r over the cut rungs (the final routed rung is exact). Proof is by induction: if the optimum of the entrants is cut, at least k entrants rank at or above it, and each of them is within iota_r of it. A property test on random instances checks the statement (it can only fail if the proof does).
- Experiment (`experiments/theory/bound.py`, no flow run): for each of the 20 design x clock groups of the cached table compute iota_1, iota_2, iota_3 over the pool; the realised log-regret of full-pool halving from R1 and from R2 (eta = 3, n0 = pool size, 200 random tie-breaks); the bound; and the ratio realised / bound. Also what a routed pilot of m = 10 random points sees of iota_r (its own max inversion over the pilot pairs, a lower bound by construction), 200 draws.
- **Predictions on record:** (1) the bound holds on every group (it is a theorem; a violation is a bug). (2) It is vacuous for R1 (iota_1 near the whole spread of routed values, since R1 ranks like noise: bound above 30% on most groups) and informative only for R2 at the relaxed clock (iota_2 under about 5%). (3) It is loose where it is informative: realised regret is typically a small fraction of the bound, because it charges the worst pair, not the pair that matters. (4) A 10-point pilot sees only a small part of iota (median under half), so iota is not pilot-checkable at the sizes that make the pilot cheap; the probabilistic version (a quantile of inversions instead of the maximum) is the open part and goes in the paper as such. If (4) is wrong and the pilot recovers most of iota, the "pilot-checkable bias" assumption of the proposal is realistic.


### Outcome (`experiments/theory/`: `BOUND.md` proof, `bound.py` about 10 s, `src/verified_dse/bound.py` with 4 new tests)
- **The bound is proved and holds in all 40 cells** (20 groups x halving from R1 and from R2, realised mean log-regret never above the sum of inversions; the property test in `tests/test_bound.py` checks 500 random instances including ties). Prediction (1) held, as it must for a theorem.
- **Prediction (2) half held.** The generic-gate rung is vacuous: iota_1 is above 0.3 (a 35% factor) on 8 of 10 groups per clock, median 0.36, so halving from R1 has a bound of 0.71 (tight) and 0.63 (relaxed) in log units. But I expected the mapped rungs to be informative at the relaxed clock (under 5%) and they are not: median iota_2 is 0.16 (tight) and 0.14 (relaxed), with only 3 of 10 groups below 0.05 at the relaxed clock, so the R2 -> R4 bound has median 0.35 / 0.23. A single bad pair in a pool of 50 sets iota.
- **Prediction (3) held strongly.** Realised regret of full-pool halving is 0.10 (tight) / 0.06 (relaxed) from R1, median 15% / 11% of its bound, and 0.000 median from R2 (it finds the routed optimum on most groups; it is not equal-budget, n0 is the whole pool). The worst-pair bound explains why plain R1 halving can lose and says nothing about how often.
- **Prediction (4) was wrong in the useful direction.** A 10-point random pilot sees a median 0.67 / 0.56 / 0.52 of iota_1 / iota_2 / iota_3 (over 19 to 20 groups, medians over 200 draws), above the half I predicted; per group it ranges from 0 to 1 (crc16 R3 relaxed: 0, because one extreme pair). So a pilot gives the order of magnitude of the inversion but never a certificate: its maximum is a lower bound by construction.
- Limits: one table (20 groups, pools of about 50, one corner); iota is measured on the pool that is searched, so on a new design it is an unknown, estimated only through a pilot; the bound ignores k_j and eta; no probabilistic (quantile) version yet, which is the part the paper needs for a guarantee with a stated probability; the proposition is simple enough that a bandit paper may state it already (not exhaustively checked).
- Next: the quantile version (replace the maximum with a conformal quantile of pairwise inversions, tie to the milestone-4 pilot and test coverage of the bound); then a larger or second-corner design set so the pool is not the limit; then milestone 7 (paper draft).

- **H2 (as stated in the proposal):** SH beats random R4 with a paired 95% interval excluding 0 on most (>= 6 of 10) designs at some budget below 60 s. **H3:** `sh_r1` loses more than 5% (mean regret) on at least one design at some budget that `sh_cal` brings within 5%.
- **Predictions on record:** H2 will mostly fail on this table (routed point costs only about 8x R1, the pool is about 50 points, so cheap rungs save little); `sh_r1` will be near random at the relaxed clock and worse at the tight clock because R1 ranks like noise and its eta = 3 cut discards the optimum two thirds of the time; `sh_r2` will beat `sh_r1` at the relaxed clock; the 6-point pilot is too small to separate tau 0.6 from 0.4 reliably, so `sh_cal` will sometimes start at R1 wrongly. If SH wins clearly the milestone-2 reading (cheap rungs do not pay here) is wrong.
- Limits known up front: pilot of 6 gives a noisy rho; pool of about 50 caps n0; costs are means over the table, not per point.

### Outcome (`experiments/halving/`, 10 designs, 400 paired seeds per cell, one core; `replay.py` runs in about 50 s)
- Mean routed regret over designs, % (tight clock; random R4 / SH from R1 / SH from R2 / calibrated SH): 10 s 6.3 / 11.4 / 8.9 / 5.9; 20 s 3.3 / 10.3 / 5.9 / 4.4; 30 s 2.1 / 10.1 / 4.5 / 3.6; 45 s 0.94 / 10.1 / 2.9 / 2.9. Relaxed clock, 45 s: 0.55 / 8.3 / 1.7 / 1.6. Full table in `experiments/halving/results.txt`.
- **H2 is not supported as a mean-regret result and only partly as a win count.** At the relaxed clock SH from R2 beats random R4 with a paired interval excluding 0 on 8 of 9 defined designs at 5 s and 6 to 7 of 10 at 20 to 45 s, which meets the proposal's literal "most designs" test, yet its mean regret is higher (1.7 vs 0.55 at 45 s) because one or two designs lose heavily (barrel16 about 12%, the ripple adder about 5%). At the tight clock it wins on 3 to 4 designs and loses on 4 to 6. The calibrated variant is the only one with a lower mean than random, and only at 10 s (5.9 vs 6.3, 3 wins, 0 losses tight; 6/0 relaxed). Mean regret is not the proposal's criterion, so I report both and do not call H2 refuted; the honest summary is "wins where R2 is faithful, loses badly where it is not, net no gain on this table".
- **Plain SH from R1 is the worst strategy at every budget of 10 s or more** (about 10% regret at the tight clock and 8% relaxed, flat once its n0 reaches the pool size: it never stops mis-ranking at R1). My prediction held. Why it is flat: with eta = 3 and an R1 ranking that is noise, the optimum survives the first cut with probability about 1/3 and the pool is too small to compensate.
- **H3 holds, with a caveat that matters.** Plain SH from R1 loses more than 5% on 8 of 10 designs at the tight clock (alu8 16.9%, fifo8x4 17.0%, barrel16 11.8% at 60 s) and the calibrated variant brings alu8 to 0.8%, fifo8x4 to 2.8% and barrel16 to 3.0%. But simply starting at R2 recovers alu8, crc16, mult8 and cmp_sat12 as well, so most of the recovery is "do not use R1", which a 6-point pilot only finds when it is lucky. The calibrated variant beats SH from R2 at 20 to 30 s tight (4.4 vs 5.9, 3.6 vs 4.5) and ties it from 45 s (2.85 vs 2.87), and it rarely beats random per design (0 to 1 of 10 from 45 s). Its worst design is the ripple adder (15.5%), whose routed scores take four distinct values, so a rank-based pilot cannot calibrate it.
- Prediction check: random-like SH R1 at relaxed clock held (worse, not equal); `sh_r2` beat `sh_r1` at relaxed (held); the small pilot sometimes starts at R1 wrongly (held: its tail at 5 s tight is identical to random because the pilot eats the whole budget).
- Limits: n0 is capped by the pool (about 50), so beyond about 30 s SH stops using the budget while random keeps drawing: the 45 s and 60 s columns favour random for that reason, the comparison is only fair up to about 30 s. eta fixed at 3 and a single pilot size; costs are table means; designs are small. The "(wins/losses)" counts cover only designs where the strategy was defined at that budget. No NSGA-II, Hyperband, BOHB or BO yet.
- Next: milestone 4 (conformal shortlist, H4), and a larger design set so the pool is not the limit; also SH with a per-design eta and a pilot-sized sweep.


## 2026-10-24 · Milestone 2: search baselines at equal wall-clock budget on the cached table (planned before running)
- Why: H2 needs baselines before any multi-fidelity method. The 1,200-run table from milestone 1 holds every rung's score and measured time for 50 to 52 usable sequences per design and clock target, so a search can be replayed on it with no re-running of the flow (cost model: a point at R1 costs the mean `t1`; at R4 costs mean `t2_yosys + t_pnr_total`; R2 costs `t2_yosys + t_pnr_to_synth_netlist`).
- Strategies, all on equal budgets B seconds, regret = routed (R4) score of the best point found over the pool minimum, minus 1; 400 seeds per cell, same seeds for every strategy (paired): (a) random search on R4; (b) hill climbing on R4 (neighbour = Levenshtein distance 1 over pass names within the cached pool, random restart when stuck); (c) screen the whole pool at R1 then spend the rest on R4 in R1 order; (d) the same at R2. Budgets 5, 10, 20, 30, 45 s.
- Limits I know up front: the pool is only about 50 points, so at 45 s random search has seen about 35 of 50 and every strategy converges to regret 0 as B grows; screening the pool at R2 costs about 40 s, which eats most of the range. This benchmark can only show the small-budget end, and it cannot yet speak to H2 at scale. Hill climbing is restricted to the cached pool, so its neighbourhoods are sparse (that handicaps it).
- **Prediction (on record):** (c) R1 screening does no better than random search at any budget (R1 tau is about 0), perhaps worse; (d) R2 screening beats random at the relaxed clock for B near its screening cost, and not at the tight clock; hill climbing is not better than random because neighbourhoods are sparse. If R1 screening beats random, H1's practical bite is smaller than its tau suggests.

### Outcome (`experiments/search_baselines/`, 10 designs, 400 paired seeds per cell, one core; `replay.py` runs in about 75 s)
- Mean routed regret over designs (%, tight clock; random / hill climbing / R1 screen / R2 screen): 5 s 10.7 / 13.5 / n/a / n/a; 10 s 6.3 / 7.4 / 15.0 / n/a; 20 s 3.3 / 3.3 / 6.5 / n/a; 30 s 2.1 / 1.7 / 5.3 / n/a; 45 s 0.94 / 0.61 / 1.08 / 1.27. Relaxed clock: 45 s 0.55 / 0.49 / 1.41 / 1.35.
- **R1 screening prediction held for budgets up to 30 s:** it is worse than random search on 10 of 10 designs at 10 s and its mean regret is 2 to 3 times random's up to 30 s (it spends 7.4 s of the budget scoring the pool with a score that ranks like noise). At 45 s the means are close (1.08 vs 0.94, tight) and the per-design picture is mixed (beats random on 7, loses on 2), so screening helps where R1 happens to be informative and costs a lot where it is not; the mean is set by the bad designs.
- **R2 screening prediction only half held:** at 45 s, relaxed clock, it beats random on 8 designs and loses on 1, but its mean regret is higher (1.35 vs 0.55), so the one loss is large. At the tight clock it is 6 wins, 2 losses and a higher mean (1.27 vs 0.94). At every smaller budget it cannot pay for itself: screening the pool of about 50 costs 38 s.
- **Hill climbing prediction was wrong:** it is not better than random at small budgets (13.5 vs 10.7 at 5 s) but is better from 30 s at the tight clock (1.68 vs 2.06; 0.61 vs 0.94 at 45 s), even with neighbourhoods restricted to the cached pool. Not tested for significance across designs; indicates local structure in the pass-sequence space.
- Reading: on this table the plain baselines are strong and the cheap rungs, used as a one-shot screen, do not beat them at equal wall-clock. That is an argument for H3 (blind screening is not safe) and against expecting H2 from a single screen; it does not test successive halving or a calibrated screen, which spend the cheap rung adaptively. The benchmark cannot show H2 itself: routed points cost only 8x R1 here, and the pool is 50 points.
- Limits: pool of about 50 sampled sequences per design (not the 400-sequence space); mean costs not per-design; no NSGA-II, Hyperband, BOHB or BO yet (proposal baselines); one corner. Not done: tests for the replay script (it runs at import; refactor when milestone 3 reuses it). Results are deterministic (seeded; an earlier draft used Python's salted `hash` and was fixed before the reported run).
- Next: milestone 3, successive halving across rungs on this table, and a larger design set so the R4 / R1 cost ratio is not 8.

## 2026-10-24 · Milestone 1: rung-bias benchmark (H1), planned before running
- Design set (10): adder (`+`, ripple, W=16), 8-bit multiplier, 4x4 MAC, 8-bit ALU, 16-bit popcount, 16-bit barrel shifter, CRC-16 byte update, 12-bit saturating add, 8x4 FIFO. Original small RTL, MIT; four are copied from `chip-pnr`/`chip-ppa-sky130mac`.
- Space: ABC pass sequences of length 0 to 3 over {balance, rewrite, rewrite -z, refactor, refactor -z, dc2, dch} (the `chip-scriptsearch` space, 400 sequences), 60 drawn with seed 0 (the empty sequence plus 59 random), the same 60 for every design; sequences ABC rejects are counted and dropped.
- Rungs: R1 generic-gate cells x depth (Yosys/ABC `-g`); R2 SKY130 HD mapped area x no-wire OpenSTA period; R3 post-detailed-placement area x period; R4 global route + `repair_timing` area x period (the reference, "routed"). Score = area x period (um^2 ns), as `chip-scriptsearch`. Flow is `chip-pnr`'s OpenROAD script, 40% utilisation.
- Clock target: the design's own baseline (empty-sequence) no-wire period ("tight", where wires force repair) and 1.5x that ("relaxed"), to see whether the answer depends on repair pressure.
- Analysis fixed now: Kendall tau-b of each rung against R4 per design and period, 95% bootstrap interval over sequences (2000 resamples); regret of the rung's pick (mean over ties) = R4(pick)/min R4 - 1; whether the rung's top 5 contains the R4 optimum. H1 holds if R1's tau is below 0.7 on at least 4 of 10 designs (at the tight period) and is refuted if tau is at least 0.7 on 9 or more. Also report R2 and R3, and rung wall times.
- Expectation I am on record with: R1 will do worst, R2 will already be close to R4 on most designs. If R1 is fine, H1 is refuted and the programme's premise (cheap rung is biased) needs rethinking for pass-sequence spaces.

### Outcome (same day, `experiments/rung_bias/`)
- 10 designs x 60 sequences x 2 clock targets = 1,200 runs; ABC aborted on 196 (all of them contain a `dch` followed later by `rewrite`/`refactor`, though 44 successful runs have that pattern too, so it is a necessary, not a sufficient, condition; 10 of the 60 sequences per design failed), leaving 50 usable sequences per design (52 for the ripple adder, whose 4 distinct routed scores make it nearly uninformative).
- **H1 holds.** The generic-gate rung (R1) has tau-b below 0.7 against routed area x delay on all 9 designs where it is defined (median -0.04 tight, -0.01 relaxed; with a 95% interval entirely below 0 on popcount16 and crc16 at the tight target). For the ripple adder R1 is constant, so tau is undefined (counted as uninformative). R1's pick costs a median 13.5% (tight) and 20.0% (relaxed) of routed area x delay.
- **My expectation was half wrong.** I expected R2 (liberty-mapped, no wires) to be close to routed on most designs. At the tight clock target it is not: tau below 0.7 on 6 of 10 designs, median 0.59, median pick regret 10.5%. At the relaxed target it is: tau below 0.7 on 3 of 10, median regret 0.0%. Repair pressure, which R2 cannot see, drives the bias. R3 (after placement) is no better than R2 at the tight target (7 of 10 below 0.7), because the timing repair that follows is what reorders designs.
- Rung wall time on these small designs: R1 0.15 s, R2 0.46 s of Yosys plus 0.32 s of OpenROAD load, R3 0.51 s, R4 0.80 s per point (OpenROAD stage times; add the 0.46 s mapping for cumulative cost) on one core. The routed rung (0.46 s mapping + 0.80 s OpenROAD = 1.3 s) is only about 8x the generic one (0.15 s) here, so the savings multi-fidelity search can claim on this benchmark are small; the benchmark needs larger designs before H2 is testable.
- Caveats: one library corner and one utilisation (40%); "routed" is global-route-estimated with `repair_timing`, not detailed-routed; sequences are the same 60 for every design; the clock targets are tied to each design's own baseline, so targets differ across designs; bootstrap intervals resample sequences within a design, not designs; no multiple-comparison correction.
- Next: milestone 2, search baselines at equal budget on this cached table; and add larger designs (the 0.8 s routed rung makes the cost ratio the weak point).

# Twin elicitation: what to pay a digital-twin provider for

*Stylised: scalar LQ plant `x' = a x + b u + w`, `a=0.9, q=1, r=0.1, σ²=1`, `a` known, the input gain `b` uncertain. The provider's posterior over `b` is a truncated normal (or a two-point law); the deployed controller is the Riccati gain `k*(b̂)` of the reported parameter or a directly reported gain. Pure Python; see `tests/` (8 tests) and `experiments/results.txt`.*

## Question
Twin-transfer showed that a twin can fit passive logs perfectly and still mistrain a controller, and randomized-twin showed that domain randomization is a Bayes decision under parameter uncertainty. This project asks the *incentive* question that follows. A principal buys a twin from a provider who knows more than they do. Which payment makes the provider report the twin that is best to train on? The usual answer, a proper score on the parameter, elicits the posterior mean; the controller does not want the mean.

## Setup
A provider holds posterior `π` over `b`. Deployed cost is `J_b(k) = σ²(q+rk²)/(1−(a−bk)²)`, infinite past the stability cliff. Two payments: **(B)** Brier, `−(b̂−b)²`, whose report is `E_π b`; **(C)** the negative deployed cost, `−J_b(k*(b̂))`, optionally capped at `M`. Payment (C) is a proper loss for the *Bayes gain* `k_π = argmin_k E_π J_b(k)`: truthful reporting means stating the gain (or, if a parameter must be stated, the *decision-equivalent estimate* `b_dec` with `k*(b_dec) = k_π`).

## Results
1. **Cost payment and Brier payment elicit different twins.** For mean 1 and posterior sd 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3 the Brier report is 1 every time; the cost-paid gain is 0.8221, 0.8182, 0.8031, 0.7784 against the certainty-equivalent 0.8233, so `b_dec` = 1.0019, 1.0075, 1.0299, 1.0684. The small-variance law `k_π − k*(E b) = −Var(b)·J_kbb/(2J_kk)` matches to 0.5% (0.8027 vs 0.8031 at sd 0.2, using the truncated law's own variance). The mean is not a sufficient statistic for the decision: two providers with identical mean reports and different spreads should be paid, and should train, differently. (For a uniform posterior this is exactly randomized-twin's gain `k_ε`, so domain randomization is the Bayes decision under the provider's own uncertainty, not a separate heuristic.)
2. **Certainty equivalence costs a quartic in spread.** Deploying `k*(E b)` instead of the Bayes gain adds `2.0·10⁻⁶`, `3.2·10⁻⁵`, `5.7·10⁻⁴`, `3.5·10⁻³` expected cost at sd 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3; doubling sd multiplies it by 16.4 then 17.6, tracking `(J_kk/2)(Δk)²` to 3% for sd ≤ 0.1 and undershooting it by 30% at sd 0.3. The mean report is nearly sufficient when the twin is precise and quickly not when it is not.
3. **Equal Brier score, unequal decisions.** With true `b=1`, reports `1−d` and `1+d` tie on squared error but the low report (higher gain, nearer the cliff) has regret 0.0066 / 0.0314 / 0.087 / 0.43 / 3.2 at `d` = 0.1 / 0.2 / 0.3 / 0.5 / 0.7 against 0.0049 / 0.0175 / 0.035 / 0.081 / 0.13 for the high report: ratio 1.3 to 24. The safe side depends on the plant (at `b=1.5` it reverses: 1.41 vs 0.016; at `b=2.0` the low report is unstable), so a parameter-error ranking of twins is not a ranking of controllers.
4. **A payment cap makes the elicited twin jump.** Posterior `b=0.6` w.p. 0.99, `b=2.4` w.p. 0.01: the clairvoyant-on-common-state gain `k*(0.6)=1.2146` is unstable on the rare plant (stable only below 0.7917). With no cap the provider reports 0.7369; with a cap `M` the report is exactly `k*(0.6)` for `M < M* = 19.32` and 0.7369 above it, with `M*` in closed form `[V − (1−p)J_{0.6}(k*(0.6))]/p`. A cap that is a fixed size in *dollars* silently decides whether rare catastrophic plants are priced in; the elicited gain is discontinuous in `M`. With unbounded-support Gaussian posteriors the uncapped payment is `−∞` for every gain and elicits nothing, so a cap is not optional there.
5. **Brier mispays information by an order of magnitude.** Resolving a posterior of variance `τ²` is worth `τ²` under Brier regardless of where the plant sits, but its decision value is `≈ ½J_bb(b₀, k*(b₀))·τ²` (0.733 vs the prediction 0.750 at `b₀=1, τ=0.05`). Per unit variance the decision value is 2.89, 1.13, 0.73, 0.34, 0.19 at `b₀` = 0.5, 0.8, 1, 1.5, 2: a 15× spread. A Brier-paid provider therefore under-invests in probing twins of weakly actuated systems, where a mistake is costliest, and over-invests for strongly actuated ones. Cost payment aligns the provider's incentive with the principal's exactly, because the provider's expected payment gain is the principal's expected cost saving.

## Limitations
One uncertain scalar, one provider, no strategic choice of information beyond a variance target, known `a`, risk-neutral provider, truncated normal posteriors with hard supports. The cost payment needs the deployed system's cost to be observed and attributed to the reported twin, which in practice is noisy and delayed (payments would come from the closed-loop logs whose blind spots twin-transfer characterises). Results are exact in the model and numerical to grid accuracy; they are not evidence about real robots.

## Relevance
If you procure simulators or digital twins, pay for the decision the twin will drive (or for the whole predictive distribution), not for the parameter mean; cap payments deliberately and know what the cap says about tail plants; and price probing of the twin by decision value, not squared error. This connects to the property-elicitation literature (a decision's Bayes act is an elicitable property via its own loss; Lambert–Pennock–Shoham 2008; Frongillo–Kash 2015 on elicitation complexity) and to verifiable ML training, where a computation provider is likewise paid for a report that another party will act on.

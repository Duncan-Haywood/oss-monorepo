# Timestep twin: what overshoot does a controller tuned in a fixed-step twin really have?

Pure Python, no dependencies. A second-order closed loop `x''=−k(x−1)−c x'` (time in `1/ω₀`, `c=2ζ`) is tuned for a step-response overshoot target in a twin whose integrator takes fixed steps `h`; the "real" plant is the exact flow (closed-form overshoot, checked against RK4 at `dt=10⁻³`). Each one-step integrator has an exact per-step squared pole modulus (the determinant of its step matrix): real `e^{−hc}`, explicit Euler `1−hc+h²k`, semi-implicit Euler `1−hc`, backward Euler `1/(1+hc+h²k)`. To first order the twin's decay rate is `σ_T=c/2+h·b/2` with `b=c²/2−k` (explicit), `c²/2` (semi-implicit), `k−c²/2` (implicit); `σ_T−σ` changes sign at `ζ=1/√2` for explicit and implicit Euler (measured: 0.742 → 0.711 for explicit and 0.675 → 0.703 for implicit as `h` falls 0.2 → 0.025), while semi-implicit Euler adds damping at every `ζ`. Tuned to 10% overshoot, the real overshoot is 0.105 / 0.111 / 0.121 / 0.142 for semi-implicit at `h`=0.05 / 0.1 / 0.2 / 0.4 (implicit: 0.110 / 0.120 / 0.142 / 0.192) and 0.091 / 0.081 / 0.065 / 0.036 for explicit, which is conservative; at a 1% target the semi-implicit twin under-claims by up to 4.9×. The excess over the claim halves with `h` (ratios 1.99–2.17 → 2.00 for semi-implicit/implicit, 1.80 → 1.99 explicit). Cheap repairs: Richardson extrapolation of the twin's overshoot at fixed gain (`2·o(h/2)−o(h)`) lands within 0.0012 (implicit, from `h=0.1`) and 0.0003 (from `h=0.05`) of the real value at `h=0.2`, target 10%; re-tuning with substeps needs ×4 twin cost for ±1 point and ×16–32 for ±0.2 points. See `paper/whitepaper.md`.

**Builds on.** The simulation-fidelity and sim-to-real direction of ARPG (<https://arpg.colorado.edu/>) and the field-robotics and UAV simulation direction of RECUV (<https://www.colorado.edu/recuv/>), where controllers are tuned in physics simulators before hardware trials; no specific paper from those groups is reproduced and nothing here is affiliated with or endorsed by them. The method is classical numerical analysis: modified-equation analysis of one-step integrators (Griffiths & Sanz-Serna 1986; Hairer, Lubich & Wanner 2006), with physics-engine context from Todorov, Erez & Tassa (2012) and sim-to-real context from Zhao, Queralta & Westerlund (2020). Companion to `latency-twin` (delay the twin omits) and `twin-transfer` (LQ sim-to-real regret) in this repository; here the error is the twin's own integrator, not its model.

Stylised: one linear oscillator, a textbook integrator of each kind (not any particular engine's scheme: real engines add contact, constraint solvers and substepping), overshoot read from sampled trajectories, no robot data. MIT.

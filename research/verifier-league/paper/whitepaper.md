# Verifier leagues: anytime-valid identification of the best of n verifiers

*Duncan Haywood. MIT licence. Code and experiments: `../src`, `../tests`, `../experiments`.*

## Abstract
`forecast-duel` compares two verifiers. A network usually has n and wants to know which is best, without fixing the sample size in advance. Under Brier loss the expected loss of a fixed report `r` is `(r−q)²+q(1−q)`, so the best verifier is the one whose report is closest to the outcome frequency `q`. Every pairwise duel has an anytime-valid betting e-process whose optimal growth rate is `KL(q‖(rᵢ+rⱼ)/2)`. Certifying that `i` is best means beating every rival, so the delay is `ln(1/α)` divided by the *smallest* such rate, and that rate belongs to the rival whose report *midpoint* with `i` lies nearest `q`. That is the best rival on the opposite side of `q`, not the runner-up: in a six-verifier league (q=0.6) the runner-up (r=0.52, same side) has rate 0.0086, while the third-best (r=0.72, opposite side) has rate 0.0026. The mixture e-process certifies within 1.35–1.60× the oracle prediction. Successive elimination stops about when certification does and cuts verifier evaluations 4–5× at n=8–12. Peeking z-tests declare a "strict best" in 59% of exact-tie runs at nominal 5%; the e-process, 1.6%. Stylised: fixed reports, i.i.d. tasks.

## 1. Setup
Verifier `i` reports a fixed `rᵢ` on i.i.d. binary tasks `Y~Bern(q)`. `E loss = (r−q)² + q(1−q)`, so the ranking is by `|rᵢ−q|`. For a pair, `Dᵢⱼ = lossⱼ − lossᵢ` takes two values (`d₁=(1−rⱼ)²−(1−rᵢ)²` on `Y=1`, `d₀=rⱼ²−rᵢ²` on `Y=0`), and `E Dᵢⱼ = 0` exactly at `q = (rᵢ+rⱼ)/2`. The null `Hᵢⱼ: E Dᵢⱼ ≤ 0` says "`i` is no better than `j`". A betting product `Kᵢⱼ(t)=∏(1+λDᵢⱼ,s)` is a nonnegative supermartingale under it, so by Ville `P(sup_t Kᵢⱼ ≥ 1/α) ≤ α`, at every stopping time.

## 2. Which rival binds
The best fixed bet grows at `KL(q‖(rᵢ+rⱼ)/2)` (the two-verifier result of `forecast-duel`; tested to 4 decimals by brute-force search over bets). The rate depends on rival `j` only through how far the midpoint sits from `q`. Write signed gaps `δ=r−q`. The midpoint's distance from `q` is `|δᵢ+δⱼ|/2`: `(|δᵢ|+|δⱼ|)/2` for a same-side rival, but only `||δᵢ|−|δⱼ||/2` for an opposite-side one. Hence an opposite-side rival is always harder than a same-side rival of the same loss, and the binding rival can be well down the ranking. E1 (q=0.6, best r=0.55): rival 0.52 (runner-up, same side) has rate 0.0086; 0.72 (third, opposite side) 0.0026; 0.85 0.0226; 0.42 0.0266; 0.30 0.0617. The best rival on each side matters, and only the closer-to-q of their midpoints binds. Practical reading: to speed up identification, add or replace verifiers on the *other* side of the truth from the leader, not the same side.

## 3. Certification delay
Certify `i` when `Kᵢⱼ ≥ 1/α` for every `j≠i`. Wrongly certifying a non-best `i` requires rejecting a true null `Hᵢ,b` against the true best `b`, so the error is at most `(n−1)α` by a union bound, or `α` per candidate. The oracle-bet delay is `max_j ln(1/α)/KL(q‖mid_{bj})`. E2 uses a 16-point geometric grid of bets scaled to each pair's admissible ceiling (a fixed grid on `[0,1)` wastes nearly every bet when score gaps are small, and gave ratios up to 2.0 before the fix). Mean certification times against the prediction, 300 runs each: league of E1 1835 vs 1148 (×1.60); `[.8,.3,.55,.1]` at q=.7 2802 vs 2074 (×1.35); `[.75,.52,.2]` at q=.6 1656 vs 1148 (×1.44); certified the true best in 297–300 of 300 runs. The overhead is the price of not knowing `q` (the mixture pays for the bets it does not use).

## 4. Multiplicity
Does the union bound over rivals bite? Errors require ties or near-ties, and on a line of Brier losses only a mirror-image report ties. E3 (1000 runs, T=400, α=0.05, q=0.5, best r=0.4): P(best wrongly eliminated) is 0.015 against an exact mirror tie, 0.019/0.013/0.014 with 1/3/8 near-tied opposite-side rivals and **no** correction, and 0.019/0.007/0.002 with Bonferroni `α/(n−1)`; same-side rivals never displaced it (0 of 1000). So in these runs the uncorrected procedure stayed far below α, since near-tied rivals' e-processes move together and the mixture is conservative. This is evidence, not a proof; a worst case with many *distinct* near-ties has not been constructed and Bonferroni stays the guaranteed choice. Its cost is the additive `ln(n−1)/KL_bind`: the Bonferroni prediction rises from 183 (n=2) to 330 tasks (n=12) in E5.

## 5. Peeking and elimination
E4 (exact tie r=.4 and r=.6 at q=.5, four clear losers, T=1000, 1000 runs): the "leader beats everyone at z>1.645, checked every task from t=20" rule declares a strict best in 58.8% of runs, though every declaration is wrong; the e-process in 1.6%. E5 (best 0.62, q=0.6, binding rival 0.40, the rest easier; 100 runs): successive elimination (drop `i` once some alive `j` has `Kⱼᵢ ≥ (n−1)/α`) stops at 249, 310, 356, 418 tasks for n=2, 4, 8, 12 (certification: 240, 331, 416, 397; within 15%), and costs 499, 648, 798, 1030 verifier-task evaluations against 480, 1322, 3331, 4761 if all verifiers ran until certification (2.0×, 4.2×, 4.6× cheaper at n=4, 8, 12). Since easy rivals fall early, cost grows about linearly in `n` from a fixed binding pair rather than as `n·T`.

## 6. Limits
Fixed reports and i.i.d. tasks; updating verifiers need a conditional null per round. Brier only. "Best" means best expected loss, so identical twins can never be certified over each other and the procedure returns nothing. Elimination validity uses the same union bound; we did not prove the tighter closed-form family-wise rate. The simulation grid, seeds and sample sizes are in `experiments/run.py`.

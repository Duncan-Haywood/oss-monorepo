# Escalating bonds in dispute games: cheap challenges, a fixed deterrence outlay, and a γ-fold capital asymmetry

*Duncan Haywood. MIT licence. Code and exact experiments: `../src`, `../tests`, `../experiments`.*

## Abstract
Optimistic verification schemes for decentralised training and compute (refereed delegation, fraud proofs, appeals courts) often settle disputes by *bond escalation*: each side must post a bond larger than the last, until a costly final oracle rules or one side stops. We solve a stylised version exactly by backward induction. A challenger opens against an honest defender, bond `r` is `b·γ^{r-1}`, the challenger posts the odd bonds, and after `R` bonds an oracle rules wrongly for the challenger with probability `ε`. Results: (i) the largest opening bond at which a false challenge still pays is `b* = εV / ((1−ε)C − εD)`, with `C, D` the unit-bond totals the two sides post at full escalation; it falls geometrically in `R` while the attacker's *total* outlay at `b*` moves only from `εV/(1−ε)` to `εV/(1−ε(1+1/γ))` (20.41 → 20.62 in our example), so escalation makes challenging cheap for honest parties at almost no cost to deterrence; (ii) against a capital-limited attacker the honest defender needs at most `γ×` the attacker's capital, exactly `γ·max{C(r) ≤ B_C}`, and the bound is attained: the second mover pays a factor `γ` per exchange, so slow escalation (`γ` near 1) saves capital and costs rounds; (iii) picking `γ` trades defender capital `∝ γ` against latency `∝ 1/ln γ`; (iv) the honest defender's lock-up cost is about 1.3% of the false challenger's loss at 1% interest per round and 21 rounds. All closed forms are cross-checked against the exact game and a strategic-form Nash check.

## 1. Game
Players: challenger `C` (false claim), defender `D` (honest). Bond `r` costs `b_r = bγ^{r−1}`, `C` posts odd `r`, `D` even `r`, up to `R`. On their turn a player posts or folds; folding forfeits all their posted bonds to the opponent and concedes the claim value `V` (if the folder is `D`). If all `R` bonds are posted an oracle rules for `C` with probability `ε` (its error rate). Payoffs relative to no challenge: `C` wins `V + P_D`, loses `−P_C`; `D` wins `+P_C`, loses `−V − P_D`. Cumulative budgets `B_C, B_D` may bind. Complete information, one attacker, no discounting. The game is small enough to solve exactly (`solve`), and independently as a strategic form over stop-round strategies (`nash_outcomes_stop_round`): the subgame-perfect payoffs are a Nash outcome in 300/300 random instances.

## 2. Deterrence and cheap opening
At full escalation `C` has posted `bc` and `D` has posted `bd` (unit-`b` totals `c = Σ_{odd} γ^{r−1}`, `d = Σ_{even} γ^{r−1}`). The oracle payoff to `C` is `εV + εbd − (1−ε)bc`, so `C` challenges iff `b < b* = εV/((1−ε)c − εd)`. This equals the numerical threshold from the exact game in 400/400 random instances (`ε` up to 0.4, `γ∈[1.1,3]`, `R≤9`), including the cases where `D`'s continuation is the binding constraint; we have a proof of the `C`-side unravelling but not a closed-form proof that `D` never prefers folding, so treat `ε<½` and the verified range as the claim.

The attacker's deterrence-relevant quantity is his total outlay `b*c`. As `R→∞` with `γ` fixed, `d/c → 1/γ`, so `b*c → εV/(1−ε(1+1/γ))`, against `εV/(1−ε)` for a single bond (`V=1000, ε=0.02`: 20.41 vs 20.62 at `γ=2`). Meanwhile the *opening* bond `b* ≈ b*c·(γ²−1)/γ^{R+1}` falls geometrically: from 20.4 at `R=1` to 0.06 at `R=9` and 10⁻⁵ at `R=21` (γ=2). Design consequence: fix the attacker's total loss for deterrence, then choose `R` so that an honest challenger can open with a trivial bond; the price is `R` rounds of latency and the capital effect below.

## 3. The budget race: the second mover pays γ
Set `ε=0` so `C` can win only by exhausting `D`. `D`'s even bond is `γ` times the odd bond before it, hence after any exchange `D`'s cumulative outlay is `γ` times `C`'s: `D(r+1) = γ·C(r)`. If some `C(r) ≤ B_C` has `γC(r) > B_D`, `C` can outlast `D`, who loses `V` whatever he does and so rationally folds immediately; otherwise `D` can always match and the oracle (never wrong at `ε=0`) punishes `C`. Hence **C wins the race iff `γ·max{C(r) ≤ B_C} > B_D`**, and `D` is safe iff `B_D ≥ γ·C⁺(B_C) ≤ γB_C`. This closed form matches the exact game in 500/500 random instances, and the bound `γ·B_C` is attained when `B_C` equals a cumulative outlay (γ=2: capital 21 forces 42, capital 20.9 forces 10). Over log-uniform attacker capital the average requirement is 1.1–1.3× for `γ∈[2,4]` but the worst case is `γ` (experiment E2). The attacker gets away with `1/γ` of the defender's capital. The asymmetry is structural: the responder always outbids. In any design that escalates, the party who should be *protected* (the presumptively honest solver) should not be the one forced to respond at scale; or `γ` should be kept small and `R` large.

## 4. Choosing γ; griefing
Capital needed by the defender grows as `γ`, rounds needed to reach a given opening bond shrink as `1/ln γ`. With a linear cost `λ_K·γ·P_C + λ_T·R(γ)` the optimum moves from `γ≈2.35` (`R=7`) when rounds are cheap to `γ≈3.75` (`R=5`) when they are expensive, for an opening bond ≤ 0.10 and deterrence outlay 20.4 (illustrative weights; `best_gamma`). An honest defender who wins still locks capital: with interest `r` per round its lock-up cost relative to the attacker's expected loss is 0.0001, 0.0014, 0.013 at `r`=0.01%, 0.1%, 1% per round with `R=21` (E5), so griefing by false challenges is cheap to deter unless rounds are very long.

## 5. Relation to prior work
Bond escalation appears in optimistic rollups and refereed delegation, including the Gensyn verification line (Verde, RepOps) which reduces disputes to a single operator re-execution; dispute-arity (this repository) analyses how many branches to open per round, and this module analyses the bond schedule around the same game. Peer-prediction-based alternatives (Waggoner, Frongillo, and coauthors) reduce reliance on an oracle; here the oracle's error `ε` is exactly what makes deterrence necessary. We do not claim novelty of the escalation mechanism, only these exact thresholds in this stylisation.

## 6. Limitations
Complete information; a single attacker (sybil splitting of capital or coalitions of challengers not modelled); no time value inside the game except in Section 4; the oracle error `ε` is exogenous and independent of the bond history; ties break toward folding; the mirror case (honest challenger, dishonest defender) needs the same race with roles swapped, where the responder disadvantage falls on the cheater. The closed form for `b*` is verified on 400 random instances rather than proven for all parameters. Numbers are for the stylised example, not any live protocol.

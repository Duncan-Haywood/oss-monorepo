# How big a pilot decides whether a biased digital twin is worth using? A correlation threshold, an exact decision-regret law, and an adaptive two-stage evaluation

*Stylised: the model of `research/twin-evaluation` (Gaussian real return linear in an observed, replayable scenario context; twin biased; real and twin execution noise independent). Pure Python; every number is from `experiments/results.txt` (seeded, ~6 min). Cell sizes are 3,000–20,000 simulated pilots (probabilities carry ≈0.003–0.007 sampling error). "Real" is itself a simulation; no robot data.*

## Question
`twin-evaluation` shows that a twin used as a control variate saves real rollouts, but only if its correlation `ρ` with the real return is high enough for its price, and `ρ` is not known before the twin exists and has been compared to reality. A practitioner has a small pilot of `m` real episodes replayed in the twin. How reliably does the pilot decide "use the twin or not", what does a wrong call cost, how much does planning the budget from the estimated `ρ̂` cost, and does doing all this adaptively (pilot reused in the final estimate) keep the estimator unbiased and its interval valid?

## Method
With cost ratio `r = c_T/c_R` the twin pays iff `c_T/c_R < (1−√(1−ρ²))²/ρ²` (`twin-evaluation`). Solving for `ρ` gives the equivalent, more convenient form
`ρ*(r) = 2√r/(1+r)`  (r=0.01/0.05/0.1/0.172 → ρ*=0.198/0.426/0.575/0.708),
and the optimal variance relative to real-only at the same budget is `(√(1−ρ²)+ρ√r)²` above `ρ*`, exactly 1 at `ρ*`. Its derivative there is `−2√r(1+r)/(1−r) ≠ 0`: **the gain is linear, not quadratic, in `ρ−ρ*`.** Consequently a wrong call at distance `Δ` from the threshold costs `slope·|Δ|`, and a rule "use the twin iff `ρ̂ > ρ*`" with `Var ρ̂ ≈ (1−ρ²)²/m` (Fisher) has, under a prior with density `f` near `ρ*`, expected regret (in units of the real-only variance, with a twin that is chosen being run with its optimal plan even when that is worse than real-only)
`f · slope · (1−ρ*²)² / (2m)`,
the integral of `slope·Δ·P(wrong | Δ)` over both sides. The plan itself, `(n, N) = alloc(ρ̂)`, is a second decision: its variance at the true `ρ` is `σ²((1−ρ²)/n+ρ²/N)` exactly, so plug-in regret is `≈ ½·curvature·(1−ρ²)²/m` with the curvature of that exact function at `ρ̂ = ρ`, computed numerically. Finally the adaptive procedure (pilot of `m` pairs; plan from `ρ̂`; finish the budget; OLS `λ̂` on all `n` pairs, pilot included; real-only fallback if `ρ̂ ≤ ρ*`) is simulated end to end.

## Results
(`r = 0.05`, so `ρ* = 0.426`; `C = 1000`, `c_R = 1`.)
1. **Misclassification is large exactly where it is cheap.** For `m` = 10/20/40/80/160 the probability of the wrong call is 0.494/0.468/0.436/0.396/0.353 at `ρ=0.40` (`Δ=−0.026`) and 0.361/0.325/0.267/0.195/0.114 at `ρ=0.50`, but 0.030/0.003/0/0/0 at `ρ=0.80`. Regret in units of real-only variance is 0.004–0.016 for the near-threshold twins (`ρ` = 0.40–0.50) at every `m` (it barely falls with `m` there: a call that is a coin flip costs little), and the largest cell is 0.029 (`ρ=0.60`, `m=10`); with `m ≥ 80` the regret is below 0.009 in all cells.
2. **The regret law holds asymptotically.** For `ρ ~ U(0.2,0.8)`, simulated prior-averaged regret over the law is 0.63/0.77/0.87/0.94/1.00 at `m` = 10/20/40/80/160 (0.017/0.0106/0.0060/0.0032/0.0017 against 0.028/0.0138/0.0069/0.0035/0.0017). The law is a large-`m` statement (it uses the Fisher standard deviation at `ρ*` and a linear gain); at `m=10` it overstates by 1.6×. It says the price of not knowing `ρ` decays as `1/m` with a constant proportional to the slope, i.e. a twin that is costlier (larger `r`) needs a larger pilot: the slope is 0.204/0.494/0.773/1.174 at `r` = 0.01/0.05/0.1/0.172.
3. **Plug-in allocation is the larger cost, and it is second-order.** For `ρ ≥ 0.7` (where `ρ̂` cannot cross `ρ*`), `m·regret` levels off at 0.27–0.33 for `m ≥ 80`, against the delta-method constant 0.301/0.276/0.267 at `ρ` = 0.7/0.8/0.9 (simulated 0.31/0.28/0.27 at `m=160`). So `m=20` costs 1.6–2.6% extra variance at `ρ=0.7–0.9` and `m=80` 0.4%. Near the threshold (`ρ=0.5`) fallbacks to real-only dominate and `m·regret` grows with `m` (0.36 → 1.08), because the regret there is the decision regret of item 1.
4. **The adaptive procedure is unbiased and its interval covers.** Pilot pairs are reused, the twin has bias +0.7 (true mean 1.0), `C=1000`, `r=0.05`, 3,000 replications per row: for noise-free (`ρ=0.707`), faithful (`ρ=0.5`) and weak (`ρ=0.2`) twins and `m` = 10/20/40, every |bias| ≤ 0.0020 (0.05 estimator sd; the largest, +0.0017 at ρ=0.707 and m=20, is 2.4 standard errors, out of nine rows and not repeated at other `m`), and 95% intervals cover 0.946–0.955. Variance over the oracle's (`ρ` known, continuous allocation; simulation error ±2.6%): noise-free 1.112/1.049/0.989 at `m`=10/20/40 against 1.336 for real-only; faithful 1.038/1.025/0.993 against 1.046; weak 1.080/1.003/1.008 against 1.000. The twin was used in 0.90/0.97/1.00 (noise-free), 0.64/0.68/0.72 (faithful) and 0.27/0.15/0.06 (weak) of the runs.

## Limitations
The decision law is asymptotic and local to `ρ*` (1.6× off at `m=10`); the prior `ρ~U(0.2,0.8)` is a choice, not data. `ρ` is estimated from the same model that generates the data, so nothing here tests robustness to non-Gaussian returns or to a mis-specified scenario distribution (which biases the control variate, see `twin-evaluation`). The cost of building the twin, the pilot's replay cost beyond `c_T` per pair, and any time-varying twin (`twin-upkeep`) are not modelled. The pilot's twin runs are wasted when the procedure falls back to real-only (charged at `c_T` each). The unbiasedness of the adaptive estimator is checked by simulation (bias ≤ 0.002), not proved; a sequential design that adapts `n` to data can in principle bias a naive estimator, and only 3,000 replications per row were run, so a bias below ~0.002 is not excluded. Preliminary.

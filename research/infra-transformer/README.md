# infra-transformer: how much heat-pump and EV load growth wears out a distribution transformer, and how much does smart charging buy back?

Track 21 (`infra`), milestone 2 of [`PROJECTS.md`](../PROJECTS.md) ("distribution transformer ageing under heat-pump and EV load growth"). The question is the one a utility planner asks before electrification reaches a street: at what share of homes with a heat pump and an EV does the pole-top or pad-mount transformer start losing its insulation faster than its rated life, and does charging EVs overnight, instead of on arrival, delay that? Produced with AI assistance (Claude).

**Live page:** https://marisstella.app/research/infra-transformer/

**Headline (modelled; an IEEE C57.91-form thermal model with swept, unverified constants, TMY weather, a standard load profile; not a field measurement).** For 10 homes on a transformer sized at 4.5 kVA per home (baseline peak is half the rating) in Greensboro, NC weather, loss of life stays below 0.01 %/yr until about **50 %** of homes have both a heat pump and an EV, then climbs steeply: **0.0075 %/yr at 50 %, 0.21 %/yr at 80 % and 5.6 %/yr at 100 %** with uncoordinated charging (about 18 years to end of life, but 92 % of that case's ageing comes from 49 hours above 140 C with a 207 C peak hot spot, where the guide's loading limits, not loss of life, decide, and the model is outside its range), against **0.0025, 0.035 and 1.07 %/yr** with EV charging valley-filled overnight, about 5 times less at 100 % (93 years). Smart charging recovers an EV alone completely (100 % of homes with an EV: 0.035 %/yr uncoordinated, 0.0026 smart, baseline 0.0019) but only part of the heat-pump cold-snap peaks: with 100 % heat pumps the peak is 2.4 times the rating at a transformer sized for 0.7 per unit, and no charging schedule fixes that. Uniform load growth of only **1.16x to 1.28x doubles** loss of life, but it starts from a baseline of ~0.002 %/yr, so doubling means nothing; the threshold that matters is the **rated-life rate (4.87 %/yr)**, reached at **3.1x** uniform growth at 0.5 loading. Where heat pumps are not needed (Miami weather) only the EV matters and smart charging removes it. The two things the result needs from outside are the constants of the transformer (paywalled in IEEE C57.91; swept here) and a real feeder's diversity.

**Diversity (2026-11-12, next step 2; [`experiments/diversity.txt`](experiments/diversity.txt)).** Replacing the fully coincident heat pumps with 20 groups of 10 thermostat-controlled two-node homes at one-minute steps, the answer to "how much is diversity worth?" is **about 3.5 times less ageing in Greensboro (0.33 to a median 0.096 %/yr at 0.5 loading, all homes with a heat pump) but only 1.4 to 1.7 times in cold Sand Point, AK**, where the pumps run flat out through the cold hours and the coincidence factor goes to 1. The thermostat schedule matters far more than diversity: a **3 K night setback ending at 06:00 for every home, with 5 kW strip heat as the second stage, raises ageing to a median 3.2 %/yr, 34 times the same homes without setback**, because every home's strip heat comes on together in the morning recovery (coincidence factor 1.00, 1-minute peak 106 kW against 89 kW). Staggering the setback end over four hours only halves that (1.9 %/yr); **locking the strip out during recovery removes it** (0.095 %/yr). Group-to-group spread is wide (0.04 to 0.45 %/yr for ten different draws of ten homes), wider than the coincident-versus-diverse gap. Diversity lives below the hour: the 10-home hourly peak per home is 7.0 kW whatever the group size, while the 1-minute peak falls from 10.9 kW (one home) to 7.8 kW (50 homes). One-minute load gives the same ageing as hourly means within a few per cent, except where load ramps hard (uncoordinated EVs), where the hourly step overstates it by about 1.4 times (coincident case: 7.5 against 5.45 %/yr, median over 20 EV-arrival draws). House constants are GridLAB-D-style defaults, not a measured stock, and each is swept (section C of the file).

![Loss of life by heat-pump control case, Greensboro and Sand Point](experiments/lol_diversity.svg)

**Builds on.** IEEE Std C57.91 (guide for loading mineral-oil-immersed transformers; clause 7 thermal model and the ageing acceleration factor `FAA = exp(15000/383 - 15000/(theta_H + 273))`, 110 C reference hot spot and 180,000 h normal insulation life, as reproduced in the open literature and confirmed for the 15000, 383 and 180,000 h figures by secondary sources). Load: the BDEW H0 household standard load profile through `demandlib` (MIT). Weather: NREL TMY3 (Greensboro 723170, Sand Point 703165) and TMY2 (Miami 12839) as bundled in `pvlib` (BSD-3). Method transfer (track 21): the thermal model is a first-order lag with a nonlinear load function, so the technique is the same as in `energy-home`'s battery loop and `grid-twin`; no CU Boulder group's work is reproduced and no connection is claimed.

## Method (`src/infra_transformer/model.py`)
- **Transformer.** Hourly. Ultimate top-oil rise `dTO_u = dTO_R ((K^2 R + 1)/(R + 1))^n`, with `K` load per unit of rating; top oil approaches it with time constant `tau_TO` (exact exponential over each hour, two years of warm-up on the cyclic year); hot-spot rise over top oil `dH_R K^(2m)` taken as instantaneous (its time constant is minutes); hot spot = ambient + top oil + gradient; ageing `FAA`; annual loss of life = sum(FAA) / 180,000 h. Not modelled: oil-viscosity correction of the time constants and losses, tap changes, harmonics, power factor below 1, cooling-mode changes.
- **Load, 10 homes.** BDEW H0 scaled to 10,800 kWh/yr per home (an assumed round figure near US averages; swept 6,000-14,000), hourly mean of the 15-minute profile. The H0 profile is a diversified German group shape, applied to the whole group.
- **Heat pump.** Electric draw = `UA (T_bal - T)+ / COP(T)` with UA 0.42 kW/K, balance 16 C, `COP = 3.2 + 0.054 (T - 8.3)` clipped to [1, 6] (a cold-climate air-source pump; the line is an approximation, swept). No thermostat setback, no defrost, no diversity: every home with a heat pump draws the same in the same hour, so the cold-snap peak is fully coincident. This is the pessimistic end of a real feeder.
- **EV.** 10 kWh per car per night (about 33 miles at 0.3 kWh/mile), 7.2 kW Level 2, arrival 18 h plus or minus 1.5 h (rounded, clipped to 16-22 h), departure 07:00. *Uncoordinated:* each car charges from arrival. *Smart:* a group valley-fill against the other load with perfect day-ahead knowledge (an upper bound on what a real scheduler achieves).
- **Sizing.** Rating = baseline annual peak / `loading`. Loading 0.5 is ~4.5 kVA per home (common practice); 0.7 and 0.9 are tight.

### Checks (`tests/`, 11)
At rated load and 30 C ambient the hot spot is 110 C, top oil 85 C and FAA exactly 1; ageing doubles about every 7 K at 110 C (the analytic 15000/383^2 slope); a year at FAA = 1 consumes 8760/180000 = 4.87 % of life; the step response reaches 1 - 1/e after one `tau_TO`; ageing rises with load and ambient; the heat-pump draw is zero above the balance temperature and obeys the COP floor; EV charging conserves energy and smart charging never raises the peak; scenarios add load. Away from rated load: at 1.5 per unit and 30 C the hot spot is 171.76 C (top oil 30 + 55 (8.2/4.2)^0.8, gradient 25 x 1.5^1.6), growth scales EV load too, and uncoordinated charging starts at arrival.

### Diversity model (`src/infra_transformer/diversity.py`, `experiments/run_diversity.py`)
- **Homes.** Each home is a two-node equivalent thermal parameter model (air and mass; the equivalent-thermal-parameter form of Sonderegger's 1978 Princeton thesis, "Dynamic models of house heating based on equivalent thermal parameters", as used in GridLAB-D's residential module, Chassin, Schneider and Gerkensmeyer 2008, [doi:10.1109/TDC.2008.4517260](https://doi.org/10.1109/TDC.2008.4517260)): UA lognormal with mean 0.42 kW/K (the base model's figure) and coefficient of variation 0.25; air capacity 1.35 h &times; UA (0.57 kWh/K), mass capacity 30 h &times; UA (12.6 kWh/K), air-mass conductance 9 &times; UA (3.8 kW/K), recalled from GridLAB-D's defaults for a 2,500 ft&sup2; house (air volume times three for furniture, 2 Btu/F&middot;ft&sup2; of mass, 1.46 Btu/h&middot;ft&sup2;&middot;F interior film), not verified; internal gains set so the nominal home balances at 16 C as in the base model. Setpoint 20 &plusmn; 1 C, thermostat deadband 1 K, random initial state; no solar gain, no defrost, no occupancy.
- **Heat pumps.** Capacity = the home's design heat load at the climate's 1st-percentile hourly temperature (Greensboro &minus;9.4 C, Sand Point &minus;8.0 C), same COP line as the base model. *Single-stage:* thermostat hysteresis, on or off at full capacity (the population model of Ihara and Schweppe 1981, [doi:10.1109/TPAS.1981.316965](https://doi.org/10.1109/TPAS.1981.316965), and Mortensen and Haggerty 1988, [doi:10.1109/59.14584](https://doi.org/10.1109/59.14584)). *Variable-speed:* feed-forward plus proportional output, clipped to capacity. *Strip heat:* 5 kW at COP 1 when the air is 1 K below the band.
- **Setback.** 3 K from 22:00 to 06:00, the same end hour for every home or uniform per home in 04:00-08:00.
- **Transformer.** `simulate_dt` runs the same C57.91-form model at one-minute steps, with the hot-spot gradient lagged by a winding time constant of 0.1 h (swept 0 to 0.15 h: no effect beyond 5 %). Base load and EVs are the hourly series of the base model, held flat within each hour; the transformer is sized to the baseline peak exactly as in section A.
- **Checks (`tests/test_diversity.py`, 9).** The one-minute transformer at a one-hour step reproduces `simulate`; constant rated load at 30 C gives 110 C and 4.87 %/yr at any step; the winding lag reaches 1 &minus; 1/e after one time constant; interpolated temperatures keep the hourly mean; at constant outdoor temperature the variable-speed population draws the base model's steady heat-pump power within 1 % and a single-stage home's duty cycle delivers it within 2 % while drawing only zero or full power; nothing runs above the balance temperature; a setback produces a morning recovery peak.

## Results (`experiments/results.txt`; deterministic)
- **A. Annual loss of life by loading and scenario (Greensboro, %/yr).** Loading 0.5: base 0.0019; EV 100 % uncoordinated 0.035, smart 0.0026; HP 100 % 0.33; HP+EV 100 % uncoordinated 5.6, smart 1.07. Loading 0.7: base 0.0054, EV uncoordinated 2.3, smart 0.0093, and all heat-pump cases above 100 %/yr (insulation gone within the year; the model is outside its range there, shown as failed). Sand Point (cold): HP+EV 100 % at 0.5 loading is 8.9 uncoordinated, 0.19 smart. Miami (no heating): HP adds nothing; EV 100 % at 0.5 is 0.075 uncoordinated, 0.0058 smart.
- **B. Peaks at loading 0.7, Greensboro.** Base peak 0.70 per unit and 80 C hot spot; EV 100 % uncoordinated 2.15 per unit and 173 C (48 hours above 140 C, the usual emergency limit); the same with smart charging 0.70 per unit and 81 C. HP 100 % peaks at 2.42 per unit and 268 C; with the EV, 3.33 per unit (uncoordinated) or 2.46 (smart). Hot spots above about 200 C are not credible; they flag overload, not a temperature.
- **C. Electrified share at which loss of life first reaches the rated-life rate.** Loading 0.5: Greensboro 100 % uncoordinated, not reached with smart charging; Sand Point 95 % uncoordinated, not reached smart; Miami not reached. Loading 0.7: Greensboro 65 % uncoordinated, 75 % smart; Sand Point 60 % and 85 %; Miami not reached. At the typical sizing, no share of EVs alone or heat pumps alone reaches it.
- **D. Uniform growth.** Growth that doubles loss of life: 1.28x (loading 0.5), 1.16x (0.7), 1.11x (0.9); growth that reaches the rated-life rate: 3.12x, 2.23x, 1.73x. A transformer's ageing is exponential in the hot spot, so doubling is cheap and not a sign of trouble when the baseline is near zero.
- **E. Sensitivity** (50 % of homes with both; rating sized to loading 0.5 on each row's own baseline for the plant-side rows, on the default baseline for the transformer-constant rows). Smart charging is lower than uncoordinated in all 30 rows, by a factor of 1.7 to 25 (the 25 is a 6,000 kWh/yr baseline, whose rating is re-sized to its own small baseline peak and which the heat pumps then overload). Across the transformer constants the factor is 1.8 to 6.1 and no row exceeds 0.025 %/yr (uncoordinated), so the 50 % result is insensitive to the unverified constants. Plant-side inputs matter more: COP 2.5 instead of 3.2 raises the uncoordinated figure to 0.051 %/yr, UA 0.6 to 0.041, EV energy 15 kWh/night to 0.024.
- **F.** The curve in the figure (share 0 to 100 %).
- **G. Diversity** (`experiments/diversity.txt`; medians and ranges over 20 ten-home groups, 100 % heat pumps, loading 0.5). Greensboro, no EV: coincident 0.33 %/yr; variable-speed 0.11 [0.04-0.55]; single-stage 0.096 [0.04-0.45]; single-stage with synchronized 3 K setback and strip 3.2 [1.1-21]; the same staggered over 4 h 1.9; strip locked out 0.095; variable-speed with synchronized setback 1.5. With smart EV charging: coincident 1.09, single-stage 0.28, synchronized setback 4.9, strip locked out 0.14. With uncoordinated EVs the EV dominates (coincident 5.45, single-stage 4.4, synchronized setback 10). Sand Point, no EV: coincident 0.080, diverse 0.048-0.057, synchronized setback 0.39, strip locked out 0.11. At loading 0.7 every Greensboro heat-pump case still fails or comes close (single-stage median 76 %/yr), so diversity does not rescue a tight transformer. Sensitivity (single-stage, Greensboro): the result moves most with heat-pump sizing (0.8 of design load: 4.2 %/yr, as strip heat runs in every cold spell; 1.25: 0.26, though its 1-minute peak is lower; the mechanism is not isolated here), deadband (0.5 K: 0.15; 2 K: 0.085) and mass (15 h: 0.25); with setback, the strip size decides (0 kW: 0.095; 10 kW: 9.7). Within-hour peaking (1-minute maximum over hourly mean of the 10-home group) is 1.06 below &minus;5 C and 3.0 between 10 and 16 C: diversity is large when it does not matter and small when it does.

## Limitations
- **The constants of the transformer are not sourced.** IEEE C57.91 is paywalled and was not reachable from this environment. The ageing constants (15000, 383 K, 180,000 h) were confirmed by secondary sources; the rise values (55 K top oil, 25 K gradient, i.e. 80 K hot spot), R = 3.2, n = m = 0.8 and tau_TO = 3 h are my recollection of the standard's ONAN distribution-class values and are *not* verified. Section E sweeps each over the range literature quotes; the 50 % result survives, the 100 % result is dominated by the overload so its exact figure does not. Check the standard before using any figure.
- **Models are not plants.** One group profile, not metered customers; a fully coincident heat-pump peak with no setback or diversity; TMY weather (a typical year, not a cold snap or heat wave, which matter most for ageing); a German profile shape on US weather. Real feeders have fewer coincident peaks and a transformer rarely sees 10 identical homes, so the 100 % rows overstate and the transformer's tolerance of occasional overload (hot-spot limits, gas bubbles) is not modelled.
- **Prior work is not reviewed, and the finding is not new** (quality audit of 2026-11-12, [`portfolio/quality/audits/2026-11-12-infra-transformer.json`](../../portfolio/quality/audits/2026-11-12-infra-transformer.json)). Hilshey et al. (2013, *IEEE Trans. Smart Grid*) on EV charging and distribution transformer ageing, Gong et al. (2012) on PHEV transformer ageing, and Navarro-Espinosa and Ochoa (2016) on low-carbon technologies on LV networks reach the same qualitative result: uncoordinated EV charging ages transformers and coordination recovers most of it. This study is a reproducible open restatement with heat pumps added, not a new finding.
- **Only the 110 C insulation is modelled.** Older 55 C-rise units use a 95 C reference hot spot and age about 4.9 times faster at the same temperature. The guide's maximum hot-spot and top-oil temperatures for each loading class are not checked, so the high-share rows can exceed them.
- **No ageing beyond insulation temperature.** Moisture, oxygen, load-tap and fault stresses, and winding paper degree of polymerisation are absent.
- **The diversity population is a model, not a feeder.** House constants are recalled GridLAB-D defaults and swept, not a measured building stock; every home has the same thermostat logic; no solar gain, occupancy or defrost; variable-speed control is idealised. The setback result depends on the stage-2 rule (strip 1 K below the band); many thermostats have adaptive recovery that avoids strip heat, which is exactly the strip-locked-out case. A metered heat-pump feeder (for example a utility AMI sample) is the check this needs.
- **Smart charging is an upper bound** (perfect day-ahead knowledge, a single group, no driver override).
- **Safety-critical designs are research.** Transformer loading decisions need the utility's engineers; nothing here is operating advice, and no real utility system was touched.

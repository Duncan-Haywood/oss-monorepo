# A safety margin certified in a Gaussian twin fails under heavy-tailed gusts with the same variance, and the fix is a tail fit, not a bigger variance

*Stylised: 1-D lateral deviation of a stabilised vehicle, `x_{t+1}=a x_t+w_t`, `a=0.8`, disturbance sd `s=0.1` m, episodes of `T=200` steps, corridor half-width `L`. The twin draws `w` Gaussian; the "real" system draws it from a Student-t with `ν` degrees of freedom rescaled to the same variance. Pure Python; every number is from `experiments/results.txt` (seeded, ~90 s). Failure probabilities are read from simulated episodes (100,000 for the Gaussian twin and `ν=3`, 20,000 for other `ν`), with standard errors printed there.*

## Question
Safe-autonomy work for UAVs and field robots often certifies a controller's safety margin in simulation, because failures are rare and real flights are expensive. A simulator's disturbance model is usually fitted to the variance of real disturbances. Gust and terrain disturbances are heavier-tailed than Gaussian, and a variance fit cannot see that. How wrong is a certificate `P(leave the corridor within T steps) ≤ δ` computed in the Gaussian twin, how large a margin does the real system need, and what can be fitted from real data to repair it?

## Model
Twin certificate: the stationary deviation is `N(0,σ_x²)` with `σ_x²=s²/(1−a²)`, and the union bound gives `T·P(|x|>L)≤δ`, i.e. `L_twin=σ_x Φ⁻¹(1−δ/(2T))`. The bound holds for the twin by construction (simulated twin failure at `δ=0.05/0.01/0.001` is 0.038/0.0084/0.0009). For the real system, `x=Σ a^k w_k` with `w` regularly varying; the single-big-jump approximation (Embrechts, Klüppelberg & Mikosch 1997) gives `P(|x|>L)≈Σ_k P(|w|>L/a^k)`, which the code evaluates with the exact Student-t tail (closed-form CDF for integer `ν`). At `ν=3` it is within the simulation noise of the stationary tail (ratio 1.34/1.11/0.99 at `L`=0.6/1.0/1.5 m, from 400,000 draws; the approximation is asymptotic, so it is loose at small `L`). Margins from it use the same union bound over `T`.

## Results
1. **The twin certificate fails by an order of magnitude or more.** At the twin's `δ=0.01` margin (0.676 m, four stationary sd) the real failure probability is 0.29/0.19/0.13/0.052 for `ν`=3/4/5/8; at `δ=0.001` (0.761 m) it is 0.208/0.117/0.065/0.017, against a claimed 0.001. The one-step variance is identical in all cases.
2. **The margin the real system needs grows with the tail, not the variance.** At `δ=0.001` it is 5.58/2.67/1.73/0.95 m for `ν`=3/4/5/8, i.e. 7.3×/3.5×/2.3×/1.25× the twin's; at `δ=10⁻⁴`, 14.4×/5.7×/3.3×/1.5×. The ratio *grows* as `δ` shrinks: rarer targets are exactly where the twin is worst. For `ν=3` the big-jump margin is conservative but valid: real failure 0.026/0.0048/0.00059 at `δ`=0.05/0.01/0.001.
3. **Inflating the twin variance cannot fix it.** A Gaussian twin scaled (×2.03) so its 95th-percentile episode maximum matches the real one is right at `δ=0.05` by construction, but at `δ=0.01` its margin (1.36 m) has real failure 0.035 and at `δ=0.001` (1.54 m vs the 4.62 m the real tail needs, empirical) real failure 0.024, a 24× miss. Calibrating at one probability level does not transfer to another when the shape is wrong (cf. the same finding for variance ratios at other lags in `odometry-twin`).
4. **Model-free repair: conformal margin from real episodes.** The `⌈(n+1)(1−δ)⌉`-th order statistic of `n` real episode maxima has real failure ≤ δ in expectation (mean 0.0107/0.0102/0.0099 at `δ=0.01`, `n`=100/200/1000; 90th percentile of the realised failure 0.026/0.020/0.014). It gives *no* bound when `n<1/δ−1`: with 200 real episodes, `δ=0.001` is out of reach, and 1000 are needed (mean 0.0010, 90th percentile 0.0022). This is the cost of validating a rare-failure claim on real data alone.
5. **Tail repair from one-step data.** Unlike the odometry bias in `odometry-twin`, a heavy tail *is* identifiable from one-step disturbances, which flights supply cheaply. A Hill estimator on the top 5% of `|w|` plus a Pareto tail in the big-jump margin (target `δ=0.001`) gives real failure (median [10th, 90th percentile over 200 repeats]) 0.00026 [0, 0.0020] at `n`=500 one-step samples, 0.00017 [0.00003, 0.00076] at 2,000 and 0.00022 [0.00009, 0.00032] at 10,000; the share of repeats above `δ` is 0.22/0.07/0/0 at `n`=500/2,000/10,000/50,000. The estimate is biased low, `α̂≈2.65` for true 3 (top 5% is not far enough into the tail of a t-distribution), which errs on the safe side here; the bias is not corrected and would be different for other tail shapes.

## Limitations
1-D linear system with a fixed stabilising gain; disturbance is i.i.d. t (real gusts are also temporally correlated, and the disturbance is not observed independent of the state); the union bound over `T` ignores clustering, so margins are conservative (real failure at the big-jump margin is 0.5–0.6× the target); the repair experiments treat the reference sample of 100,000 simulated real episodes as the population and resample from it; the "real" system is a simulation, not flight data. Thresholds, `a`, `s`, `T` and `ν` are chosen, not measured. Preliminary; not evaluated on hardware.

## Next steps
Correlated gusts (the persistent component of `odometry-twin` combined with a tail); state-dependent safety filters and control-barrier functions; hybrid certification with importance sampling in the twin (tilting toward big jumps) and few real episodes as a correction; scoring the twin's tail claim with proper scoring rules for tolerance/tail quantities (`tail-risk-elicitation`, `twin-elicitation`); domain randomisation over `ν` (`randomized-twin`).

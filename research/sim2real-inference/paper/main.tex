\title{What is a biased simulator worth for evaluating a robot policy?\\ Variance reduction yes, certificates no}

\begin{abstract}
A digital twin gives unlimited but biased runs; real trials are unbiased but scarce. We ask how many real trials a twin saves when evaluating a policy, and whether any saving survives a guarantee that holds whatever the twin's bias. On real data (SIMPLER's published paired tables, 36 policy--task cells) a leave-one-cell-out calibrated twin has RMSE 0.118 and is worth about 10 real trials (interval 5 to 26 resampling cells; 3 to 54 resampling tasks and policies); for a policy and task the calibration has not seen, the raw twin is the better predictor, worth about 5 to 7 trials. On 21 MuJoCo twin--real model pairs (paired correlation $\rho$ from 0.17 to 0.88) a control-variate estimator that pairs twin and real runs on the same context multiplies the effective real sample by up to 3.2, within 6\% of $1/(1-\rho^2)$ at $n=30$ real and $N=300$ twin runs, while unpaired twin runs give none. Plug-in 95\% intervals undercover on the bounded, skewed outcome (down to 86.7\%). Most distribution-free certificates we built (empirical Bernstein, a capped-bet betting interval, a pilot-checked discrepancy bound, a failure-rate bound, anytime confidence sequences) were valid but never tighter, and up to 3.4 times wider, than their real-only baselines at $n\le 240$; an uncapped hedged-capital betting interval changes this on the pendulum pairs (narrower than real-only betting on 20 of 21 pairs at $n=240$, median ratio 0.78) when the twin mean is treated as exact; made valid with a finite twin pool (union bound) it is narrower on 20 of 21 pendulum pairs at $n=960$ with $20{,}000$ twin runs, 7--8 of 21 with $4{,}000$, and none with $500$; on cart-pole 7 of 168 cells with $20{,}000$ or $100{,}000$. We prove why this is not only a flaw of our constructions: any valid 95\% lower bound has worst-case gap at least $1-(2\delta)^{1/m}\approx 2.3/m$ even for a perfect twin. The twin's value is variance reduction for point estimates; whether it also shortens a valid certificate is open.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Real-robot evaluation is the bottleneck of robot learning; generalist policies are often compared on a few dozen real trials with no interval \cite{kressgazit2024empirical}. Simulated evaluation scales but is biased \cite{li2024simpler}. Prediction-powered inference \cite{angelopoulos2023ppi,angelopoulos2023ppipp} gives valid intervals from a small labelled set plus many model predictions, and the classical control variate is its tuned form \cite{peherstorfer2018survey}. A simulator differs from a generic predictor in one way that matters here: it can replay the same scene (context) as the real trial, so twin and real outcomes are paired. We study what that pairing buys and where it stops.
\paragraph{Contributions.} (1) A real-data check on SIMPLER's published tables: the twin is worth about ten real trials for a seen policy and task, and five to seven for a new one. (2) An estimator library and a 21-pair MuJoCo benchmark showing the pairing gain follows $1/(1-\rho^2)$ and unpaired runs give none. (3) A negative result across five certificate constructions, and the condition that reverses it: a betting interval with a finite twin pool of about $20{,}000$ runs beats real-only betting on 20 of 21 pendulum pairs. (4) A distribution-free floor, with proof, on any twin-assisted lower bound, tight within about $1.3\times$ at $\delta=0.05$. (5) Two uses of the twin beyond point estimates, active selection of real contexts (gain only where twin error is predictable) and anytime sequences (no gain). Robustness checks on a second task, an unbounded outcome and SureSim's setting are in the appendix. All claims are pre-registered in the repository's \texttt{LOG.md}, with refuted hypotheses reported.

\section{Setting and estimators}
A context $c$ (initial state, payload, reference) yields a real outcome $R(c)\in[0,1]$ and a twin outcome $T(c)\in[0,1]$. We observe $n$ pairs $(R_i,T_i)$ and $N-n$ extra twin runs, and estimate $\mu=\E R$. The family $\hat\mu_\lambda=\bar R-\lambda(\bar T_{\mathrm{paired}}-\bar T_{\mathrm{all}})$ contains real-only ($\lambda=0$), PPI ($\lambda=1$) and the control variate with $\lambda$ fitted by least squares. It is unbiased for any twin bias. With paired correlation $\rho$ the variance ratio is $E\approx 1/(1-\rho^2(1-n/N))$.

\section{Real data: SIMPLER}
We transcribed the published Visual Matching real and SAPIEN success rates \cite{li2024simpler} (36 cells; the eggplant column implies $n=30$, not the 24 in Appendix B, which a transcription test enforces). Raw simulated error has debiased MSE 0.0212 [0.0134, 0.0297], five times the real-only variance at the paper's $n$ (0.0042). A leave-one-cell-out calibrated twin has RMSE 0.118 [0.074, 0.158]; break-even is $n^*=10.1$ real trials [5.1, 26.5]. Pearson $r=0.90$ [0.84, 0.95] between simulated and real rates, so rank agreement is excellent while the level error equals one real-trial standard error at $n\approx15$. Checked against the authors' own tables (\texttt{simpler\_env/utils/metrics.py}), 70 of 72 transcribed values agree; the two that differ are the real eggplant rates of the Octo models (0.233, 0.433 against 0.250, 0.400); re-reading Table V of the PDF shows our values are the paper's and the repository disagrees with its own paper there. Re-running with the authors' values, with either $n$, and with the sixth Google task (42 cells) gives $n^*$ 10.2 to 11.3 and $r$ 0.90 to 0.91. Only policy-level rates are published, so the paired control variate is untested on real data. Intervals above are bootstrap over cells; resampling whole tasks and policies together (4{,}000 resamples) widens $n^*$ to [2.7, 53.9] and $r$ to [0.74, 0.97], so the honest summary is about ten real trials with an order-of-magnitude interval. That calibration is leave-one-cell-out, so it has seen the held-out cell's task and policy elsewhere; holding out a whole task leaves it unchanged ($n^*=10.3$), holding out the policy lowers it to 7.1 (calibrated MSE 0.0195 against 0.0212 raw), and holding out both makes the calibrated twin worse than the raw one (MSE 0.0290, $n^*=4.8$ for the calibrated, intervals overlap throughout). For a new policy on a new task, the raw twin is the better predictor and is worth about five to seven real trials. Judged by order instead of level, the twin ranks 71 within-task policy pairs correctly 88.7\% of the time [81.7, 96.6]; a real evaluation matches that with 11 trials per policy [5, 38] (task-cluster bootstrap, published rates taken as truth), so the two prices agree.

\section{Simulated benchmark}
A torque-driven MuJoCo pendulum arm tracks a sinusoid (a second, unstable task is in the replication paragraph below). The twin is the nominal model; the ``real'' model adds an Ornstein--Uhlenbeck disturbance and one omitted effect (friction, delay, sensor noise, actuator gain, payload mass) at four swept magnitudes, plus a no-effect pair: 21 pairs, 4{,}000 contexts each. These are model-versus-model pairs with swept, not sourced, magnitudes. The disturbance and magnitudes were raised after a probe gave $\rho=0.999$ everywhere, looking only at $\rho$ and not at any estimator.

\paragraph{Pairing gain (H1, H2).} Figure~\ref{fig:e} shows measured $E$ at $n=30$, $N=300$ within 6\% of the closed form on every pair. The paired-over-unpaired ratio has a lower 95\% bound of at least 2 on 13 of 21 pairs and fails where $\rho<0.75$; unpaired twin runs cost 2--4\% (estimating $\lambda$ on uncorrelated data). $E$ ranks with $\rho$ (Spearman 0.999) but also with the Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance between marginals ($-0.954$), so detectability is not an independent predictor on this benchmark.

\paragraph{Intervals.} Plug-in $t$ intervals fall below 93\% on 15 (control variate) and 20 (PPI++) of 21 pairs at $n=30$ (worst 88.8\%, 86.7\%); real-only reaches 91.2\% at worst because outcomes are bounded and left-skewed (skewness $-2.4$). A studentised bootstrap lifts mean control-variate coverage from 92.0\% to 93.6\% at 14\% greater width; the percentile bootstrap is worse (90.7\%).

\paragraph{Certificates (Table~\ref{tab:cert}).} The twin's variance gain is real for point estimates but none of the five constructions turns it into a tighter guarantee. The binding terms are the range ($1+\lambda$ after rescaling, or $2b$ with $b$ the pilot maximum of $|R-T|$, 0.26--0.36 even at $\rho\approx0.87$) and the price of checking an assumption on a pilot ($\gamma=1-0.01^{1/k}$, 0.056 at $n=240$).

\paragraph{A larger twin pool changes the picture (m7h--m7j).} Table~\ref{tab:cert} stops at capped bets. With the cap raised to $0.99/m$ (positivity of the hedged capital allows it) and the twin mean bounded from $N$ iid twin runs by a second betting interval (union bound, so the combined interval is valid at $0.05$), the twin-assisted interval is narrower than real-only betting on 20 of 21 pendulum pairs at $n=960$ with $N=20{,}000$ (median width ratio 0.84--0.86, best 0.66), on 7--8 of 21 with $N=4{,}000$, and on none with $N=500$; coverage is at least 98\%. On cart-pole it wins in 7 of 168 cells (appendix). So a twin-assisted certificate can be shorter when twin runs are cheap enough to draw many, which the floor below does not forbid: it limits the $1/m$ term in the real runs, not the twin-mean term. The $N=20{,}000$ draws are iid from a $4{,}000$-context pool, so this speaks to interval width only.

\paragraph{A floor.} \textbf{Theorem.} Let $L$ be any lower confidence bound for $\mu$ with $P(L\le\mu)\ge1-\delta$ for every joint law of $(R,T)$ on $[0,1]^2$, using $m$ paired runs and any number of twin-only runs. With $\epsilon^*=1-(2\delta)^{1/m}$ there is a law with a perfect twin ($R=T=1$) under which $P(L\le 1-\epsilon^*)\ge 1/2$. \emph{Proof.} Under $P_1$, $T=1$ and $R=0$ with probability $\epsilon$ independently ($\mu=1-\epsilon$); under $P_0$, $R=T=1$. Twin-only runs have the same law under both; the paired data coincide with probability $(1-\epsilon)^m$. Validity under $P_1$ gives $\delta\ge P_1(L>1-\epsilon)\ge(1-\epsilon)^mP_0(L>1-\epsilon)$; at $\epsilon^*$, $(1-\epsilon^*)^m=2\delta$, so $P_0(L>1-\epsilon^*)\le1/2$. \hfill$\square$

So a twin cannot reduce the $\approx\ln(1/2\delta)/m$ term, even a perfect one. The floor is 0.0095 at $m=240$, against 0.060 for real-only empirical Bernstein and 0.193 for our bounded-discrepancy bound; on the all-ones sample the exact valid bound is $\delta^{1/m}$, so the floor is tight to about $1.3\times$. There is room for a better construction, but not for one that beats the $\sqrt{\mathrm{var}/m}$ term by more than the twin's variance share. An instance-wise bound involving $\rho$ is open.

\section{Related work}
Prediction-powered inference \cite{angelopoulos2023ppi,angelopoulos2023ppipp}, active inference \cite{zrnic2024active}, design-based surrogates \cite{egami2023surrogates} and multifidelity Monte Carlo \cite{peherstorfer2018survey} supply the estimators; we add context pairing for simulators and measure it. Confidence sequences and betting intervals \cite{howard2021confseq,waudbysmith2020betting} supply the anytime constructions. SureSim \cite{badithela2025suresim} applies prediction-powered inference with non-asymptotic intervals to robot policy evaluation with paired real and simulated runs, the closest prior work; m7g tests its regime of $n$ with our pairs. SIMPLER \cite{li2024simpler} uses simulation as a proxy; \cite{kressgazit2024empirical} sets reporting standards. Rare-event simulation and validation \cite{okelly2018rare,corso2021survey} estimate simulator-side quantities. Sim-to-real work \cite{tobin2017dr,zhao2020survey} closes the gap for training, not for evaluation.

\section{Limitations}
Model-versus-model pairs on two simulated tasks and two outcome forms (a bounded score and an unbounded cost, the latter certified only through a clipped mean); no real-robot episode pairs (the per-episode SIMPLER logs were not obtained); pools are finite populations sampled with and without replacement; one betting construction and one $\delta$ split; certificate comparisons stop at $n=1{,}920$ (and at $n=240$ for most constructions); the floor is a worst-case statement and says nothing about instance-wise bounds. Two pre-registered clauses were mis-specified and are corrected in the log (the $\rho\ge0.98$ tier in 3f was empty; the count of pairs with $\rho\ge0.8$ in 5b was 12, not 7).

\section{Conclusion}
A context-paired twin is a variance-reduction device worth up to $1/(1-\rho^2)$ real trials when $\rho$ is high, and about ten real trials at SIMPLER's level of fidelity. It does not shorten a distribution-free certificate at the sample sizes tested, and a worst-case floor shows part of this is unavoidable. Open: an instance-wise bound under a checkable discrepancy assumption; per-episode real pairs; certificates for unbounded outcomes (only a clipped-mean lower bound with a twin-chosen range was tested, m7d, and the twin did not shorten it, m7e; the twin mean was taken as known).

\section{Robustness and extensions}
These paragraphs support the claims above; they are pre-registered steps of the repository log (\texttt{LOG.md}).
\paragraph{A second task (m7a).} To test whether the above is a property of paired inference or of the pendulum, we repeat the benchmark on a MuJoCo cart-pole balanced from a random start by a linear feedback law (unstable plant; a fall ends the episode; outcome is survived fraction times $1-\mathrm{RMS}$ angle$/0.25$), with the same five omitted effects at four swept magnitudes, 21 pairs ($\rho$ 0.37 to 0.86) and 4{,}000 contexts each. The push-noise level was set from a 200-context probe that looked only at $\rho$. The pairing gain replicates: all 16 pairs with $\rho\ge0.75$ have a paired-over-unpaired variance-ratio lower bound of at least 2, and unpaired $E$ is 0.958 to 0.983 everywhere. The predictor result replicates with a clearer margin: Spearman$(E,\rho)=0.909$ against $-0.708$ for the KS distance, difference of absolute values in $[0.021,0.494]$ (pendulum: $[0.008,0.146]$); the rank correlation is below the 0.95 we pre-registered. The interval shortfall does \emph{not} replicate: the control variate or PPI++ falls below 93\% at $n=30$ on 3 of 21 pairs (worst 91.98\%), against 15 and 20 of 21 on the pendulum. The undercoverage is therefore partly task dependent, tied to that outcome's skew, and concentrated on low-$\rho$ pairs where $\lambda$ is noisiest.

\paragraph{An unbounded outcome (m7c).} Re-running the same 21 cart-pole pairs with the tracking cost $\int(100\theta^2+(x-x_{\mathrm{ref}})^2)\,dt$ (no early stop, no upper bound; skewness 1.89) leaves the pairing gain intact (all 17 pairs with $\rho\ge0.75$ have a variance-ratio lower bound of at least 2; unpaired $E$ 0.948 to 0.971). The control variate covers at least 93.3\% at $n=30$ on every pair, while PPI++ falls below 93\% on 18 of 21 (worst 90.2\%), so the interval shortfall is the fixed-weight estimator's, not pairing's. The predictor claim does not survive: Spearman$(E,\rho)=0.884$ against $-0.809$ for KS, difference of absolute values in $[-0.070,0.299]$, which includes 0. We therefore state $\rho$ as a predictor only for bounded outcomes. A certificate for this outcome is available only for the clipped mean $E[\min(R,c)]\le E[R]$ with $c$ the twin's 99.9th percentile: the real-only empirical-Bernstein gap is 27\% of the mean at $n=240$ (median over pairs), but on the three large-friction pairs the twin quantile is below the real outcome and the clipped mean is 14\% to 84\% short.

\paragraph{A twin-chosen range for the unbounded cost (m7d, m7e).} At $q=0.999$ the twin's quantile gives $c=2.009$ for all 21 pools, and clipping loses under 5\% of $E[R]$ on 18 of 21 pairs (median 0.23\%); at $q=0.99$ more than 3\% of real outcomes exceed $c$ on 7 of 21 pairs, so a twin quantile transfers only when the omitted effect is small. The median relative gap of the real-only lower bound on $E[\min(R,c)]$ is 1.51, 0.83, 0.47 and 0.27 at $n=30,60,120,240$. Adding the twin as a control variate on the clipped cost, with the twin mean treated as exactly known (the most favourable case), gives a smaller mean gap than real-only on 0 of 21 pairs at every $n$ (median 0.333 against 0.271 at $n=240$), even where the clipped $\rho$ is near 0.94: doubling the residual range costs more than the smaller residual variance saves. Coverage is 100\% on every cell. The certification result therefore holds on a bounded score, a binary failure indicator and a clipped unbounded cost. An upper bound on the cost would need a tail assumption; the checkable form ``real excess over $c$ is at most $k$ times the twin's'' needs $k^*\le 3$ on none of the 21 pairs at $q=0.999$ (median $k^*=7.8$) and $k^*>10$ on eight, in the thousands for the three large-friction pairs (m7f), so it is no basis for an upper certificate.

\paragraph{An uncapped betting interval (m7h).} The earlier betting interval (m3d) capped its bet at $1/2$ over the candidate mean, and the cap bound on 99--100\% of bets. Positivity of the hedged capital allows a cap of $0.99/m$, so we repeat the comparison with that cap (PrPl-EB bets, $\theta=1/2$, 300 replications, $n\in\{60,240,960\}$, both pools). Real-only betting is narrower than real-only empirical Bernstein on every pair at $n=60$ and $240$ (median width ratio 0.28 and 0.45 on the pendulum). With the twin mean treated as exact, the twin-assisted betting interval is narrower than real-only betting on 20 of 21 pendulum pairs at $n=240$ (median ratio 0.78, minimum 0.59) and at $n=960$ (median 0.57), but on only 4 of 21 at $n=60$, and on 1 and 4 of 21 cart-pole pairs at $n=240$ and $960$. Coverage is at least 98.7\%. The negative certification results above therefore hold for empirical-Bernstein and capped-bet intervals, not for certification in general. The exact twin mean is a favourable case: a valid bound must also bound the twin mean from a finite pool, which we have not yet done.

\paragraph{A finite twin pool (m7i, m7j).} The exact twin mean is a favourable case. We make the interval valid by a union bound: a betting interval for the rescaled residual and one for the twin mean from $N$ iid twin draws, with the error budget split between them (200--300 replications, pendulum pairs; cart-pole wins on none). With $N=500$ the combined interval is narrower than real-only betting on 0 of 21 pairs at $n=960$; with $N=4{,}000$ on 7--8 of 21 (best width ratio 0.80, median about 1.06); with $N=20{,}000$ on 20 of 21 (median ratio 0.84--0.86, best 0.66), for either an even split or giving the twin mean a tenth of the budget. Coverage is at least 98\%. The $N=20{,}000$ draws are iid from the 4{,}000-context pool, so this speaks to interval width only. A twin-assisted certificate is thus shorter than a real-only one when the twin pool is large, which is cheap, and not when it is small. \paragraph{What decides the win (m7k--m7o).} On cart-pole, pools of $20{,}000$ and $100{,}000$ twin draws win in 7 of 168 pair--$n$--$N$--$f$ cells, against most pendulum cells. Neither $\rho$ nor the residual-to-outcome sd ratio predicts the win (Spearman 0.09 and 0.17 at $n=960$, 42 pairs), and a threshold on the real-only width over the twin-mean width fitted on the pendulum scores 4.2\% on cart-pole (m7m). Measuring the residual interval directly (m7n), the combined width is the sum of the residual and twin-mean intervals to within 0.4\% (median, 336 cells), so the win is decided by whether the residual interval is shorter than the real-only one. A variance-only model of that ratio is off by 33\% (median), by 14\% on the pendulum and by about 48\% on cart-pole, where the residual interval alone is wider than the real-only one (median ratio 1.33). Post hoc, the model's miss tracks the mean of the real outcome (Spearman 0.81): the betting interval for an outcome near its ceiling is narrower than its sd suggests, and the rescaled residual gets no such help. We tested the ceiling explanation by moving the outcome (m7o, 84 cells, 100 replications): flipping $R\to1-R$ leaves the ratio of residual to real-only width unchanged (the interval is symmetric, so this checks the code), and a monotone power transform that moves the mean of $R$ to $1/2$ takes the cart-pole median ratio from 1.32 to 1.03 (share of cells above 1: 0.93 to 0.52) and leaves the pendulum at 0.64. About 90\% of the cart-pole excess over 1 disappears, but the twin is still not a clear win there, and the Spearman correlation with the mean of $R$ only falls from 0.62 to 0.51. The ceiling effect is therefore a supported but incomplete mechanism from two systems, model-versus-model pairs and one transform; the power scale changes the estimand, so this describes the interval, not whether a twin would pay on the original scale.

\paragraph{Larger $n$ (m7g).} The certificate comparisons stop at $n=240$, and the closest related work, SureSim \cite{badithela2025suresim}, reports an interval 14.4\% narrower than real-only at $N=700$. We extend a fixed-weight PPI empirical-Bernstein interval ($\lambda=1$, no pilot split, residual range 2, twin mean from $4{,}000$ twin runs) to $n=240$--$1{,}920$ on both task pools. It is narrower than real-only on 0 of 21 pairs in each pool at every $n$ (median width ratio 1.80--1.89 pendulum, 2.28--2.57 cart-pole). With the twin mean treated as exact the ratio falls to 1.04--1.13 on the pendulum at $n=1{,}920$ and a plug-in crossover from population standard deviations lies at $n^*=2{,}567$ (best pair; median 4{,}112) on the pendulum and $\ge 53{,}096$ on cart-pole. Coverage is 100\% throughout. We have not reproduced SureSim's gain and do not know why, so the claim is that this construction on these pairs needs thousands of real trials to win. We then read the full SureSim text and matched its stated setting (m7q): $\alpha=0.1$, $n=60$ paired, $N=700$ simulation-only, objects scored as averages over 5 contexts, both its uniform-PPI summand and a two-stage split, with betting intervals. On 84 pair-by-$k$ cells the two-stage interval is narrower than real-only in 1 (pendulum gain-3, $k=1$, ratio 0.93, $\rho=0.52$) and the median ratio is 1.47 to 1.86 (pendulum) and 1.72 (cart-pole); uniform PPI has median ratio 1.34 to 2.01, narrower on none; coverage at least 96\%. Averaging iid contexts did not raise $\rho$, so it is not SureSim's smoothing (matched seeds, 100 simulated perturbations per hardware object), and the difference remains unexplained. The first author's public repository for the paper (commit \texttt{463bb2f}, 2026-02-04) is an unmodified project template with no data or estimator code, so that repository gave nothing to replicate. The project page links a second repository (\texttt{irom-princeton/rapid-policy-evaluation}, MIT, commit \texttt{e9e6cb5}) that holds their interval code but not the paired hardware scores. Running their \texttt{ppi\_uniform} and \texttt{gold\_only\_est} unchanged on our pairs in the same setting (m7u), the uniform-PPI interval is wider than their real-only interval on all 84 pair-by-$k$ cells (median ratio 2.00--2.19) with coverage at least 95.5\%. The difference from their reported gain is therefore not the interval implementation; it can only be settled with their paired scores, which we do not have.

\paragraph{Active selection (H4).} Sampling real contexts in proportion to a gradient-boosting prediction of $|R-T|$ (an $n/3$ uniform pilot fits it), with inverse-probability weights, cuts MSE against uniform at $n=120$ by 17--35\% on the three pairs where twin error is predictable from context (cross-fitted $R^2>0.45$), is 8\% worse in the median on weakly predictable pairs, and loses on 18--19 of 21 pairs at $n=30$--60. Coverage of the plug-in interval is 92.4\% at worst.

# Research log

## 2026-10-20 · HCMST couples lens: relationship quality, unmarried and same-sex couples, checked on a later cohort

- New data: Stanford's *How Couples Meet and Stay Together*, official files from data.stanford.edu. The 2009 cohort gives 3,009 partnered couples (1,928 married, 1,081 unmarried, 474 same-sex), re-interviewed through 2017: 8,046 couple-periods and 527 break-ups, exactly HCMST's own combined break-up variable (503) plus the 24 wave-6 break-ups. Every sample count matches Stanford's documentation. 98 partner deaths are censored.
- Logistic per-period hazards held out by couple (5 repeats × 5 folds, 1,000 couple-bootstrap CIs). The additive core model gains 25.5% (22.6–28.3%) in log-loss over a period-only model, C-index 0.878 (0.864–0.892), calibration slope 0.98; the riskiest tenth breaks up at 4.3 times the average rate. Relationship quality alone gains 6.2%; boosting (26.3%) adds little and is not exported. Within married couples the C-index is 0.84 (0.80–0.88), unmarried 0.755, same-sex 0.83.
- External check: the core model trained on 2009 and applied to the separate 2017 cohort gives AUC 0.925 (0.906–0.941) for break-up by 2020 (observed/expected 0.90) and 0.928 by 2022 (1.03).
- Break-up by 2017 by rated relationship quality: excellent 14.3%, good 36.3%, fair 55.0%, poor or very poor 60.3% (62 couples). Married couples: 4.6%, 18.9%, 27.5% and 47.1%. Odds ratios per period, holding the other inputs fixed (associations): good 2.57, fair 5.11, poor 13.7 (7.1–29.5) against excellent; not living together 3.56; unmarried 2.08; each doubling of years together 0.66; two women against a man and a woman 2.57. Meeting online (0.89, 0.61–1.18), interracial pairing, mixed party, different religions and parental disapproval show no clear effect.
- The bundle carries a `population_reference` (weighted p1–p99 of held-out predicted break-up by 2.2, 4.1 and 8.4 years, by marital status, quality, age, education, how met, interracial and duration; cells of 100+ couples, extreme quantiles only for 200+/500+). Median 8-year break-up: 11.2% overall, 7.1% married, 60% unmarried. All five exported models match Python to 10<sup>-9</sup> (`app/tests/hcmst.test.mjs`).
- Limits: one partner reports for both; attrition is heavy (46% response by wave 5), assumed unrelated to break-up given the inputs; the penalty was chosen once on all couples, a slight optimism; the 2017–2020 window includes the pandemic's start. The lens is listed in the app; scoring it needs a relationship-quality field on the form, next.

## 2026-10-20 · App: compare with the population, "just me", candidate partners, batch CSV and a dashboard

- New `app/tools/population_reference.py` refits each NSFG 2002 lens's models on the published five folds (reproducing the held-out gains: additive 3.024%, tree 0.914%, logistic 2.982%, forest 2.262%, boosting 2.588%) and writes survey-weighted quantiles of every woman's held-out 5/10/20-year dissolution risk at her wedding, overall and by seven subgroups (cells of at least 100 marriages, nothing person-level). The median predicted 10-year dissolution is 33.7%, against the survey's own Kaplan–Meier 36.2%. The official CDC file is byte-identical to the mirror copy used before.
- `population.js` places a couple at a percentile overall and within each subgroup it belongs to. NLSY79 lenses say they have no reference, because their person-level extracts are not in the repository.
- "Just me" averages one person's risk over a partner distribution (weighted spouse-trait frequencies, cells of at least 30). It is shown only when the publishable cells cover at least half of that person's group's marriages, and says why otherwise.
- Candidate partners (`batch.js`) are ranked per lens, with an "indistinguishable from the top" flag when model ranges overlap; nobody is called ideal. Batch CSV scoring validates every row, caps at 10,000, and downloads one row per couple, lens and horizon. Nothing leaves the browser and batch data never go in the URL.
- The dashboard (`dashboard.js`) ranks partner profiles for a given person, groups those overlapping the best into one tier, shows one trait at a time, and ranks what-ifs with model agreement. Non-negotiables (locked or excluded values, a maximum age gap) live in the link's `#` fragment.
- Tests: 32 app test files and 18 `test_app.py` tests pass; browser checks at 1200 and 390 px in light and dark showed no horizontal scroll and no unlabelled inputs.

## 2026-10-20 · Lifelong marriage needs mortality: UN WPP 2024 death rates and a competing-risks module

- New data: UN World Population Prospects 2024 single-year U.S. death rates by sex (`mx1dt`, CC BY 3.0 IGO), from the `wpp2024` R data package on GitHub. SSA, CDC and the Human Mortality Database are still unreachable. Life expectancy recomputed from the rates matches the UN's published e0 within 0.02 years at 12 points from 1950 to 2023 (2023: men 76.862 against 76.864, women 81.847 against 81.849) (`reproducibility/un_wpp2024_mortality/`).
- New `app/mortality.js` `competingRisks()` splits each marriage year exactly between divorce and the death of either partner, using constant hazards and calendar-year cohort death rates. It returns P(divorce first), P(widowhood first, i.e. lifelong) and the unresolved remainder, and stops where a lens gives no number. It reproduces the Python reference on 24 couples to within 10<sup>-9</sup>.
- Why it matters: for a man and a woman both 30, marrying in 2025, the chance both are still alive is 93.3% after 20 years and 58.2% after 50. Even at a constant 1% divorce rate, 62.3% of such marriages end in widowhood rather than divorce (37.7% in divorce). "Chance of no divorce by year 40" therefore overstates how many marriages are still going then.
- Limits: these are population death rates. Married people live longer and spouses' deaths are correlated; neither is modelled. Rates after 2023 are UN projections.
- `app/ROADMAP.md` section A now lists what workstream A has delivered (four datasets, three lenses, the mortality table) and what remains blocked (later NSFG cycles, NLSY97, HCMST, GSS, Divorce Predictors Scale, CDC aggregates).

## 2026-10-20 · Out-of-distribution score so the app can abstain on unusual couples

- New `tools/ood.py`: the squared Mahalanobis distance of a couple's fixed inputs from the 4,126 NSFG 2002 first marriages, turned into a percentile among held-out women (5 person folds). It ships as `ood` in `models/nsfg2002_family.json`, and `engine.oodScore()` matches Python on 68 of 68 rows to 10<sup>-9</sup>.
- Examples: a wife 24 / husband 26 couple wed in 1995 scores the 26th percentile, a 38/40 couple wed in 2002 the 86th, a 17/45 couple the 98.8th, and a 16/60 couple 99.1, above the suggested 99th-percentile abstention line. The held-out tail is heavy because a few recorded ages are implausible, so the score complements per-input range checks rather than replacing them.
- `app/ROADMAP.md` section B now lists what workstream B finished (individual lens, NSFG classical family, abstention score) and what remains: page wiring with C, Cox/Weibull, k-NN, OOD for NLSY79, and per-couple intervals.

## 2026-10-19 · Classical model family on NSFG 2002: logistic hazard, naive Bayes, forest, boosting

- Trained four more model families on workstream A's NSFG 2002 first-marriage intervals (4,126 marriages, 1,492 dissolutions), using the same person folds. Held-out deviance gains over a constant rate: discrete-time logistic hazard 3.0% (95% person-bootstrap CI 1.9–4.1), Poisson gradient boosting 2.6% (1.6–3.7), random forest 2.3% (1.5–3.0), naive Bayes 0.2% (−1.8 to 2.1). A's additive spline Poisson gets 3.0% (2.0–4.1) and marriage length alone 0.4%.
- Flexible models did not beat additive ones, the same pattern as the NLSY79 couple models. Naive Bayes is badly overconfident at the top: it predicts 173 dissolutions per 1,000 marriage-years in the riskiest tenth against 91 observed.
- `engine.js` now runs logistic, naive Bayes, forest and boosted models, with exact explanations: log-odds terms, and Saabas path attributions for tree ensembles. It matches 272 of 272 scikit-learn predictions to 10<sup>-9</sup>. The bundle is listed for the app's lens selector as `models/nsfg2002_family.json`; scoring it needs workstream C's NSFG form adapter.

## 2026-10-19 · Relationship-quality lenses: Fair's affairs surveys and Thornes & Collard

- New data: Ray Fair's two affairs surveys, *Redbook* 1974 (6,366 married women, 2,053 reporting an affair) and *Psychology Today* 1969 (601 men and women, 150). They come from statsmodels v0.14.4 on GitHub. The 601-person file matches an independent R (AER) copy row by row. They add the input the government surveys lack: self-rated marriage happiness. The outcome is a reported affair, not divorce (`reproducibility/fair_affairs/`).
- Redbook, share reporting an affair by marriage rating: 74.7% "very poor", 55.1% "fair", 18.1% "very good". Held out (5×5-fold): rating alone gives AUC 0.687 (8.6% log-loss gain); the additive logistic on all inputs AUC 0.744 (13.2%); a depth-3 tree 0.722 (11.3%). Psychology Today is weaker and noisy: additive logistic AUC 0.691, fold gains −3% to +15%.
- Odds ratios (associations, cross-sectional, direction unknown): 0.49 per rating point and 0.69 per religiousness point in Redbook; 1.30 for men and 1.46 with children in Psychology Today.
- Thornes & Collard (1979, 1,036 British people, via `vcd::PreSex`): odds of being divorced rather than married are 2.88 (2.10–3.96) with premarital sex and 5.73 (3.55–9.24) with extramarital sex, gender-adjusted. The sample is case-control-style (48% divorced), so it gives odds ratios only and no app number (`reproducibility/thornes_collard_presex/`).
- Engine: added a `logistic_glm` model kind (probability output; contributions on the log-odds scale). Both Fair bundles are listed in `models/index.json` without an adapter. Parity: 300 of 300 Python predictions are reproduced to 10<sup>-9</sup> (`app/tests/fair.test.mjs`). The exporter moved to `reproducibility/bundle_export.py`; the NSFG bundle regenerates byte-identical.
- Still blocked today: CDC, UCI, GSS, NLS and Stanford HCMST (HTTP 000).

## 2026-10-19 · NSFG 2002 lens: first-marriage dissolution from the wedding day

- New dataset: NSFG 2002 (Cycle 6) women's first marriages. These are 4,126 ever-married women aged 15–44 with month-exact wedding and dissolution dates, 1,492 divorces, annulments or separations, and 30,341 person-years. CDC is still blocked, so the file comes from Allen Downey's ThinkStats2 copy on GitHub, checked against the official record count (7,643) and weighted total (61.56 million) (`reproducibility/nsfg2002_first_marriage/`).
- Survey-weighted Kaplan–Meier: 78.0% of first marriages are still intact at 5 years, 63.8% at 10 and 49.3% at 20. Among wives who married under 20, 68.7% are intact at 5 years, against 87.2% for wives who married at 25–29.
- Person-held-out (5-fold) Poisson deviance gain over a constant rate: duration-only curve 0.38%, additive spline Poisson 3.02% (folds 2.30–4.13%; 2.65% over the duration curve), depth-3 Poisson tree 0.91%. Held-out calibration runs from 1.3% to 9.5% a year observed, against 1.6% to 10.3% predicted.
- Associations from the additive model, holding everything else fixed: wife married at 17 versus 22, rate ratio 1.63; premarital cohabitation 1.24; husband previously married 1.29; husband with children from a prior relationship 1.47; same race-ethnicity 0.72; wife from an intact family 0.70. These are not causes.
- Exported as `app/models/nsfg2002_first_marriage.json`, in the same `poisson_glm`/`tree` schema as the NLSY79 bundle plus `inputs`, `abstain` and `weighted_survival` fields. The engine reproduces 180 of 180 Python predictions to 10<sup>-9</sup> (`app/tests/nsfg2002.test.mjs`). The husband's education was left out because it is recorded only for women still married to that husband, so it would leak the outcome.
- Still blocked from cloud sessions today: CDC, NLS, ICPSR, UCI, Stanford (HCMST), GSS, Hugging Face, Zenodo and Dataverse. Next for workstream A: Fair's affairs (Rdatasets, reachable) and later NSFG cycles if a verifiable mirror turns up.

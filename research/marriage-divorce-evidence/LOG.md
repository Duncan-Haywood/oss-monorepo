# Research log

## 2026-10-20 · Flexible models and a pooled nine-survey model: neither beats the single-lens additive models where data are large

- Flexible models were tuned with nested, grouped cross-validation on five lenses: LightGBM, boosting, forests, an EBM, an elastic net with pairs, and a stack. None beat the lens's best GLM with a 95% interval above zero. GSS: random forest +0.42 points (−1.55 to 2.25). HCMST: stack +0.65 (−0.11 to 1.43). Tree models fit training folds far better than held-out ones; on GSS, LightGBM scored 44.5% on training folds against 7.4% held out. Shuffled outcomes scored at or below zero for every model. Six large lenses were not run (see NEXT.md).
- The pooled Bayesian hazard model covers nine surveys, 321,121 units and 44 inputs, with survey-specific baselines and chained-equation imputation. Held out by person, it beats the single-survey models in HCMST (+4.81 points, 1.88–7.79) and the LA newlyweds (+1.78, 0.60–3.16) and loses in NSFG (−0.85), NLSY79 (−0.50) and Canada (−0.40). Leaving one survey out shows that the effects carry across surveys and the baselines do not. 90% intervals covered 85% of held-out groups. It has not been exported to the app.
- [NEXT.md](NEXT.md) lists the action items for later runs: the form inputs, the guided decision flow, the large-lens model runs, the pooled-model export, and the owner asks.

## 2026-10-20 · In-browser training runs the five folds in parallel: 45.5 s → 18.8 s

- The "Train the models yourself" panel now runs a pool of workers, by default one per spare core: at most five, and fewer on devices reporting 4 GB of memory or less. Visitors can choose 1–5 threads.
- Each worker loads the NSFG extract once and takes tasks from a shared queue: the five held-out folds, then the refits on all marriages. When the last fold arrives, the next free worker merges the folds and computes the metrics. Arrays move between threads by transfer, not copy, and the page's own thread only routes messages.
- All five models at the Python settings, in headless Chromium on a 4-core cloud machine:

  | Threads | Wall time | Longest main-thread stall |
  | --- | --- | --- |
  | 1 | 45.5 s | 93 ms |
  | 3 (the default here) | 18.8 s | 57 ms |
  | 4 | 17.6 s | 62 ms |
  | 5 | 17.5 s | 86 ms |

  A 390 px run with 25 boosting rounds on 3 threads took 6.9 s.
- The metrics are identical at every thread count: GLM 5.688%, tree 3.333%, boosting 6.30%, C 0.695 / 0.651 / 0.703. Merging folds in the page took stalls to 177 ms at 5 threads, so the merge moved into a worker.
- Cancel stops every worker. There were no console errors and no horizontal scroll. `tests/train_parallel.test.mjs` computes the folds in scrambled order through structured cloning and matches the single-thread result to 10<sup>−12</sup>. No real phone has been timed yet.

## 2026-10-20 · Before the wedding: leakage audit, harmonized features and cross-dataset validation on five surveys

- Question: how well can divorce be predicted from facts known on the wedding day? Built one harmonized pre-wedding table per first-marriage lens (NSFG 1995–2023 43,336 marriages; NLSY79 6,768; NLSY97 5,076; SIPP 176,303; Canada GSS 48,656), each reproducing its lens's published sample exactly, with strict timing rules (`reproducibility/prewedding/SPEC.md`).
- Leakage audit: four lenses used information from after the wedding. NLSY79 spouse traits from the first post-wedding interview (published 5.07% → 4.52% additive, 5.60% → 4.54% boosting), NLSY97 spouse education and pregnancy defined by a later birth (4.82% → 4.50%), NSFG degrees earned in the wedding year (4.74% → 4.52%), Canada region at interview (3.18% → 3.07%); SIPP's main model was clean. Missingness itself leaks (in NLSY79 29.7% of marriages with a blank roster spouse ended within two years against 6.1%), so the harmonized models use no missing indicators or "Unknown" levels.
- Within each survey, held out by person: C 0.634–0.664 and AUC at 10 years 0.66–0.68 for the best model, 10-year observed/expected 0.99–1.03. Boosting beats the additive model by at most 0.7 points and not at all in NLSY97; a discrete-time logistic hazard equals the additive Poisson model.
- Across surveys (20 train→test pairs): ranking transports within the U.S. (C 0.61–0.66; NSFG → NLSY97 0.647 with observed/expected 0.99) but levels are off by up to a third, and U.S. models predict 1.5–2.2 times Canada's divorce while ranking at C 0.59–0.61. Wedding year must not be extrapolated: NLSY97's additive model loses 31–38% on older cohorts unless it is dropped. NSFG transports best, so it is the candidate for a "before the wedding" app lens (next round).

## 2026-10-20 · Decision evidence: what changing a decision did in trials, and how strong the evidence is for each input

- New `reproducibility/decision_evidence/`: 46 records. 35 are evidence about decisions (12 randomised trials or trial subgroups, 7 meta-analyses of trials, 6 quasi-experiments, 1 selection model, 7 adjusted observational including 5 from this project, 1 cross-sectional, 1 review) and 11 are decision-aid standards and risk-communication studies. Each has design, population, n, outcome, effect, follow-up, caveats, verbatim quote and DOI. All 46 pass the literature-evidence checks (quote fragments verbatim in the cached source, every quoted number present, every stored value printed or recomputed from printed inputs) and all 36 DOIs match Crossref. Provenance: 19 full text, 21 abstract only, 1 secondhand, 5 from this project's own METHODS files. Source texts stay out of Git.
- Relationship education: Army PREP lowered divorce at 2 years (7.6% vs 11.9%; 8.1% vs 14.9% at the higher-risk site, none at the other); an engaged/newlywed trial found 11% vs 24% dissolution at 3 years, but a one-session film-discussion arm matched 15 hours of skills training. The federal trials are null on stability: Building Strong Families 57.4% vs 60.5% still together at 3 years (p = .053, wrong direction), Supporting Healthy Marriage 81.5% vs 81.5% at 30 months (impact 0.0, SE 1.0); ACF-funded programmes pooled show small quality gains (d = .114) and no stability effect.
- Couple therapy improves satisfaction (d = .585 vs no treatment over 30 RCTs) but no trial compares divorce with untreated couples over years; 25.7% and 27.9% of seriously distressed couples had separated 5 years after IBCT or TBCT.
- Causes: no-fault divorce laws brought divorces forward (+0.27 per 1,000 people a year in the first two years) without a lasting rise; plant-closure job loss raised divorce 18% in Sweden (CI includes 1) and not detectably in the US; $25-50k lottery wins changed nothing; a welfare work-incentive trial cut divorce 3.5 points among recipient families but not applicants. No experiment or instrument estimates age at marriage, education, cohabitation or religious attendance effects on divorce.
- `FACTORS.md` / `factor_grades.json` grade all 10 changeable inputs (numbers copied from verified records; the build fails if a grade claims more than its evidence). `DESIGN.md` is a 14-item IPDAS and risk-communication checklist mapped to the app's views with today's status (absolute risks with time frames: met; icon arrays, evidence grades beside what-ifs, interventions as options, user testing: not yet). `app/data/decision_evidence.json` is the app-ready export; `DecisionEvidenceTests` re-check it.

## 2026-10-20 · Compare many scenarios in the calculator, lens by lens

- The owner asked for the app to "easily compare many scenarios". A new "Compare options" tab (framed as a decision aid: each saved scenario is an "option", the baseline is "your current plan") saves the full form (both profiles, the marriage, every optional lens question and the published-evidence answers) as named scenarios, up to 50, with rename, duplicate, delete, reorder, load-into-the-form-and-re-save, and "vary one thing": copies that differ in one answer across its allowed values (age at the wedding, years married, living together first, degree, schooling, relationship quality, marriage happiness, religious attendance, work hours, wages, earlier marriages and children, family of origin, relationship status, how you met, a baby expected, home ownership), at most 12 per click, labelled by the change.
- The comparison is one column per lens at a horizon the lens supports (defaults: the three lenses `summary.js` ranks most relevant to the baseline). Each cell gives the expected break-ups out of 100 couples like you (and the percentage) at one horizon shared by every option, with the range across the lens's models; the change from the current plan in points, with the smallest-to-largest change across the models, and as a ratio; and a paired verdict from each scoring model's own difference: "models agree" (every model moves the same way, each by at least 1 point), "most models agree (k of n)" (a strict majority, median move at least 1 point), or "cannot tell apart" with the reason (the models disagree on the direction, or the change is under 1 point). Overlapping min–max ranges are not used for this: they measure how much the models disagree on the level, not on the difference. - New `app/mortality.js` `competingRisks()` splits each marriage year exactly between divorce and the death of either partner, using constant hazards and calendar-year cohort death rates. It returns P(divorce first), P(widowhood first, i.e. lifelong) and the unresolved remainder, and stops where a lens gives no number. It reproduces the Python reference on 24 couples to within 10<sup>-9</sup>.
- Why it matters: for a man and a woman both 30, marrying in 2025, the chance both are still alive is 93.3% after 20 years and 58.2% after 50. Even at a constant 1% divorce rate, 62.3% of such marriages end in widowhood rather than divorce (37.7% in divorce). "Chance of no divorce by year 40" therefore overstates how many marriages are still going then.
- Limits: these are population death rates. Married people live longer and spouses' deaths are correlated; neither is modelled. Rates after 2023 are UN projections.

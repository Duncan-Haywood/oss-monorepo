Will your marriage last? A divorce risk calculator for before you marry

Thinking of marrying someone, or comparing possible partners? Enter what you will both know on the wedding day — ages, schooling, family background, earlier marriages and children, living together first, religion — and see what explainable models trained on U.S. and Canadian surveys estimate for the chance of divorce in each year from the wedding, the chance of a lifelong marriage counting mortality, and which pre-wedding traits they link to lower risk. Already married? Switch to the “Already married” view. Everything runs in your browser; nothing you type is sent anywhere.

Read this first. These are statistical associations in past surveys, not a forecast for you and not causes. Most lenses beat a flat average rate by only 3–9% on held-out people; the one that asks about relationship quality (HCMST) does far better, because how a relationship feels now says a lot about the next few years. Fidelity, commitment and much else are not in any of the data. Use it to explore the evidence, not to choose or judge a partner.

What the surveys don’t measure (published research, lower confidence)

Some well-studied risks are not in any lens’s survey: destructive conflict, depression, alcohol problems, neuroticism, earlier cohabitations. Answer any of these to shift the lens’s curve by hazard ratios published in peer-reviewed studies. Every question is optional. Only numbers re-checked word for word against the full text of the source are used, and factors the selected lens already models are not asked, so nothing is counted twice. This is a literature-based adjustment: the studies used other populations and measures, so it is less certain than the lens itself. The answers stay in this page; the link carries only the earlier-cohabitations and no-religion answers, never those about conflict, depression, alcohol or neuroticism.

About Gottman’s accuracy claims

John Gottman’s lab is often quoted as predicting divorce with “over 90% accuracy”. Those figures come from discriminant analyses fitted and scored on the same small samples, with no holdout. When few couples divorce, a rule that predicts nobody divorces already scores high, so the table sets each claim against that rule, using only base rates a retrieved source prints.

  • At 12–17% divorce, predicting that nobody divorces scores 83–88%. A model has to beat that, out of sample, to show skill.
  • Out of sample the accuracy falls: Heyman & Slep (2001) report that “accuracy and predictive value drops precipitously during crossvalidation”, and Kim, Capaldi & Crosby (2007) found that “the major findings of Gottman et al. failed to replicate” in 85 at-risk couples.
  • What holds up prospectively is hostile conflict measured as a hazard: Birditt et al. (2010) followed 373 couples for 16 years, and wives’ destructive conflict in year 1 predicted divorce (HR 1.51 per point on a 1–4 scale). That is the number this page uses for the conflict question; it never uses an accuracy figure.
  • Gottman’s work identifies real correlates of unhappy marriages, especially contempt and hostility. The headline accuracy figures are not evidence that one couple’s divorce can be predicted with 90% accuracy.

          How it works and what it cannot tell you

          • NLSY79 lens. National Longitudinal Survey of Youth 1979 (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics): 40,818 two-year intervals from 5,735 married respondents and 726 legal divorces, survey-weighted. Respondents were 35–64; marriages up to 49 years long. Two additive Poisson regressions, a spline model with schooling-pair and earnings-role interactions, and two shallow Poisson decision trees.
          • NLSY79 individuals lens. The same survey, but each partner is scored from their own traits only (age, age at first marriage, sex, degree, work hours, religious attendance, family income) and the two rates are averaged: 42,259 intervals, 5,899 currently married people, 854 divorces. Additive and spline Poisson models and two Poisson trees; the best beats a flat rate by 1.9% held out.
          • NSFG 2002 lens. National Survey of Family Growth, Cycle 6 (NCHS): 4,126 women’s first marriages followed from the wedding day, 1,492 ending in divorce, annulment or separation, 30,341 person-years. An additive spline Poisson model and a depth-3 Poisson tree, with a duration-only curve as the baseline; a second lens runs a discrete-time logistic hazard, a random forest and gradient boosting on the same data (naive Bayes is shown but not averaged: it is overconfident at the top). It covers newly-weds and young couples; it knows nothing about income or schooling. A couple whose combination of inputs is more unusual than 99% of the surveyed marriages (distance from the training data) gets no number.
          • Newer lenses. NLSY79 and NLSY97 first marriages from the wedding (with the spouse’s traits); NSFG 1995–2023 pooled (the wife’s report, and a both-sexes view from each spouse’s own report); Statistics Canada GSS first marriages (one partner at a time); SIPP first marriages, and SIPP married couples one year ahead with both spouses’ earnings and wealth; ACS one-year divorce incidence (cross-sectional); GSS panels, divorced or separated within 2 years from marriage happiness; HCMST, a break-up within about 8 years from relationship quality, for married and unmarried, same-sex and different-sex couples; and 368 Los Angeles newlyweds over 12 years from satisfaction, self-reported hostility and conflict questions that approximate trained observers’ codes. Each lens shows only the horizons its follow-up covers, refuses couples outside its population, and gives no number when an input it has no “unknown” group for is missing.
          • Exact in the browser. Every model was trained in scikit-learn, exported to JSON and runs here in a small JavaScript engine that reproduces scikit-learn’s predictions to 10−9 (tested).
          • Per year. Each model gives an annual divorce rate for the couple at each marriage year (ages and duration advance; everything else stays as entered). A year’s divorce probability is 1 − e−rate, and the chance of still being married multiplies those years together.
          • Couple. Most surveys interviewed one spouse. Lenses built on one respondent score the couple from each partner’s point of view (where that partner fits the lens) and average them; the NSFG lenses use the wife’s report, as the survey did; the newlywed study measured both spouses. Same-sex couples get numbers only from the lenses whose data include them (HCMST, SIPP first marriages, ACS); the others show no number rather than guess.
          • Outside the data. Years outside what a lens saw (NLSY79: ages under 35 or over 64; NSFG: beyond year 24 of marriage) are shaded, and the numbers there are extrapolation. A couple a lens cannot speak to at all (for NSFG: a wife married before, or a wedding age outside the data) gets no number from that lens. Wealth is scored only by the SIPP couples lens and occupation by none (it appears as ACS baselines); relationship quality is scored only by HCMST, the GSS panels (marriage happiness) and the newlyweds.
          • Population comparison. For the NSFG 2002 lenses, every surveyed woman’s first marriage was scored at its wedding by models fitted on the other four-fifths of women (the same held-out folds as the skill numbers), and the app stores only survey-weighted percentiles of those predictions, overall and in groups of at least 100 marriages. Your percentile is read off those, interpolated, and capped at “below p1” and “above p99”. The NLSY79 lenses have no reference yet (their person-level data are not in the repository).
          • Published evidence (lower confidence). The optional questions under “What the surveys don’t measure” apply hazard ratios from 9 published records (Birditt et al. 2010, Breslau et al. 2011, Roberts et al. 2007, Rosenfeld & Roesler 2019, Manning & Cohen 2012, Killewald 2016), each quote and number checked against the full text. Every hazard ratio is drawn from its published interval plus a between-study spread, stacked adjustments are shrunk for overlap, and the result is labelled literature-based. Full appraisal of 286 records: EVIDENCE.md.
          • Not causal, not personal. Coefficients describe who divorced in one cohort. No validated “ideal partner”, safe anniversary or personal forecast is established. Full methods: ml_couples/METHODS.md and the model card.

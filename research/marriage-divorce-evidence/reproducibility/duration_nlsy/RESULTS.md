# Does a particular marriage anniversary mark a clear risk drop?

The observed annual divorce rate generally declines with accumulated marriage duration, but this analysis does **not** identify a statistically clear anniversary threshold.

Crude weighted divorces per 1,000 observed person-years were 16.74 at 0–<5 married years, 12.92 at 5–<10, 12.70 at 10–<15, 8.99 at 15–<20, 6.90 at 20–<25, 5.32 at 25–<30, and 2.78 at 30–<40. The 40+ estimate was 1.46, but rests on only two divorce events and has a wide approximate 95% interval of 0.32–6.68. It should not be treated as a reliable threshold or near-zero risk.

At the same modeled age-55–<65 covariate distribution, adjusted annual rates per 1,000 were 6.93, 5.70, 6.03, 4.79, 4.11, 3.61, 2.60 and 1.98 across the same bands. Adjustment includes both spouses' schooling, earnings role, combined wage quartile, current age, marriage order, wave and respondent sex. These model-standardized rates rely on pooling information across the observed sample; several individual older target cells contain few events.

None of the seven predefined adjacent-band contrasts was statistically clear. Even before multiplicity adjustment, the smallest p-value was 0.135; after Holm correction the smallest was 0.945. An omnibus test across all duration indicators gave p=0.0234, which is compatible with an overall duration association but does not locate a sharp step at any anniversary.

These data concern a left-truncated cohort observed in midlife/later life. They do not represent all newlywed marriages from inception. The annual rate for an eligible marriage already in the 20–<25-year band is a conditional observed incidence estimate, not its next-five-year divorce probability. No cumulative five-year risk is inferred by multiplying or compounding these rates.

# Joint annual ACS analysis and first-marriage sensitivity

Source is the original 2023 national 1-year ACS person microdata already extracted in sibling `acs/persons_30_64.parquet`. Retains ages30–64. Run `OPENBLAS_NUM_THREADS=1 python3 analyze_joint.py`, then `python3 derive_first_marriage.py` and `OPENBLAS_NUM_THREADS=1 python3 analyze_first_marriage.py`. Python requires numpy, pandas, scipy, pyarrow.

## Two denominators

Annual outcome is MARHD=1, divorce in preceding12months, a rolling interview window rather than calendar-year2023. The approximate annual at-risk population is currently married or separated (MAR1 or4) OR reporting divorce in preceding12months. The comparison denominator includes every ever-married respondent (MAR!=5). Numerator is identical under both denominators, but denominator population and standardization targets differ. Neither estimates exact start-of-year married exposure or person-time. Do not name these proportions lifetime risk or multiply them by years.

The approximate denominator excludes people divorced before the window who did not remarry; this avoids diluting the denominator with many people with no current marriage exposure. However, it includes newly married and remarried respondents with partial-year exposure, excludes some people married at window start who became widowed, and only samples surviving resident respondents. Current marital status, age, education, employment and income are observed at interview. Past-year income overlaps outcome and can follow divorce. The design does not establish temporality, eliminate selection, or identify causes.

## Joint models

Separate sex-specific survey-weighted logistic regressions include age in5-year categories, degree level, total personal income in2023dollars, current employment, current number of marriages (once/twice/three-plus), and broad race/ethnicity as contextual adjustment. Race is not a recommendation or a desirability ranking; no race-based profile is defined. All factor main effects are included simultaneously. Income and employment are now mutually adjusted. No interactions are included; odds effects are assumed homogeneous within sex across model covariate levels. Category definitions match ACS baseline analysis.

Current number of marriages may change after divorce if remarriage occurs in the same12month window. Its coefficient is descriptive, not a prospective marriage-order effect. Most-recent-marriage year is complete for all ever-married records, including divorced respondents. It was omitted from the all-marriage models because same-window divorce/remarriage may reset it after the event. The first-marriage analysis below addresses part of this problem.

## First-marriage restriction

Restrict to MARHT=1 (only one marriage to date), MARHM!=1 (not married within preceding12months), at least1calendar year since most recent marriage, and the approximate at-risk denominator. Recent divorce respondents remain eligible. This avoids resetting marriage year/count through same-window remarriage and removes newlywed partial-year exposure. It also excludes people who divorced and remarried, so these selected survivor samples cannot estimate the full risk of all first marriages at inception.

Replace current age with approximate age at marriage and duration; do not also enter current age because they are mathematically related. Duration=2023−MARHYP, categorized1–4,5–9,10–19,20+years. Approximate age at marriage=AGEP−duration, categorized<22,22–26,27–31,32–36,37+. Missing marriage month and birthday timing can shift derived age/duration about a year. For recently divorced respondents, duration runs through interview rather than divorce date, extending past actual married exposure up to12months. These are coarse controls, not exact event-time survival analysis.

## Predictive margins and common scale

For each sex/denominator, factor-level predictive margins replace one covariate level for every member of that eligible population, retain their other observed covariates, predict using the fitted model, and average using survey weights. This creates a common standardized probability scale for comparisons within the same model. Setting a variable does not represent an identified causal intervention, and can generate covariate combinations with little support. Reported observed subgroup n/events help identify sparse categories but do not prove overlap for every modeled combination.

`marginal_contrasts.csv` compares each factor level with a predeclared reference (first level in code) in matched replicates; these are formal differences in percentage points, not comparisons of overlapping marginal CIs. `factor_association_ranges.csv` summarizes highest-minus-lowest point-estimate margins within each factor, excluding armed-forces category from employment and excluding race from ranking. These extrema depend on the chosen category boundaries and their spread, are selected after fitting, and do not define a unique ordering of importance. Their CIs condition on the selected pair and are not selection-adjusted. Neither ranges nor odds ratios rank causal effects or predictive variable importance. No multiplicity correction is applied; all analyses are exploratory.

## Profiles

Seven profiles were declared before the final fitting (the two $200k+ profiles were added as a specified extension, not selected by fitting): bachelor/employed/$50–74k/one marriage; bachelor/employed/$100–149k/one; master/employed/$100–149k/one; bachelor/employed/$200k+/one; master/employed/$200k+/one; bachelor/not-in-labor-force/zero-or-negative-income/one; some-college/employed/$25–49k/two. The first-marriage sensitivity omits the last profile. Age and contextual race distributions are standardized over the sex-specific eligible target; first-marriage models standardize age at marriage, duration and race. These are descriptive comparisons, not optimization, personalized predictions, or advice to change income/work status. In particular, low observed divorce among women with no income can reflect marital financial dependence, selection, reverse causality or other confounding. Profiles include joint observed n, events and raw weighted proportion; disagreement with standardized model estimates reflects compositional adjustment and model assumptions.

## Uncertainty and checks

All point fits use PWGTP; refit each model with all80replicate weights. Each replicate predictive margin averages over that replicate's target weights. Standard errors use SDR variance4/80sum((replicate−point)^2), retaining covariance for contrasts. Report95% normal intervals on probabilities/differences; odds-ratio intervals are exponentiated coefficient intervals. Model score convergence checks are retained. Profile estimates depend on the logit functional form, additive specification and support; survey CIs do not incorporate uncertainty due to model misspecification, unmeasured confounding, imputation or denominator reconstruction errors. CIs for margins are not currently clipped, though every estimate here is positive and away from1.

Important positivity limitation: factor margins that force age-at-marriage or duration separately while retaining the other may imply current ages outside the sampled30–64range, and some education/income/work combinations are uncommon. Age-at-marriage and duration margins therefore involve model extrapolation and should not be treated as precise guidance about timing marriage. Profile predictions preserve each target member's joint age-at-marriage/duration combination and are preferable for the income/degree profile comparisons, though support still varies across other covariates.

For factor priority use the predeclared reference contrasts: high-school/GED versus BA; $100–149k and $200k+ versus $50–74k; unemployed versus employed; two marriages versus one (only all-marriage model). They share the same percentage-point scale within a sex/denominator/model. Extreme category ranges remain exploratory and should not drive the main ranking.

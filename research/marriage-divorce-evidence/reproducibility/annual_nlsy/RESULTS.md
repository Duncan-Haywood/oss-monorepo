# What the prospective annual analysis supports

The primary denominator includes legally married **and separated** respondents. In 45,977 observed intervals from 6,238 respondents, there were 1,463 legal divorces and 369 widowhood censors over 91,578.75 unweighted person-years. Weighted annual incidence was 14.24 divorces per 1,000 person-years among men (approximate 95% CI 12.97–15.64) and 14.38 among women (13.15–15.73). These are midlife-to-later-life NLSY79 cohort rates, not current U.S. rates for every adult or lifetime risks.

Already being separated strongly changes the immediate legal-divorce outlook. Currently married baselines had 10.30 divorces per 1,000 person-years among men and 8.32 among women; separated baselines had approximately 119 per 1,000 for both sexes. Excluding separated people removes an important legal-divorce risk group. Conditioning on separation state changes associations because it is also part of the pathway to divorce.

Jointly adjusted associations without conditioning on separation state show the strongest supported financial contrast in the bottom family-income quartile: versus the second quartile, rate ratio 1.64 (1.27–2.12) for men and 2.93 (2.26–3.81) for women. Men's top versus second quartile rate ratio was 0.65 (0.45–0.94). Predictors refer to prior-calendar-year combined family income at the interval's start, not personal salary. Separation may already have changed income before baseline; adding baseline separation state attenuates these financial associations. These are therefore not causal estimates of earning more money.

Men's graduate-versus-high-school adjusted rate ratio was 0.64 (0.39–1.03); bachelor's 0.75 (0.54–1.04). Women's graduate-versus-high-school rate ratio was 1.83 (1.22–2.74). Thus a generic 'more degrees always means lower annual divorce risk' profile is unsupported. These are separate respondent samples, not spouse pairs, and concern marriages surviving into the observed ages. Detailed PhD/professional cells are too sparse for useful rankings.

Weekly religious attendance in 2000 did **not** show a clear lower prospective annual rate after adjustment. Results remain uncertain when attendance is refreshed in 2012. A prior cross-sectional association with religious practice should not be converted into a demonstrated protective annual effect.

Two prespecified currently-married profiles illustrate observed rates, without searching for a minimum or requiring religion: college credential or higher, above-median family income, and 2,000+ respondent work hours had 5.68 divorces per 1,000 person-years among men (3.97–8.14; 643 respondents, 45 events). Among women, the college+/upper-income profile with fewer than 2,000 hours had 5.52 (3.37–9.06; 478 respondents, 20 events), versus 7.50 (5.04–11.16; 516 respondents, 36 events) with 2,000+ hours. The overlapping intervals and selection do not justify recommending reduced employment. These profile rates should be compared with the currently-married baseline subgroup, not with the primary denominator containing separated people.

All intervals and adjusted models use baseline-wave survey weights. Uncertainty clusters repeated observations by person but cannot reproduce full survey PSU/stratum variance. Complete-case model samples contain 569 male and 587 female divorce events; missing follow-up and covariates can bias comparisons. See METHODS.md and primary audit files for exclusions and verification.

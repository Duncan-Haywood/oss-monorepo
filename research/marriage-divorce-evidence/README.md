# Which couple characteristics predict annual divorce incidence?

**Headline:** in original public U.S. microdata, interpretable models identify broad associations but modest prediction gains among intact marriages. The enriched additive couple model reduced respondent-held-out Poisson loss by about **3.4%** versus a constant rate; more flexible trees/interactions did not improve on it. Higher schooling is associated with lower incidence, but no validated personal matchmaking optimum or safe anniversary cutoff is established.

**Try it — will your marriage last? A divorce risk calculator for before you marry:** the [calculator](app/) ([live](https://duncan-haywood.github.io/monorepo-projects/research/marriage-divorce-evidence/app/)) opens on **Before the wedding**: for an engaged couple, or for you and a candidate partner, it estimates the chance of divorce in each year from the wedding day and the chance of a lifelong marriage counting mortality.

- It uses only facts known by the wedding day, and only lenses whose clock starts at the wedding.
  - **Default:** workstream B's harmonized before-the-wedding model. It is fitted on 43,336 NSFG 1995–2023 first marriages, scores both partners as the surveyed spouse and averages them.
    - Held-out skill: C 0.661, AUC at 10 years 0.656.
    - It ranks as well on other surveys: C 0.647 on NLSY97, 0.626 on SIPP, 0.621 on NLSY79 and 0.611 on Canada. It predicts Canada's level 1.7 times too high.
  - **Alongside it:** NSFG 1995–2023 pooled, NLSY97, NLSY79 first marriages, SIPP first marriages, Statistics Canada GSS and NSFG 2002.
  - **Example, typical engaged couple** (28 and 30, bachelor's degrees, living together first, no children before): 7.4% break-up risk by 5 years, 12.5% by 10, 19.1% by 20. That is the 2nd percentile of NSFG's first marriages, whose median 10-year risk is 28%.
  - **Example, younger couple** (22 and 23, a child before the wedding, not living together first): 22.5% by 10 years, the 32nd percentile.
- **Not engaged yet?** For a dating or cohabiting couple (different- or same-sex), two HCMST 2009–2017 models estimate the chance of marrying each other within 8.4 years against breaking up first. The inputs are your ratings of the relationship, years together, living together, parents' view, how you met, ages and schooling. Held-out AUC is 0.73 for marrying and 0.71 for breaking up; on HCMST's later 2017 cohort, never used in training, it is 0.79 and 0.85 by 2022. Then the pre-wedding lens takes over. Two additive models are run, so the page shows their range. A couple living together for 3 years who rate the relationship excellent and good: 60% marry each other within 8.4 years (models 51–70%) and 32% break up first (22–42%). The same couple rating it fair and living apart: 9% (7–12%) and 87% (82–92%). Across the survey, 22% married and 54% broke up first.
- Lenses that score marriages already under way sit behind **Already married**: NLSY79 couples and individuals, GSS panels, HCMST couples, the Los Angeles newlyweds, SIPP couples and ACS.
- How well can divorce be predicted before the wedding? Modestly. The leakage-free models in [`reproducibility/prewedding/`](reproducibility/prewedding/) rank who divorces first at a C-index of 0.63–0.66 within each survey and 0.61–0.66 across U.S. surveys. That is better than chance, far from certain.
- The lenses disagree on levels. For a typical engaged couple (28 and 30, both with a bachelor's degree, living together first, no earlier marriages or children), the chance of a break-up within 10 years runs from 6.7% (NSFG 1995–2023, models 3.8–9.7%) to 24.7% (NSFG 2002, which also counts separations and holds weddings at 2002). The app shows every lens side by side and never pools them.

In either view, enter two profiles to see:

- a **summary across the evidence lenses for your stage** (9 scorable before the wedding, 7 when already married): each lens’s chance of divorce or break-up at the horizons its own data support (5/10/20 years for the from-the-wedding lenses; 2 years for GSS; 2.2/4.1/8.4 years for HCMST; 2.3–12 years for the Los Angeles newlyweds; 1 year for SIPP couples and ACS), with the range across its models, its held-out skill (gain, C-index, AUC), how typical the couple is of its data, its population percentile, and a plain-language synthesis of where the lenses agree or disagree and which is most relevant — never a pooled number;
- lenses that ask about the relationship itself: relationship quality (HCMST, for married and unmarried, same-sex and different-sex couples), marriage happiness (GSS panels), and newlywed satisfaction, self-reported hostility and conflict behaviour (an approximation of trained observers’ codes, labelled as such);
- year-by-year divorce odds and the chance of no divorce by year 40 for the year-by-year lenses;
- per-input explanations, one set per partner where a lens scores the couple from both sides (each exact for that partner; the couple's rate is their average);
- how much the models agree;
- which changes the models link to lower risk.
- where the couple sits among each lens’s surveyed population, overall and within their own subgroups (percentiles of held-out predictions, at the reference’s own quantity: cumulative risk, or the annual rate for NLSY79 couples), plus the observed ACS one-year divorce rates for people like each partner, with 95% intervals;
- a "just me" estimate averaged over the partners people like you actually married;
- a ranked table for several candidate partners, grouping those the models cannot tell apart;
- a dashboard of partner traits and what-ifs, with non-negotiables you lock or exclude;
- a **profile report** for you and for the person you are looking at: the predicted break-ups if you married them, averaged over the partners people like you actually marry, and if you "dated everyone" (every partner type in the survey, weighted by how common it is), as N in 100 with model ranges and intervals; whether that is above or below average; the lowest-risk partner profiles and where the models disagree; which factors move the risk most for your profile, with their evidence grade; and the changes with the biggest predicted drop. It also charts [marital satisfaction by years married](reproducibility/satisfaction_by_duration/README.md) (GSS: 75% of newlyweds "very happy", 61–64% at 3–30 years; the same HCMST couples rated their relationship 0.14 points lower four years on) and shows that [dating length before marriage](reproducibility/dating_length/README.md) adds no held-out skill in HCMST (−0.12 points, 95% CI −0.90 to +0.72);
- batch scoring of a CSV of couples, entirely in the browser, with a downloadable results file;
- **compare options**: save the whole form as named options (up to 50), make "vary one thing" copies (age at the wedding, education, living together first, relationship quality, marriage happiness and more), and see every chosen lens side by side against your current plan: break-ups out of 100 couples like you with the model range, the change in points (with its spread across models) and as a ratio, how many models agree on the direction, a paired verdict from each model's own difference ("models agree", "most models agree", or "cannot tell apart" because the models disagree on the direction or the change is under 1 point), the population percentile and out-of-data flags, plus a small chart per lens; export JSON or CSV, or share a link that never carries sensitive answers;
- optional questions on what the surveys do not measure (destructive conflict, depression, alcohol problems, neuroticism, earlier cohabitations), applied as a clearly labelled, lower-confidence literature-based adjustment from verified published hazard ratios, next to official CDC/NCHS first-marriage disruption tables for the couple's characteristics.

A couple a lens cannot speak to gets no number from it. Scenarios can be shared as a link; the profiles stay in the link's `#` fragment and are never sent to a server.

## Can divorce be predicted before the wedding?

Modestly, and about equally well in five national surveys (about 280,000 first marriages in
NSFG 1995–2023, NLSY79, NLSY97, SIPP and Statistics Canada's GSS), using only facts knowable
on the wedding day: ages, education by the wedding, earlier children, living together first,
race/ethnicity, family of origin, religion raised and wedding year. Held out by person, the
best model ranks a couple who will divorce above one who will not 63–66% of the time
(C 0.634–0.664; AUC at 10 years 0.66–0.68), well calibrated within each survey. Trained on
one U.S. survey and tested on another, it keeps most of that ranking (C 0.61–0.66), but the
predicted level can be off by up to a third, and in Canada U.S. models predict 1.5–2.2 times
the divorce observed. A leakage audit found post-wedding information in four existing lenses
(spouse traits recorded after the wedding, a degree earned in the wedding year, region at
interview); removing it costs 0.1–1.1 points of held-out gain, and boosting's edge over an
additive model disappears. Details, tables and caveats:
[`reproducibility/prewedding/README.md`](reproducibility/prewedding/README.md).

## Published evidence

[`reproducibility/literature_evidence/EVIDENCE.md`](reproducibility/literature_evidence/EVIDENCE.md) collects 286 published estimates of divorce predictors (hazard and odds ratios, life-table probabilities, correlations, prediction accuracies), each with a verbatim quote mechanically checked against its source and a DOI checked at Crossref. It says which predictors are robust (age at marriage, education, premarital birth, premarital mental disorders, parents' marriage, neuroticism) and which are fragile (wedding spending, meeting online, interracial pairings). It also reads Gottman's "90%+ accuracy" claims against the base rate: they are in-sample, and predicting that nobody divorces scores 66–88% on the same samples (above one of the claims). The app uses 9 of the records, and only for factors the chosen lens does not already model. The NCHS life tables (Bramlett & Mosher 2002; Copen et al. 2012) are in `app/data/nchs_disruption_tables.json`.

## Evidence for decisions

To support decisions rather than only estimate risk, [`reproducibility/decision_evidence/`](reproducibility/decision_evidence/EVIDENCE.md) collects 46 verified records: randomised trials of relationship education and couple therapy, natural experiments on causes of divorce, and decision-aid standards. The Army PREP trial cut divorce at 2 years (7.6% vs 11.9%, driven by one site), but the two largest federal trials found no effect on staying together (Building Strong Families 57.4% vs 60.5%; Supporting Healthy Marriage 81.5% vs 81.5%). [FACTORS.md](reproducibility/decision_evidence/FACTORS.md) grades the evidence for every input the app can change: only relationship education, couple therapy (for satisfaction, not divorce) and income support have experimental evidence; age at marriage, education, cohabiting first, premarital births, religious attendance, debt and conflict rest on adjusted observational data. [DESIGN.md](reproducibility/decision_evidence/DESIGN.md) turns IPDAS decision-aid standards and risk-communication trials into a 14-item checklist for the app (absolute risks with time frames, one denominator, icon arrays, uncertainty, values clarification). The app-ready file is `app/data/decision_evidence.json`.

What each lens adds:

- **NLSY79 individuals** scores each partner from their own traits only, so the spouse's traits never enter, and averages the two rates. It is a second, independently fitted view of the same outcome. The app uses its currently-married population. The population that includes already-separated people scores 12.6% held out, but only because separated people are about to divorce.
- **NLSY79 couples** adds both spouses' schooling, degree, wages, work hours and earnings roles, own religious attendance, marriage order and duration. It cannot say anything reliable about couples under 35.
- **NSFG 2002** (two lenses: A's additive spline and tree, and B's classical family) covers what NLSY79 cannot: young couples and newly-weds, counted from the wedding day (wives married at 15–39, marriages up to 24 years). It adds ages on the wedding day, living together first, the husband's earlier marriage and children, race-ethnicity and the wife's family of origin. It knows nothing about income or schooling. It covers only a woman's first marriage, so a previously married wife gets no number. Both NSFG lenses also abstain when the couple's inputs taken together are more unusual than 99% of the surveyed marriages (B's Mahalanobis distance score; a 17-year-old bride and 50-year-old groom score 99.0, a 27/29 couple 21). Each input can still be inside its own range when this happens. Weddings after 2002 are scored as 2002 weddings, and the app says so.

- **NLSY79 first marriages** follow the same cohort from each wedding (not from age 35), rebuilt from the official public release. The rebuild first reproduced the couple models' published sample exactly (40,818 intervals, 5,735 people, 726 divorces) and their held-out gains within 0.003 points. Spouse traits add the most held-out skill (0.54 points), then age at marriage and wedding year (0.40) and youthful delinquency (0.19); schooling, AFQT, race, sex and income add about nothing once those are in.
- **GSS panels** are the cleanest prospective test of self-rated marriage happiness: 2.5% of "very happy" spouses were divorced or separated two years later, 5.5% of "pretty happy" and 23.6% of "not too happy" (9.4 times very happy). Being divorced before (2.51) and younger age also predict; same religion, attendance, degrees and sex frequency do not once the rest is held fixed. Because 60% say "very happy", 159 of the 203 dissolutions still came from very or pretty happy marriages.
- **HCMST** is the first lens with a rating of relationship quality, unmarried and same-sex couples, and two partners described by one respondent. Couples who rated their relationship poor or very poor broke up by 2017 at 60.3% against 14.3% for "excellent" (married couples: 47.1% against 4.6%); the per-period odds ratio is 13.7 (95% CI 7.1–29.5) for poor against excellent, holding the other inputs fixed. Not living together (3.56) and being unmarried (2.08) also raise the odds; each doubling of years together lowers them (0.66). Much of the pooled skill comes from separating new and unmarried couples from long marriages, so the married-only C-index (0.84) is the fairer guide for a married couple. The app lists this lens with its skill; scoring it needs a form field for relationship quality, which comes next.

- **NSFG 1995–2023** pools all eight NSFG cycles and more than doubles the 2002 lens's held-out skill: boosting adds +3.43 points of deviance gain (3.12–3.75) and +0.058 in C over the 2002 lens on the same folds; about a third comes from more data and the rest from new inputs (years of premarital sex, the wife's degree by the wedding, mother's education and age at first birth, religion raised, nativity). The riskiest tenth breaks up at 13.5 times the safest tenth's rate. Living together first, a 1.24 rate ratio in the 2002 lens, drops to 1.01 (0.97–1.06) once premarital sex and births are adjusted for and later cohorts are added. First marriages are getting more stable: 75.3% of 1964–79 weddings were intact at 5 years, 85.0% of 2010–23 weddings.
- **Los Angeles newlyweds** is the project's independent test of the claim behind Gottman's work, on a sample with real divorces and trained-observer codes of each couple's conflict discussion (positivity, negativity, effective communication). Held out, the observed codes add nothing once satisfaction, money and each spouse's own risk factors are known: −0.05 points of log-loss gain (95% CI −0.51 to +0.39), C-index +0.004 (−0.010 to +0.018). On their own they barely beat chance (C-index 0.53). Self-reported satisfaction (0.72 to 0.78 odds per SD) and a self-report hostility questionnaire (1.33 to 1.38 per SD) do predict. The null covers these three composite codes in this sample, not every observational system.
- **NLSY97** is the young cohort (born 1980–84) the NLSY79 lenses lack, with marriages through 2024. Given everything else, only couple-level facts add held-out skill: the partner's traits (0.32 points) and relationship history (0.23); test scores, behaviour, family background and economics each predict alone but add about nothing once those are in. Marrying at 34 rather than 20 goes with 0.55 times the rate, a spouse with 16+ years of school against 12 with 0.64, two or more earlier live-in partners with 1.33, and four years of living together before the wedding with 0.68.
- **Statistics Canada GSS Families** is the largest lens and the first outside the U.S. Living together first goes with a 1.81 times higher dissolution rate (95% CI 1.70–1.92), a premarital birth 1.54, marrying before 20 against 20–24 1.49 and marrying at 35 or older 0.60. Canadian women report first marriages lasting much longer than U.S. women do in NSFG 2002 (for weddings 1970–2002, 82.6% intact at 10 years against 63.8%). The surveys differ in mode, ages and years, so that gap is what each survey reports, not a clean country effect.

- **SIPP and ACS (Census)** add scale and money. SIPP's 176,303 retrospective first marriages give the same picture as NSFG with five times the marriages (74.0% still together at 10 years; 59.1% if married under 20, 86.2% with a bachelor's by the wedding, 82.5% for 2010s weddings against 70.8% for 1970s ones). Its one-year couple panel is the project's only wealth data: negative net worth goes with 24.4 separations per 1,000 a year against 4.9 at $1M+, renters 19.5 against owners 8.9, but once both spouses' demographics and education are known, earnings, income, net worth, debt and home ownership add nothing measurable held out (−0.79 points, 95% CI −2.82 to +0.91; 312 events, so a modest effect is not ruled out). ACS 2008–2024 gives one-year divorce incidence for 12,760 subgroups with replicate-weight CIs: 13.6 per 1,000 married people a year (2019, 2021–24), down from 19.3 in 2008; highest at 6–10 years of marriage (20.8) and for people married three or more times (23.6).

**More predictors: race detail, family of origin, religion, politics, occupation and wealth (2026-10-20).** Every lens was refitted with the richer inputs its data hold, and each addition was tested held out against the current model (paired person-bootstrap CIs; `reproducibility/*/extended_heldout.csv`). **None adds held-out skill whose interval excludes zero** in a prospective lens: GSS panels (finer family of origin −0.11 points; religion detail incl. prayer, strength, fundamentalism, denomination +0.10; politics +0.24; race detail −0.06; occupation −0.38; wealth −0.21), NLSY97 (all six blocks within ±0.12), NLSY79 (within ±0.05), NSFG 1995–2023 (within ±0.03), Statistics Canada family of origin (−0.00) and SIPP couples (occupations +0.11, −0.08 to +0.30; wealth components −0.35). Several still sort risk on their own and are listed with CIs in [`app/data/predictor_atlas.json`](app/data/predictor_atlas.json): in the Canadian 2017 cycle, parents who never married each other (1.59 times the separation rate) and parents who separated before 15 (1.30) matter, a parent's death does not (1.01); NSFG stepfamilies 1.52. ACS adds observed one-year divorce rates for every census race group and for Asian and Hispanic origin subgroups (Asian Indian 5.2 per 1,000, Black 23.6), and for 25 occupation groups, 20 industries, household income and home ownership — all measured after any divorce (`app/data/acs_occupation_divorce.json`). `app/data/race_coverage.json` says which race categories each lens can distinguish (NSFG and HCMST score Asian as "Other"; NLSY as "non-Black non-Hispanic"). The extended models ship in each bundle's `models_extended`, tested to 10<sup>-9</sup>, but are not scored until the form asks for their inputs.

No lens covers wealth prospectively beyond SIPP's one-year panel. The targets differ (legal divorce at ages 35–64 versus any break-up of a first marriage from the wedding day), so the app shows them side by side and never pools them.

**Lifelong, counting mortality.** The app runs workstream A's competing-risks module, using UN World Population Prospects 2024 U.S. death rates, once per model. Each year the divorce rate competes with the death of either partner.

- For the default 36/38 couple over 40 years, the NLSY79 couple models give: 31.0% of marriages end only when a partner dies (models 28.8–33.0%), 18.9% end in divorce (12.9–25.6%), and 50.0% are still going with both alive at year 40.
- For the 27/29 couple, NSFG's broader outcome gives 13.1% lifelong, 49.8% ending in a break-up, and 37.0% still going at year 40.

Long horizons lean on years outside each lens's data (13 of 40 for NLSY79, 16 of 40 for NSFG here), and population death rates overstate married people's mortality.

**Why the side-by-side view matters.** For the 27/29 couple below, both NLSY79 lenses extrapolate below their trained age of 35. They give 86.1% (couples) and 86.0% (individuals) no divorce by year 10. NSFG, inside its data, gives 74.8% (A's models) and 78.6% (B's family, models 75.8–82.2%). Part of the gap is the different outcome, which includes separation. Part is what extrapolation hides. For the default 36/38 couple, both NLSY79 lenses are inside their data and close: 88.6% (couples, models 84.9–92.4%) and 89.6% (individuals, 88.8–90.6%). More lenses (HCMST, GSS, small relationship-attitude datasets) are planned in [`app/ROADMAP.md`](app/ROADMAP.md).

**A young couple on the NSFG lens.** Take a woman of 27 and a man of 29, both White, marrying now after living together, where she grew up with both parents and he has no earlier marriage or children. The two models give 74.8% no break-up by year 10 (range 74.2–75.4%). The survey's own weighted Kaplan–Meier curve, with no model, shows 63.8% of all first marriages intact at 10 years and 70.9% where the wife married at 25–29. Two of the couple-level what-ifs move the additive model up: not living together first (+4.3 points) and marrying five years later (+3.8). The tree does not split on either, so the average is about +2 and only 1 of 2 models agrees. These are associations; couples who cohabit or marry young differ in ways the survey does not measure.

**How much the suggestions mean** (the app's default couple: a 36-year-old woman with a bachelor's degree and a 38-year-old man with some college, newly married): the five models put the 10-year chance of no divorce at 88.6% (range 84.9–92.4%). All 150 partner profiles in the search have a model range that overlaps the top profile's (91.6%; lowest 84.0%), so the models cannot separate them, and on their own the models put two different profiles first. No change to the woman's searched traits raises the estimate. The largest change for the man, completing a professional degree, adds 2.6 points on average, but the models range from 0.0 to 4.6 points and only 3 of 5 move up (the trees do not split on it). Suggestions are associations in survey data, not causes. The tiles count divorces only, as if both partners live.

## Flexible models and a pooled multi-survey model

Two 2026-10-20 tests of "more flexible models" and "pool every survey":

- **Flexible models** ([flexible_models/](flexible_models/README.md)): tuned LightGBM,
  gradient boosting, forests, extra trees, an EBM, an elastic-net GLM with pairwise
  interactions, and a stack. On five lenses, none beat the lens's best additive model with a
  95% interval above zero. Tree models overfit the small surveys: calibration slopes ran
  0.55–0.81. Learning curves flatten after about three-quarters of the data, and 2–8 inputs
  carry most of the skill.
- **Pooled multi-survey Bayesian hazard model**
  ([reproducibility/unified_model/](reproducibility/unified_model/README.md)): nine surveys,
  321,121 units and 44 inputs, with chained-equation imputation of inputs a survey never asked.
  Held out by person, it beats the single-survey models in HCMST (+4.8 points, 1.9–7.8) and the
  LA newlyweds (+1.8, 0.6–3.2), and loses slightly in NSFG, NLSY79 and Canada. It is not in the
  calculator yet.

## Research limits

Annual person-time incidence, ACS past-year report proportions, current divorced prevalence and retrospective dissolution are distinct targets. Do not pool their rows or convert annual rates directly into lifetime risk. NLSY79 is an older cohort, with survivor/attrition selection and approximate respondent-cluster uncertainty rather than complete survey-design variance. Most results are exploratory; the duration adjacent-band tests use Holm adjustment. Tree splits are predictive partitions, not confirmed biological or social thresholds. These models do not establish causal effects, fidelity, personal compatibility or a forecast for an unmarried 27-year-old. Wealth/business income and spouse belief histories are not interchangeable with wages or own attendance.

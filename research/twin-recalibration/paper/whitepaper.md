# Twin recalibration: how often, and how hard, to re-identify a twin of a drifting plant

*Stylised: scalar plant `x' = a x + b_t u + w`, `w ~ N(0,σ²)`, `a=0.9, q=1, r=0.1, σ²=1`, gain `b=1` at the start of each cycle and a random walk `b_t = b_{t−1} + η`, `η ~ N(0,q_d)`. The twin is frozen between recalibrations. A recalibration is an open-loop experiment of `N` steps with a Rademacher input `u = ±√v` (so `Σu² = Nv` exactly) followed by least squares on `x' − a x = b u + w`; the controller then plays the certainty-equivalent gain `k*(b̂)` for `L` steps. Excess cost is measured against a clairvoyant controller that sees `b_t`. Pure Python; see `tests/` and `experiments/results.txt`.*

**Builds on** (not affiliated with or endorsed by): `twin-upkeep`, `twin-transfer` and `randomized-twin` in this repository, which supply the LQ model and the regret coefficient. Related to the digital-twin and sim-to-real themes of the HIRO group (manipulation workcells) and RECUV / Autonomous Systems IRT (UAV and field-robot simulation), as a question about how a twin's parameters should be maintained; it reproduces none of their results.

## Question
`twin-upkeep` shows that a twin filtering the controller's own data tracks a drifting gain cheaply. Many twins are not maintained that way: a simulator is re-fitted from a dedicated experiment (a calibration run, a system-identification sweep) that takes the plant off production. How often should that be done, how long should each experiment be, what happens if the drift rate is misjudged, and what does this way of maintaining a twin cost relative to always-on filtering?

## Results
1. **A closed-form schedule.** Let `ρ = ½ J_kk (dk*/db)² = 0.5656` be the second-order excess cost per unit variance of the gain error (checked against the exact Gaussian average to 3% in `tests/`), and `c` the excess cost of one experiment step over the clairvoyant stage cost. In the idealised model (linear experiment cost, drift inside the window neglected, `L ≫ N`) the average excess per step is `Nc/T + ρσ²/(Nv) + ρq_d(T−1)/2`, minimised at `T* = (4cσ²/(vρq_d²))^{1/3}`, `N* = √(ρσ²T*/(vc))`, giving `3A/√T* − ρq_d/2` with `A = √(cρσ²/v)`. For `v=1` an open-loop step costs `c = c₀ + γv = 4.189 + 5.363 = 9.552` (exact steady state; `c₀` is the price of leaving the loop open). At `q_d = 10⁻⁶ … 10⁻³` the formula gives `T* = 40728, 8775, 1890, 407`, `N* = 49.1, 22.8, 10.6, 4.9` and excess `0.0346, 0.0744, 0.160, 0.345`; an integer optimiser of the same idealised cost finds `N,T = (49, 40660), (23, 8803), (11, 1922), (5, 409)` with excess `0.0345, 0.0743, 0.160, 0.342` (within 1.0% in every case).
2. **Equal thirds.** At the optimum, experiment downtime `Nc/T`, estimation-noise regret `ρσ²/(Nv)` and drift regret `ρq_dT/2` are equal (each `A/√T*`; `0.0115 / 0.0535 / 0.1152` at `q_d = 10⁻⁶ / 10⁻⁴ / 10⁻³`, all three columns identical to five digits). Total excess is therefore three times any one of them, and any measured imbalance tells which knob to turn.
3. **Cube-root laws.** Excess `∝ q_d^{1/3}`, `T* ∝ q_d^{−2/3}`, `N* ∝ q_d^{−1/3}`: fitted slopes of the integer optimiser over `q_d = 10⁻⁷ … 10⁻⁴` are `0.333`, `−0.664`, `−0.328`. Excess also scales as `c^{1/3}` (fitted slope `0.336` over `c = 0.96 … 28.7`): a 10× cheaper experiment lowers the excess only 2.17× (`0.0341` vs `0.0743`), and a longer interval `T*` and shorter `N*` are the compensation. The exponent differs from the Young/Daly `√` law for checkpointing because here regret grows *linearly* with the age of the estimate, so the cost of waiting is quadratic in time.
4. **Robustness.** Recalibrating every `sT*` (with `N` re-optimised) costs `(2s^{−1/2} + s)/3` times the optimum, matched by direct evaluation to three digits (`s = 0.25, 0.5, 2, 4`: `1.417, 1.109, 1.138, 1.667`). Since `T* ∝ q_d^{−2/3}`, misjudging the drift by 4× moves `T` by 2.52× and costs at most 26% (19% on the other side): the schedule needs the drift rate to within a factor of a few, not precisely.
5. **Experiment amplitude.** Because `c = c₀ + γv`, information per unit cost improves with amplitude and saturates at `c/v → γ = 5.36`: `c/v = 22.1, 9.55, 6.41, 5.63` and idealised excess `0.0985, 0.0744, 0.0652, 0.0624` at `v = 0.25, 1, 4, 16` (`q_d=10⁻⁵`). At `v = 16` the formula gives `N* = 1.7`, outside its validity, and a real experiment would also hit actuator limits; the gain from amplitude is real but capped at about 16% between `v = 1` and `16`.
6. **Full model.** Counting the exact experiment cost (the state variance starts low and the experiment's inflated variance leaves a transient after control resumes) and the gain drift inside the window (`q_d(N−1)(2N−1)/(6N)`, exact for a random walk), the optimum moves to shorter cycles and lower cost: `T = 38450, 7537, 1456, 274` with excess `0.0335, 0.0695, 0.1385, 0.266` (the idealised formula overstates the cost by 3–30%, and its schedule evaluated in the full model costs at most 4.6% more than the full-model optimum).
7. **The second-order optimum is unsafe.** The regret law is second order, but a certainty-equivalent gain from an estimate below `b̂ = 0.214` destabilises the plant (`b·k*(b̂) ≥ 1+a`), and a frozen gain also fails once the true gain drifts past `2.31`. The per-cycle failure probability, integrating a Gaussian estimate over the cliff and taking the drift's first-passage probability by the reflection principle, is `0.66%, 5.3%, 14.8%` at the full-model optima for `q_d = 10⁻⁵, 10⁻⁴, 10⁻³`; simulated (3 seeds × 1500 cycles): `0.6%, 3.4%, 7.9%` (the analytic value is an upper bound for the discrete walk). Demanding failure probability `≤ 10⁻³` per cycle forces longer experiments (`N = 36, 38, 36`) and, at fast drift, much shorter cycles (`T = 6385, 683, 69`), and raises the model's excess to `0.0812, 0.505, 4.42`: `1.2×, 3.6×, 16×` the unconstrained optimum. At `q_d = 10⁻³` the safe schedule spends over half of every cycle in experiments. Simulated failures at the safe schedules: `0.04%, 0.02%, 0%`.
8. **Simulation vs model at the safe schedule** (6 seeds, mean over cycles that did not diverge): `0.0916` vs `0.0812` (`q_d=10⁻⁵`, all seeds `0.085–0.100`, so the model is 11% low), `4.63` vs `4.42` (`10⁻³`, +5%), and for `q_d = 10⁻⁴` four of six seeds give `0.507–0.528` against `0.505` while two contain a single near-cliff cycle with a huge cost (`953`, `1.72`), so the mean is dominated by the tail: the expected excess of a Gaussian estimate is formally infinite, and a failure-probability constraint bounds only the diverging cycles, not the near-cliff ones. Treat the quoted excess as the typical (median-cycle) cost.
9. **Price of not using operating data.** Against the always-on Kalman twin of `twin-upkeep` on the same plant (regret `0.00069, 0.00217, 0.00688, 0.0219` at `q_d = 10⁻⁶ … 10⁻³`), the idealised batch schedule is `50×, 34×, 23×, 16×` worse and the safe one `49×, 37×, 73×, 202×`. The idealised ratio scales as `q_d^{1/3−1/2}`: fitted slope `−0.169` (law `−1/6`), so batch recalibration is relatively *worst* for slowly drifting plants; the safe ratio is not a power law because stability, not statistical efficiency, binds at fast drift. The dominant cost is the price of an open-loop experiment (`c = 9.55` per step against a stage cost of `1.07`).

## Limitations
One parameter (`b`), scalar state, `a` and the noise variance known, random-walk drift with the gain re-centred at `b=1` each cycle (simulated cycles are i.i.d. by construction; without re-centring the random walk leaves the neighbourhood). Second-order regret; the third-order and cliff behaviour is captured only through the failure probability, and the mean excess of a Gaussian estimate is infinite, so all costs are for cycles that did not diverge. Open-loop Rademacher experiments only: experiments with dither on top of the controller (much cheaper per step) are the bridge to `twin-upkeep` and are not modelled. Experiments of the twin's *simulator* (as opposed to the plant) cost nothing here. The equal-thirds law and cube-root exponents hold for the idealised objective; in the full model the ratios drift by up to 30%. The formula for `N*` is not meaningful below `N ≈ 5`. Simulations are seeded pure Python; the cost-relevant tail is heavy, so few seeds give noisy means (`q_d = 10⁻⁴`).

## Relevance
For a twin re-fitted from dedicated experiments, choose the interval from the drift rate with the `q_d^{−2/3}` law, spend equal effort on the three terms, and do not fine-tune: a factor-4 error in the drift rate costs at most a quarter. But size the schedule from the stability margin, not the second-order optimum, when the drift is fast: the safe schedule can cost an order of magnitude more, and it is dominated by an always-on filter by one to two orders of magnitude. The practical lesson for digital-twin maintenance is to make experiments cheap (in-loop dither, not downtime) before making them frequent.

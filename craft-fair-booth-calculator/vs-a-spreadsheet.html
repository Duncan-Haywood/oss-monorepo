Craft fair booth calculator vs a spreadsheet or gut math

Most makers already do a version of this in their head before they apply: sales minus the booth fee. It is fast, and it is wrong in the same direction every time, because it treats the booth fee as the only cost and gross sales as if every dollar of them were kept.

The gut check, and what it leaves out

One event, two answers

Take this calculator's own default event: a $250 booth, $25 jury fee, a 90-mile round trip, $1,500 in expected sales, 30% materials, and a mixed cash/card till. The gut check says:

Sales minus booth fee$1,500 − $250 = $1,250 "profit"

Run the same event through every cost actually paid, and the answer looks nothing like that:

Fixed cost paid before any sale (booth, jury fee, mileage, parking, display)$370.30
Materials and card fees out of $1,500 in sales$854.66
Cash left over$645.34
26 committed hours (prep, travel, setup/teardown, selling) at $25/hr$650.00
Left after paying yourself−$4.66

The day still clears its bills by a wide margin, which is exactly the kind of result a "sales minus booth fee" gut check reads as a clear win. Once the maker's own time is priced at a modest $25 an hour, the same day comes out four dollars and change short of that rate. Neither number is wrong — they answer different questions — but only one of them is the one a spreadsheet line usually shows.

Where a spreadsheet gets unsold stock backwards

The other place a quick spreadsheet goes wrong is unsold stock, and it goes wrong in either direction. Some makers ignore it entirely, which silently overstates how much the event actually cost to attend if a large share of the table came home. Others swing the other way and charge the whole table's materials against one weekend, which is how a perfectly fine market gets recorded as a loss — the candles that did not sell are still in the van, not gone. This calculator asks what you are bringing at retail only to report the break-even as a sell-through percentage, so you can see what share of the table has to move without charging the unsold remainder against the day at all.

Common questions

What does "sales minus booth fee" get wrong?

It counts gross sales as if every dollar were kept. In reality, materials already spent on what sold and the card processor's cut both come out of that dollar before anything is left, and the booth fee is only one of several costs paid before a single sale — the jury fee, mileage, lodging, parking, and display all belong in the same bucket. A quick mental subtraction misses all of that, and it counts none of the maker's own hours at all.

Why does a spreadsheet total still miss the real answer?

A one-line spreadsheet usually stops at cash left over, which answers whether the day covered its bills but not whether it paid the maker anything for their time. On this calculator's own default event, cash left over is $645.34 — a day that looks clearly profitable — but once prep, travel, setup, teardown, and selling hours are valued at even a modest $25/hour, the day actually falls $4.66 short of that rate. A spreadsheet that never enters an hourly figure cannot show that.

Why does a per-transaction card fee matter more than the percentage suggests?

A processor's fixed fee is charged per sale, not per dollar, so it only becomes a rate once divided by what a typical sale is worth. Ten cents on a $6 sticker is a much bigger bite than ten cents on an $80 print, and a headline "2.6% + 10c" hides that difference completely — a spreadsheet with one blended fee percentage cannot see it either, unless it is built to divide the fixed fee by the average order value the way this calculator does.

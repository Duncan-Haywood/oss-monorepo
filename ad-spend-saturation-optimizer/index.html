How it is calculated

Curves: each channel's weekly points (spend, revenue) are fitted by least squares with two shapes: a saturating exponential, a(1 - e^(-s/k)), and an S-shaped Hill curve, a s^h / (k^h + s^h), where the first dollars do less than the later ones. The S-shape is used only when it improves small-sample AIC by more than 2. Marginal return is the slope of the curve at the average of the last eight weeks' spend. Best split: an exact dynamic programme over a 400-step grid of the same total, so S-shaped curves are handled correctly; each channel stays between 0.25x and 2x its current spend and under 1.25x its highest week. Ranges: 100 seeded resamples of your own weeks. Profit view: margin times fitted revenue minus spend, along the fitted frontier.

Not modelled: carry-over beyond one decay rate per channel (a single geometric rate is fitted when it clearly helps), seasonality, promotions and price changes, overlap or halo between channels, attribution error (revenue is whatever your file credits to a channel, often platform-reported and overstated), incrementality, and any channel with under eight weeks or almost constant spend, which is left where it is. The resampled ranges cover noise in the fit, not a wrong curve shape. Unverified: checked against simulated data with known curves, an analytic optimum and a brute-force search in unit tests, not against a published marketing dataset.

Common questions

What is marginal ROAS and why does it matter more than average ROAS?

Average ROAS is total revenue divided by total spend. Marginal ROAS is what the next dollar earns. Because ad channels saturate, the marginal figure is always lower than the average and is the one that tells you where to put more money. A channel with a 3.0 average ROAS can have a marginal ROAS under 1.0 if it is already spending near its ceiling.

How does the tool find the best split of my budget?

For each channel it fits a diminishing-returns curve to your own weekly spend and revenue, then searches every split of the same total budget with an exact dynamic programme and keeps the one with the highest total fitted revenue. Each channel is limited to between a quarter and double its current spend, and never beyond 1.25 times its highest week, so the advice stays near spend you have actually tried.

What data do I need?

One row per date and channel with the date, the channel name, the spend and the revenue credited to that channel. Daily or weekly rows both work; daily rows are added up into weeks. Each channel needs at least eight weeks, and spend has to have varied. A channel whose spend never changed cannot show what happens when it does.

How accurate is it?

The tool tests itself. It refits on your data up to some weeks before the end, predicts revenue in the final weeks from the spend that really happened, and compares that with a flat ROAS line. If the curve does not beat the flat line, or the error is large, something other than spend, such as seasonality or promotions, is driving your revenue and the split should be tried on a small share of budget first. The resampled ranges show noise in the fit, not whether the curve shape is right.

What is it not good at?

It models carry-over from one week to the next as a single decay rate, and ignores seasonality, promotions, price changes, overlap between channels and brand effects. It uses whatever revenue your file credits to each channel, which is often platform-reported and overstated and is not proof that the ad caused the sale. It cannot see what happens at spend levels you have never run.

What does the profit view mean?

You enter your gross margin. Contribution is margin times fitted revenue minus ad spend. The tool reports the weekly total where that peaks along the fitted curves. It ignores everything except attributed revenue and ad spend, and the peak is usually flat, so treat it as a range to test rather than a target.

Merchant Feed Check vs. eyeballing the feed in a spreadsheet

Opening the feed in a spreadsheet and scanning it row by row is the normal first check before a sync, and it works well for the obvious case — a screaming promotional title, a repeated SKU. Two of the most common documented disapproval reasons are specifically the kind that look like ordinary data on a visual scan, because the value that fails is the same length and shape as every valid value around it.

Why the checksum and the format rule are the quiet ones

A GTIN is 8, 12, 13, or 14 digits. A single mistyped or transposed digit produces another number of the exact same length, made entirely of digits — it does not look different from a valid one in the next cell. Telling them apart means running the GS1 check-digit calculation on every row: weight each digit of the payload 3 and 1 alternating from the digit immediately left of the check digit, sum the weighted digits, and the check digit must equal (10 - sum mod 10) mod 10. That is not a check anyone runs by hand across a feed of any real size.

The price format rule is quieter still, because the failure is an absence rather than a wrong value. Google's and Meta's shared spec requires NUMBER CURRENCY — 19.99 USD, not 19.99 or $19.99. A spreadsheet applying its own currency number format renders 19.99, $19.99, and 19.99 USD as visually similar cells; nothing on screen signals that the underlying string never carried the required three-letter code.

Common questions

Why doesn't scanning the feed by eye catch a bad GTIN?

A GTIN is a 13-digit (or 8/12/14-digit) number, and a transposed or mistyped digit produces another number of the exact same length made entirely of digits -- it looks identical to a valid one sitting in the next cell. Telling the two apart means running the GS1 check-digit calculation (weight each digit 3/1 alternating from the right, sum, and the check digit must equal (10 - sum mod 10) mod 10), which nobody does by eye on a 2,000-row feed. Google and Meta both run this calculation and reject the mismatch as a bad or missing product identifier.

Why doesn't scanning the feed by eye catch a missing currency code?

The published price format is "NUMBER CURRENCY", e.g. "19.99 USD" -- but a bare "19.99" reads as a completely ordinary price to anyone scanning the column, especially in a spreadsheet where every other price cell is formatted as currency by the spreadsheet software itself, hiding that the underlying feed value never carries the required currency code. The rejection only shows up once the feed is parsed against the spec's exact regular-expression shape, not from looking at the rendered column.

What does a spreadsheet scan catch that this checker doesn't add much to?

A title in all caps with "50% OFF" and "FREE SHIPPING" in it, or the same SKU appearing on two rows, both look wrong on sight -- this checker flags the same two rows for the same reasons, so a careful reviewer scanning for those specific shapes was already going to catch them. The value here is narrow: two specific, well-documented rejection reasons that are constructed to look like ordinary data on a visual scan, checked mechanically on every row instead of on however many rows get a close look.

Does this replace reviewing the feed before it syncs?

No. It is a deterministic, row-by-row check against Google's and Meta's own published product-data specification, not a review of whether the products, pricing, or category placement make business sense. A reviewer scanning the feed still catches the obviously wrong-looking rows and anything domain-specific this tool has no rule for; this checker adds the two specific, quiet failure modes a visual scan is structurally unlikely to catch.

Nothing here says a spreadsheet review is a bad first check — it is exactly right for the case it is good at. This page describes two specific, narrow failure modes that are constructed to look like ordinary data, not a fault in reviewers.

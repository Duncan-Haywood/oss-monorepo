    What this does not cover

    • Federal tax only. State treatment of conversions varies and is not modelled.
    • Simplified Form 8606. Line 4 (contributions made after year-end and counted for the prior year), inherited IRAs, and prior-year distributions of basis are not modelled.

    Common questions

    What is the pro-rata rule for a backdoor Roth IRA?

    The IRS treats all of your traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRAs as one pot, measured on December 31. When you convert to Roth, the tax-free share of the conversion is your total after-tax basis divided by the total value of that pot plus the amount converted. You cannot pick only the after-tax dollars to convert.

    How do I avoid the pro-rata rule?

    Move your pre-tax traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA money into an employer 401(k) or a Solo 401(k) that accepts roll-ins before December 31. Money in a 401(k) is not counted on Form 8606, so the ratio goes to 100% and the backdoor conversion is tax-free. Only pre-tax money can be rolled in; the plan must accept it.

    Does the pro-rata rule count my 401(k) or my Roth IRA?

    No. Only traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRAs are counted, on December 31 of the conversion year. Balances in an employer 401(k), 403(b), or any Roth account are not part of the calculation.

    Which line of Form 8606 is the taxable amount?

    Part I of Form 8606 divides your total basis by your year-end IRA value plus distributions plus the conversion. Multiplying the conversion by that ratio gives the nontaxable part; the rest is taxable and flows to your return.

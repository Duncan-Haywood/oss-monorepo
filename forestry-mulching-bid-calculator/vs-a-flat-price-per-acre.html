This calculator vs. a flat price per acre

“$X an acre” is not a price for a mulching job — it is a price for one specific density, quietly applied to every lot that calls in.

What a flat price per acre is

A flat rate per acre is the fastest way to quote forestry mulching or land clearing: multiply the acreage by a number and read off a price. It is the number a landowner holding a competitor's flyer will call and ask you to match, whatever is actually standing on their lot.

The number behind it was set for one density class and then reused for every lot afterward, whether or not the next job looks anything like the one the rate was built on.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the acreage, the mulching head's width, a density class (light brush, medium brush, heavy brush, or heavy timber, or your own timed advance speed and pass count), terrain, machine cost inputs, and mobilization, and it computes hours first, from head width, advance speed, and pass count, then reports the per-acre price last — as the quotient it actually is — for this job, not a generic one.

What a flat price per acre doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same $/acre on a fence line growing back in and on heavy understory with stems up to 12 inches, even though the second takes more than nine times as many machine-hours per acre to clear. Computes acres per machine-hour from head width, advance speed, and passes for the density actually on the ground, so a denser job costs more per acre than a lighter one.
Has no line for tooth wear, ownership cost per billable hour, or mobilization, regardless of how far the machine has to travel or how many hours a month it actually bills. Prices operator wage, fuel, tooth wear, maintenance, and ownership separately, then adds mobilization as its own line because it does not scale with acreage.
Gives the estimator a number to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the break-even price per acre and the dollar gap against a flat per-acre rate for each density, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before you commit to it.

On its own default density (medium brush) that rate still clears a real margin. Run your own lot's density and acreage in the calculator to see where it falls.

A flat per-acre rate is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is carried from the job it was set on to a denser one without checking, and nothing about a flat number tells you when that has happened. This calculator does not know your local market; it replaces the arithmetic a flat rate skips.

Common questions

Why does a flat price per acre get a mulching bid wrong?

Because the cost driver is the production rate -- acres cut per machine-hour -- and that rate is set by what is standing on the ground, not by the acreage. Light brush under 2 inches clears roughly nine times faster than heavy understory with stems up to 12 inches on this calculator's own defaults, so the same flat rate that is profitable on a light job can lose money outright on a dense one, and a per-acre number has no way to say which job it is looking at.

What does a flat price per acre usually leave out entirely?

Stem density and size. A quote of dollars per acre treats a fence line growing back in the same as heavy understory with stems up to 12 inches, when the second takes more than nine times as many machine-hours to clear as the first on the same acreage -- production rate, not acreage, is what the machine is actually paid for.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

On this calculator's own shipped defaults, a flat $1,000-an-acre quote on a 3-acre lot earns $2,059.29 on light brush -- a 68.6% margin -- but loses $508.70 on heavy brush and $3,810.40 on heavy understory with stems up to 12 inches, a -127.0% margin against a job whose own break-even cost is $2,270.13 an acre.

Is a flat price per acre ever the right way to quote?

For a narrow band of jobs at nearly the same density, a per-acre rate can land close. The risk is carrying the same rate onto a denser job without re-checking it, because the rate does not know what is standing on the next lot and neither does the estimator until density and stem size are actually spelled out.

Does this calculator know what materials or labor cost in my market?

No. It has no data on local labor, fuel, or equipment rates. It takes the acreage, the mulching head's width, the advance speed and passes for the density you describe, and your own wage, fuel, tooth, maintenance, and ownership costs, and turns that into a price for this job -- the arithmetic a flat per-acre rate skips, not a market survey.

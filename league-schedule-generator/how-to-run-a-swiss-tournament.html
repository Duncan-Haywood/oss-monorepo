How to run a Swiss-system tournament, with a worked example

A Swiss tournament is for a club night or a weekend event with more players than there is time for a round robin. Nobody is knocked out, everyone plays every round, and each round pairs people who are doing about equally well. Chess clubs, darts leagues, board-game and card-game events use it.

A worked example

Six invented players, seeded in the order they signed up. Six players need 3 rounds. Round 1 has no scores yet, so the seed order decides: the top half plays the bottom half.

Round 1WhiteBlack
Board 1AnaDee
Board 2BenEli
Board 3CyFay

Suppose Ana, Ben and Fay win. The three winners now have 1 point and the three losers 0, so round 2 pairs winners with winners and losers with losers, with nobody meeting a previous opponent:

Round 2WhiteBlack
Board 1AnaBen
Board 2FayDee
Board 3EliCy

Fay, on 1 point, plays Dee, on 0, because the other two 1-point players already meet each other: the tool shows that as a "floater" and says so rather than hiding it. Colours alternate, so nobody is white three times running. The pairing is checked by a separate validator before it is shown.

Questions

How many rounds does a Swiss tournament need?

Enough that one player can be clearly ahead: the next whole number above log base 2 of the player count. Six or eight players need 3 rounds, sixteen need 4. More rounds sort the middle of the table more finely, but the top places are usually settled by this number.

How are players paired in a Swiss round?

Everyone is sorted by score, then players with the same score are paired top half against bottom half, so the strongest of a group meets the weakest of that group. Nobody plays the same opponent twice. In round 1 nobody has a score yet, so the seed order decides.

What happens with an odd number of players?

One player gets a bye each round and is scored as a win. The bye goes to the lowest-ranked player who has not had one yet, so nobody gets two byes before everyone has had one.

How are ties in the standings broken?

By Buchholz: the sum of your opponents' scores. Beating players who went on to do well is worth more than beating players who lost everything. Players still level after that keep their seed order.

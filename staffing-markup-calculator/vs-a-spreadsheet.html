Staffing markup calculator vs. a spreadsheet

A spreadsheet can price a placement's real margin correctly. It has to carry a formula for every burden line, cap FUTA and SUTA at their wage base by hand, and get rebuilt correctly every time a rate or a comp code changes -- or it quietly reports markup / (1 + markup) instead, which is only right when the pay rate is the whole cost.

Price a real placement →

A spreadsheet is how most independent agencies price a placement today, and there is nothing wrong with the arithmetic itself: markup on the pay rate, burden as a percentage, margin as a formula. The formula most desks actually use is markup / (1 + markup) — 45% markup becomes a 31% margin — and that identity is exactly right when the number being marked up is the whole cost of the placement. It isn't. FICA, federal and state unemployment tax, workers' compensation, PTO accrual, benefits, onboarding and payroll admin all sit outside the pay rate, and a one-line formula has no way to know that.

This calculator prices the loaded cost directly: it charges FUTA and SUTA against their wage base rather than as a flat percentage, so a short assignment carries the effect on its own; it applies the workers' comp class rate to the comp-eligible payroll, excluding the overtime premium where the state allows it; and it prices the cost of funding payroll for the days between paying workers weekly and collecting the invoice on the client's actual terms. It reports the real gross margin next to the margin the markup implies, and the break-even and target-margin markups directly — no formula to write or re-check.

Common questions

Can a spreadsheet price a staffing placement's real margin?

It can, but the one-line formula most desks use — markup / (1 + markup) — only gives the real gross margin when the pay rate is the whole cost being marked up. In staffing it isn't: FICA, FUTA, SUTA, workers' comp, PTO, benefits, onboarding and payroll admin all sit outside the pay rate and inside the real cost. A spreadsheet can price that correctly too, but it has to carry a formula for each of those lines, including capping FUTA and SUTA at their wage bases rather than charging them as a flat percentage, and that formula has to be rebuilt correctly every time a rate, a comp code, or a state changes.

Why does the usual markup-to-margin formula overstate the real margin?

Because it assumes the pay rate is the only cost in the placement. Employer burden — FICA, unemployment tax, workers' comp, PTO accrual, benefits, screening and payroll admin — is real cost that sits outside the pay rate, so it is invisible to markup / (1 + markup) entirely. A 45% markup implies a 31% margin by that formula; once burden, the cost of funding payroll on the client's terms, and bad debt are counted, the placement can earn well under half of that, or lose money outright on a heavy workers' comp class code.

What does a hand-built spreadsheet have to get right that this calculator does automatically?

Three things that are each easy to get wrong once and never notice: charging FUTA and SUTA against their wage base with min(wages, wageBase) rather than as a flat percentage on every dollar, so a short assignment doesn't silently overcharge or undercharge the unemployment line; excluding the overtime premium from workers' comp payroll where the state allows it, rather than charging comp on the full 1.5x wage; and pricing the cost of funding payroll for the days between paying workers weekly and collecting the invoice on the client's actual terms, which a markup-only sheet usually omits altogether.

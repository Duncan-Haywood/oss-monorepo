This calculator vs. a flat rate per square foot

"$3.50 a square foot" is not a price for a driveway — it is a price for one specific method, thickness, and scope, quietly applied to every driveway that walks in the door.

What a flat per-foot rate is

A flat rate is the fastest way to quote: multiply the paved square footage by a number and read off a price. Homeowners shop it because it is easy to compare between bids, and crews use it because it needs no takeoff. It is also the rate a customer holding a postcard or a competitor's website will ask you to match.

The number behind it was set for one job — one method, one thickness, no apron, no failed base, minimal sawcut — and then reused for every driveway afterward, whether or not the next driveway looks anything like the one the rate was built on.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the driveway's square footage, thickness, and paving method (overlay, mill-and-overlay, or full-depth), the street apron, a failed-base patch, the sawcut, and your loaded labor and overhead. It converts area and thickness into mix tons by method, adds milling, demo, base-gravel, and sawcut hours separately, and returns a walk-away price below which the job loses money — for this driveway, not a generic one.

What a flat per-foot rate doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same $/sq ft whether the job is an 800 sq ft 2-inch overlay or a 1,000 sq ft 3-inch full-depth replacement — a several-times difference in mix tons for a similar floor area. Converts each method's area and thickness into compacted mix tons from the asphalt's own density, so the tonnage tracks the job actually described, not the floor plan alone.
Prices "the driveway" as one paved area, with no separate line for the street apron, a failed-base patch, or the sawcut at the garage. Times the apron at its own thickness, failed base at gravel tons and demo hours, and sawcut at hours per lineal foot, so that scope shows up instead of being absorbed into the paved area or skipped.
Assumes sound existing asphalt underneath, so a job needing a failed-base patch gets quoted the same as one that does not. Adds demo, dump, and gravel-base cost only when that scope is turned on, so the two jobs price differently — about $4,700 walk-away versus a $3,780 flat quote on the same 1,080 sq ft of paved area once a 200 sq ft failed base and an 80 sq ft apron are real.
Bakes payroll burden into a round "labor rate" nobody re-derives, so the rate quietly drifts from the crew's real loaded cost. Loads the entered hourly wage by the entered payroll-burden percentage every time, so the labor cost tracks the actual burden rather than a memorized rate.
Gives the crew a price to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the walk-away price, the effective hourly rate, and a three-method comparison on the same driveway, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before the crew commits to it.

A flat rate is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is applied outside the one job it was built for, and nothing about a postcard rate tells you when that has happened. This calculator does not replace judgment about your market; it replaces the arithmetic a flat rate skips.

Common questions

Why does a flat rate per square foot get asphalt paving wrong?

Because mix tons come from thickness and method, not from the floor area alone. An 800 square foot overlay at 2 inches needs a fraction of the hot-mix a 1,000 square foot full-depth job at 3 inches needs, before the apron or a failed base is even counted. A flat rate charges both the same and is wrong on one of them every time.

What does a flat rate usually leave out entirely?

The street apron, a failed base, and the sawcut. A 4-inch apron at the street is its own thicker lift, a failed base means demo, dump, and gravel before a single ton of mix goes down, and a clean sawcut edge is its own hour stack. A rate built around "square feet of driveway" has nowhere to put any of that.

What happens when the base has failed?

A flat overlay rate assumes sound existing asphalt underneath. On a real 1,000 square foot job with a 200 square foot failed-base patch and an 80 square foot apron, the walk-away cost this calculator returns is about $4,700 — while a $3.50-a-square-foot quote on that same paved area comes to about $3,780. Quoting the flat rate on that driveway loses money before the crew leaves the yard.

Is a flat rate ever the right way to quote?

For a narrow band of near-identical jobs — same method, same thickness, no apron, no failed base, minimal sawcut — a flat rate can land close. The risk is applying it outside that band without noticing, because the postcard rate does not know which band a given driveway is in and neither does the crew until they measure it.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local rates, mix prices, or what competitors charge. It takes the wage, mix cost, and overhead you enter and turns them into a walk-away price for the method, area, and scope you describe — the arithmetic a flat rate skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat rate per square foot is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works until the job stops matching the one the rate was set on. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it. The $3,780 and $4,700 figures above are this calculator's own arithmetic on the example job described in its README, not a claim about any specific contractor's real costs.

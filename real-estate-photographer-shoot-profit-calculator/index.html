    The model, and what it leaves out

    • Hours worked = round-trip drive + on-site time + editing time + admin, plus the expected extra time from add-ons at your take rates.
    • Profit = package fee + expected add-on fees − mileage − gear wear − editing service cost (if outsourced) − add-on direct costs − card fees.
    • Per hour = profit ÷ all hours worked, not just the hours on site.
    • Fee for target = the package fee that earns your target per hour for that tier, holding add-ons and costs fixed.
    • Edit vs outsource = your editing minutes per photo priced at your target rate, against the service price per photo.
    • Not modelled: income tax and self-employment tax, rush fees, reshoots, MLS licensing terms, travel surcharges, second-shooter pay and seasonality.

    Common questions

    How much should a real estate photographer charge?

    That depends on your market, but the useful number is what each package earns per hour of total time, not per shoot. A cheap small-condo package that needs a 40-minute drive can pay less per hour than a large house at triple the fee. Enter your own prices and times above.

    Is it worth outsourcing real estate photo editing?

    When your editing time, priced at the hourly rate you want to earn, costs more than the service charges per photo, outsourcing frees hours. It only pays if you resell those hours as shoots. The calculator shows the per-photo comparison and the monthly value.

    Which add-ons are worth selling?

    Each add-on earns its fee minus extra on-site time, extra editing time and direct cost. Raise the percentage of shoots that buy it and watch the average profit per shoot change.

    How many shoots a month do I need?

    The month summary shows profit after overhead and the number of shoots at which pay above your target hourly covers your fixed monthly costs.

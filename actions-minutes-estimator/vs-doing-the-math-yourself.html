This tool vs. doing the math yourself

Doing the math yourself usually means multiplying a job's wall-clock minutes by how often it runs, and stopping there. That gets close on a single small Linux job. It goes wrong in three specific, easy-to-miss ways once a workflow uses a different runner class, a matrix, or a larger runner -- each below with the real arithmetic and a number worked through this tool's own code, not hand-computed.

This tool reads the workflow YAML and applies the same three rules automatically, per job and per matrix leg, so a number it prints has already accounted for all three.

Mistake 1: forgetting the OS multiplier

A minute on a macOS runner does not draw one minute from your included allowance -- it draws 10 (Windows draws 2; Linux draws 1). Hand math that just adds up wall-clock minutes treats every runner the same.

Worked example: a macOS job that runs 15 wall-clock minutes a month. Naive math: 15 minutes against the allowance. What actually happens, run through estimate():

  • Wall-clock minutes: 15
  • Included-allowance minutes actually drawn: 150 -- ten times the naive figure, because RUNNER_CLASSES.macos.multiplier is 10.

On a Free plan's 2,000-minute allowance, that one job alone is 7.5% of the month's included minutes -- from something hand math would have logged as a rounding error.

Mistake 2: rounding a matrix once instead of per job

GitHub rounds each job's duration up to the next whole minute, and it does that per job, not per workflow run. Hand math that multiplies total wall-clock time by a rate rounds once, at the end -- which is the wrong place to round when a matrix is involved.

Worked example: a 12-shard test matrix, each shard finishing in 30 seconds. Naive math: 12 × 0.5 = 6 minutes billed. Run through the same estimate() call:

  • Wall-clock minutes: 6
  • Billed minutes: 12 -- exactly double, because each of the 12 legs rounds its 0.5 minutes up to a full minute on its own before anything is summed.

The gap grows with the matrix, not with the job: a 40-shard matrix at 15 seconds each is 10 wall-clock minutes and 40 billed minutes, a 4x gap from the same rounding rule.

Mistake 3: larger runners drawn from the wrong pool

A larger GitHub-hosted runner (an -8-cores label, for instance) is billed per minute from the first minute, priced per core, and does not draw from your included allowance at all. Hand math tends to either fold it into the same allowance a standard runner uses, or skip it because "it's free minutes like the rest" -- both read the rule backwards.

Worked example: an 8-core Linux runner, 6 wall-clock minutes per run, 120 runs a month (roughly one push an hour during a working day). Run through estimate():

  • Total wall-clock minutes: 720
  • Included-allowance minutes drawn: 0 -- none of this comes out of the free allowance
  • Direct cost: $23.04 a month, billed separately, at $0.004 per core-minute across 8 cores

A workflow using this runner can look free in a spreadsheet that only tracks "minutes against the allowance" -- the allowance column reads zero while the account is actually being charged.

Questions

Is eyeballing a workflow file really that unreliable?

For a single small job on a Linux runner, hand math gets close enough. It stops working the moment any of three things are true: a job runs on macOS or Windows, a job is part of a matrix, or a job uses a larger runner -- each of those changes the arithmetic in a way that is easy to apply backwards from memory, and this tool exists because getting any one of them wrong understates the real bill by a wide margin.

What is the single easiest mistake to make by hand?

Treating a larger runner's minutes as if they draw from the same included allowance as a standard runner. They do not -- a larger runner is billed per minute from the first minute, outside the free allowance entirely, which is the opposite of how a standard macOS or Windows job behaves. Getting this backwards is the single easiest way to project a cost of zero for a job that is actually the most expensive one in the file.

Why does a matrix of short jobs cost more than the wall-clock time suggests?

GitHub rounds each job's duration up to the next whole minute, and it does that per job, not per workflow run. A matrix of twelve 30-second jobs is 6 minutes of wall-clock work, but it is twelve separate roundings to a full minute each -- 12 billed minutes, double the wall-clock figure. Multiplying total wall-clock time by a single per-minute rate misses this because it rounds once instead of twelve times.

Do I need to know the billing rules myself to use this tool?

No, and that is the point of it. The OS multipliers, the per-job rounding, and the larger-runner allowance rule are exactly the arithmetic this tool applies to your pasted YAML, so a number it prints has already accounted for the three mistakes on this page without you having to re-derive them by hand.

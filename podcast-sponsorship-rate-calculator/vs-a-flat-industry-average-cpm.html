This calculator vs. a flat industry-average CPM

A rate report's single "average podcast CPM" number prices a pre-roll, a mid-roll, and a post-roll spot identically. This calculator's benchmark ranges price them differently — and for a real show, using the flat number on all three can mean underselling your best slot and overpricing your weakest one in the same pitch.

What each one prices

A flat industry-average CPM is the single cost-per-1,000- downloads figure podcast ad rate reports and blog posts commonly cite as "the average podcast CPM." Multiply it by your downloads in thousands and you have a price — the same price for a pre-roll spot, a mid-roll spot, and a post-roll spot, and the same price whether the ad is host-read or produced, because a single average has no input for placement or read type at all.

This calculator applies a separate benchmark CPM range to each placement, because sponsors do not value them equally: mid-roll ads sit inside the episode where listener drop-off is lowest and are commonly cited as commanding the highest rate, pre-roll comes next, and post-roll — played after much of the audience has already stopped listening — is priced lowest of the three. It also discounts a produced spot from the host-read benchmark, since a pre-recorded ad carries no live host endorsement.

Common questions

What is a flat industry-average CPM?

A single cost-per-1,000-downloads figure — podcast ad rate reports and blog posts commonly cite one number as "the average podcast CPM" — multiplied against a show's downloads to get an ad price. It needs one number and gives the same answer for a pre-roll, mid-roll, or post-roll spot, and the same answer whether the ad is host-read or produced, because it has no input for either.

Why does a flat CPM misprice individual spots so often?

Because a single average blends three placements that sponsors do not value equally. Mid-roll ads sit inside the episode where listener drop-off is lowest and typically command the highest CPM; pre-roll and especially post-roll are worth less. A flat average sits between all three, so it understates the mid-roll and overstates the post-roll for the same show at the same time.

Does the gap matter at typical show sizes, or only at scale?

It shows up at ordinary sizes. An 8,000-download business show pricing three spots off one flat average leaves real money on the mid-roll and asks a sponsor to overpay for the post-roll — both in the same negotiation, from the same flat number.

Does a flat CPM adjust for host-read vs. produced the way this calculator does?

No — a flat average has no read-type input, so it prices a live host endorsement the same as a pre-recorded studio spot. This calculator's ranges discount produced spots from the host-read benchmark, since a produced ad carries no live endorsement.

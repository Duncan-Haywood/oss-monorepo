This calculator vs. a flat per-foot rate card

$7/ft × 80 feet is $560 whether the dispatch is a scheduled Tuesday afternoon or a midnight call during a storm, and whether the crew is back down the ladder in 2.5 hours or 4.5. A rate card cannot see either difference; this calculator prices both.

What a flat per-foot rate card is

A per-foot rate card is the fastest way to quote over the phone: multiply the footage a customer describes by a number and read off a price. It is easy to compare between competing bids, and it is the number a prospect holding a competitor's flyer will ask you to match.

The number was set once, for an ordinary scheduled job, and then reused for every call afterward — including the ones that are not ordinary: a storm-night dispatch with an after-hours premium, or a job where the ice turns out thicker than the phone estimate assumed.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the footage, crew size and hourly pay, travel hours and miles, steamer fuel and equipment cost, whether the call is an emergency dispatch, overhead, target margin, and a minimum service charge. It turns the actual cost into a price, a break-even rate per foot for that footage, and — once the crew is back down the ladder — a check of the real profit against the actual on-site hours, not the estimate the quote was built on.

A flat per-foot rate card is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when a call turns out to be an emergency dispatch or the hours run past the estimate, and nothing about a per-foot number tells you when either has happened. This calculator does not replace judgment about your market; it replaces the arithmetic a flat rate skips.

Common questions

Does a flat per-foot rate card charge more for an emergency call?

Not unless the operator remembers to mark it up by hand. A rate card is just feet times a number, so an 80-foot job prices at the same $560 whether it is a scheduled Tuesday afternoon appointment or a midnight dispatch during a storm. This calculator applies the entered emergency premium to labor and travel time -- never to fuel or equipment, which cost the same at 2am as at 2pm -- so the real cost of a storm call shows up before the quote goes out.

How much does an unpriced emergency call actually cost in margin?

On this calculator's own default 80-foot job -- a two-person crew at $34/hr each, 2.5 estimated on-site hours, 1 hour of travel, 35 round-trip miles, $20 of steamer fuel, $15 of equipment cost, 12% overhead, a 30% target margin, and a 35% emergency premium -- a scheduled call costs about $332.02 to run and the $560 rate-card quote leaves roughly $227.98 profit, a 40.71% margin. The identical job dispatched as a storm-night emergency costs about $425.32 to run once the premium hits labor and travel; the same $560 quote now leaves about $134.68, a 24.05% margin -- about $47.60 short of the price that would actually hold the 30% target margin.

Does a rate card check itself against the hours the crew actually worked?

No -- a rate card is set from the phone-estimate footage before the truck rolls and never revisited. This calculator does: on the same default job, if the ice turns out thicker than the phone estimate and the crew's 2.5 estimated on-site hours become 4.5 actual hours, the $560 quote does not change but the real cost rises to about $484.34, cutting profit from about $227.98 down to about $75.66 without the operator ever being told the margin moved.

Is a flat per-foot rate card ever the right way to quote?

For a run of similar, scheduled, daytime jobs with dependable ice thickness, a flat rate card can land close enough. The risk is the two things it cannot see by itself: whether a specific call is an emergency dispatch, and whether the hours actually spent match what the phone estimate assumed. Neither shows up in a per-foot number until the invoice is already written.

Does this calculator know what to charge in my market?

No. It has no data on local rates or what competitors charge. It takes the footage, crew, travel, fuel, equipment cost, emergency premium, overhead, and target margin you enter, plus the actual on-site hours once the job is done, and turns that into a price, a break-even rate per foot, and a real profit and margin -- the arithmetic a flat rate card skips, not a market survey.

Nothing here says or implies that quoting a flat per-foot rate card is a bad-faith practice — it is a common, honest shortcut that works until a call turns out to be an emergency dispatch or the hours run past the estimate. This page describes where that shortcut breaks down, not a fault in anyone who uses it. The $332.02, $560.00, $227.98, $425.32, $134.68, $47.60, $484.34, and $75.66 figures above are this calculator's own arithmetic on its own default example job, not a claim about any specific contractor's real costs.

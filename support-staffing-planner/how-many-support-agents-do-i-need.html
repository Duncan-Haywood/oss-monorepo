How many support agents do I need? Erlang C staffing explained

Short answer: enough that the share of contacts answered within your target time is met in every half hour, plus the people who are paid but not on the queue. Dividing workload by a comfortable occupancy looks reasonable and misses the target badly at small volumes. The queueing formula that gets it right is Erlang C.

A worked example

Between 09:00 and 09:30 you expect 30 contacts and each takes 300 seconds (five minutes) to handle. The workload is 30 × 300 / 1,800 = 5 erlangs: five agents would be busy the whole half hour if nothing ever queued.

  • 6 agents is 83% occupancy, but the Erlang C formula says only about 45% of contacts are answered within 20 seconds.
  • 7 agents answers about 72% in 20 seconds.
  • 8 agents answers about 86% in 20 seconds, with an average wait of 17 seconds, at 63% occupancy. That meets an 80% in 20 seconds target.
  • At 30% shrinkage (breaks, meetings, leave) you must schedule 8 / 0.7 = 11.4, so 12 agents for that half hour.

The occupancy-only rule would have staffed 6 and missed the target by a wide margin.

From the requirement to a schedule

Once every half hour has a requirement, you need shifts that cover all of them for the least paid time. The Support Staffing Planner takes your contacts per interval and the shift lengths you allow, finds the cheapest combination of shifts that covers every interval (an exact solution, not a rule of thumb), and compares it with flat peak staffing, with full-length shifts only, and with the occupancy rule. It also prices each service target and shows what a shorter handle time or fewer contacts would save.

What to feed it

  1. Contacts per half hour from your phone, chat or ticket system for a typical week, one row per interval.
  2. Average handle time in seconds, including after-contact work, ideally per interval because it often changes through the day.
  3. Shrinkage from your own schedule and payroll data, and a fully loaded hourly cost.

Erlang C assumes random arrivals and that nobody hangs up while waiting, so real service is usually slightly worse than the formula says. Treat the result as a planning aid and check it against a few real weeks.

Questions

What service level should a small support team aim for?

The common phone target is 80% answered within 20 seconds, but it is a choice, not a rule. Email and chat teams often use 90% within a few minutes. The planner prints what each target costs so you can see what the last ten points are worth.

Why not just divide workload by a target occupancy?

Because queues are not smooth. At 5 erlangs, 6 agents is 83% occupancy, which sounds reasonable, but only about 45% of contacts are answered within 20 seconds. Eight agents answer about 86%.

What shrinkage should I use?

Add up the paid time agents are not on the queue: breaks, meetings, training, leave and sickness. Thirty percent is a common starting figure for phone teams; use your own payroll and schedule data.

This calculator vs. a flat rate per square foot

"$9 a square foot installed" is not a price for a turf job — it is a price for one specific turf type, base depth, and scope, quietly applied to every yard that calls in.

What a flat rate per square foot is

A flat rate per square foot is the fastest way to quote: multiply the lawn's area by a number and read off a price. Homeowners shop it because it is easy to compare between bids, and it is the number a homeowner holding a competitor's flyer will ask you to match.

The number behind it was set for one job — one turf type, one base depth, a prepared pad, no sod demo, no tree wells — and then reused for every yard afterward, whether or not the next yard looks anything like the one the rate was built on.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter the turf type (landscape, pet/play, or putting green/sports), lawn square footage, roll waste percent, crushed-rock base depth, sod demo, perimeter and seam length, tree wells or obstacle cuts, and pet drainage, plus your own labor hours and material costs. It prices turf, base, sod demo, infill, seams, and drainage separately by task and turf type, then returns the walk-away price and a bid price above your target margin — for this job, not a generic one.

What a flat rate per square foot doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same $/sq ft whether the turf is landscape, pet, or putting green — even though pet turf alone adds drainage hours, drainage material, and heavier infill that landscape turf never sees. Prices turf, infill, and drainage per turf type, so landscape, pet, and putting green are never quoted off the same number.
Has no line for sod demo, regardless of whether the existing lawn has to be stripped, hauled, and dumped before turf goes down. Prices sod-demo hours and dump-ton weight directly, so a job with an existing lawn to remove costs more than one on a prepared pad.
Prices the lawn's measured square footage, with no separate line for the roll waste that turf's 15-foot width always leaves on the cutting-room floor. Applies your waste percent to the lawn area to get the turf you actually have to buy, and prices install labor and turf material off that larger number.
Has no line for tree wells, obstacle cuts, or a deeper base, regardless of how many the yard needs. Prices tree wells per opening and base tons per inch of depth, so a yard with two tree wells and a 4-inch base costs more than the same square footage with neither.
Gives the estimator a number to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the walk-away price and the dollar gap against what a flat rate would have quoted the same job, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before you commit to it.

A flat rate is not wrong because it is simple — it is wrong when it is applied outside the one yard it was built for, and nothing about a per-square-foot number tells you when that has happened. This calculator does not know your local market; it replaces the arithmetic a flat rate skips.

Common questions

Why does a flat rate per square foot get a turf quote wrong?

Because the cost driver is turf type and scope, not area. Pet turf on the same footprint as landscape turf needs a heavier turf face weight, drainage hours and material, more infill, and usually a deeper base -- and a job with sod demo or tree wells adds hours and dump weight a landscape-only job never sees. A flat per-square-foot rate charges all of that the same as a bare pad on a prepared lot.

What does a flat rate usually leave out entirely?

Sod demo, base depth, pet drainage, and tree wells or obstacle cuts. A job that needs the existing lawn stripped, hauled, and dumped, plus two tree wells cut into the turf, is doing four things a bare-pad landscape job is not -- and a per-square-foot number has no line for any of them.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

On this calculator's own shipped presets: a 600 sq ft landscape job with sod demo prices at $5,468.14, which works out to $9.11 a square foot. Applying that same $9.11 rate to an 800 sq ft pet-turf job with sod demo and two tree wells quotes $7,290.86 -- $495.89 below that job's own $7,786.75 break-even cost, before a dollar of profit. The flat rate does not just cut the margin on the pet job; it prices it as a loss.

Is a flat rate per square foot ever the right way to quote?

For a narrow band of near-identical jobs -- same turf type, similar base depth, no sod demo or wells either time -- a flat rate can land close. The risk is applying it outside that band without noticing, because the rate does not know which job it was built on and neither does the estimator until the takeoff is actually done.

Does this calculator know what turf or labor costs in my market?

No. It has no data on local material or labor rates. It takes the turf type, lawn square footage, waste percent, base depth, sod demo, seams, tree wells, and the labor hours and material costs you enter, and turns that into a price for this job -- the arithmetic a flat rate skips, not a market survey.

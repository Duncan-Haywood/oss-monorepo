This calculator vs. working out the excess business loss limitation by hand

The arithmetic looks simple — a loss compared against a threshold. The part that goes wrong by hand is which year's threshold, and what counts toward the loss in the first place.

Common questions

What's the single most common mistake working this out by hand?

Assuming the 2026 threshold rose from 2025's, the way almost every other inflation-indexed tax figure does. It actually fell — from $313,000 to $256,000 single/HOH/MFS, and $626,000 to $512,000 MFJ — because OBBBA rebased the indexing formula.

What's the second most common mistake?

Including wages, interest, dividends, or capital gains in the net business loss figure. Only aggregate income and deductions from your trades or businesses count toward the threshold comparison — other income is what the ALLOWED loss then offsets, not part of the loss itself.

What arithmetic mistake gets made most often?

Assuming a joint return doubles automatically without checking — it does, but only because married filing jointly is a genuinely separate, higher figure in the statute, not a rule applied uniformly to every tax threshold. Getting the actual dollar amount wrong for the wrong reason still produces a wrong number.

What planning mistake gets made most often?

Treating a disallowed loss as gone rather than carried forward. The excess above the threshold becomes a net operating loss carryforward, usable against up to 80% of a future year's taxable income — not a permanently lost deduction, but also not immediately as valuable as it would have been this year.

Does this calculator know my complete tax situation?

No. Neither this calculator nor a by-hand worksheet built from this page computes your business income or deductions, models the QBI-deduction interaction, or values a future NOL carryforward. Both apply the same published federal threshold to the numbers you enter; verify against IRS guidance or a qualified tax professional before relying on either.

    Same logic, zero dependencies, one file:

    node cli.mjs next "30 2 * * *" --tz America/New_York
node cli.mjs next "30 2 * * *" --tz America/New_York --count 5 --from 2027-03-13T00:00:00Z --json

node cli.mjs drift 09:00 --tz America/New_York --year 2027 --json

    Get cli.mjs — Node 18+, nothing to install, no network call ever. Both subcommands exit 1 when they find DST risk, so either works as a one-line CI check before a new scheduled job ships.

    What's checked

    CheckHow
    Spring-forward skipA cron occurrence's local wall-clock time (e.g. 02:30) does not exist on that calendar date in the chosen timezone, because clocks jumped forward past it
    Fall-back overlapA cron occurrence's local wall-clock time occurs twice on that calendar date, because clocks were set back through it — both real UTC instants are reported
    Fixed-UTC-cron driftA target local time is converted to UTC once per DST segment of the year; more than one distinct UTC clock time means a single fixed-UTC schedule cannot hold the local time steady

    Month and day-of-week accept the standard 3-letter names (JAN–DEC, SUN–SAT), case-insensitive, anywhere a number is allowed — singly, in a list, or as a range (MON-FRI). Minute, hour, and day-of-month stay numeric. Day-of-month and day-of-week follow the standard cron rule: when both are restricted (neither is *), a date matches either one, not both — the single most common cron misreading.     Common questions

    Why does a cron job skip or double-fire around daylight saving?

    A cron expression that names a wall-clock time (like 02:30) in a timezone that observes DST is naming a local time that, twice a year, either does not exist (spring forward: clocks jump from 02:00 straight to 03:00, so 02:30 never happens that day) or happens twice (fall back: 01:00-01:59 occurs twice). Cron implementations do not agree on what to do then — some skip the run entirely, some fire once, some fire twice — so this tool reports the ambiguity rather than guessing which scheduler you run.

    Why does a schedule drift an hour twice a year even without a skip or double-fire?

    Many schedulers — GitHub Actions' own schedule: cron among them, by its own documentation — always interpret the expression in UTC, with no timezone field at all. A single UTC cron expression cannot hit the same local wall-clock time year-round in any zone that observes DST: it is exactly right for half the year and off by the DST offset, almost always one hour, for the other half. That is not a skip or a double-fire, just a job that quietly runs an hour early or late for months at a time.

    Does this tool know what my actual cron daemon or scheduler will do?

    No, and it does not claim to. Different cron implementations genuinely disagree on spring-forward and fall-back behavior. This tool computes the two facts that are true regardless of implementation — whether a named local time exists uniquely, doesn't exist, or exists twice on a given date — and leaves the scheduler-specific behavior to your own scheduler's documentation.

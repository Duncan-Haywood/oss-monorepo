This calculator vs. a flat monthly fee

“$50 a month, every account” is not a price for a maintenance contract — it is a price for one account's visits, technician hours, and callback risk, quietly applied to every account after it.

What a flat monthly fee is

A flat monthly maintenance-plan fee is the fastest way to sell a preventive agreement: pick a round, competitive-sounding number and quote it to every customer. It is easy to advertise, and it is the figure a homeowner holding a competitor's flyer will ask you to match.

The number behind it was usually set once, off a simple account — one system, a modest visit schedule, little callback history — and then reused for every account afterward, whether the next one has one system or three, four visits a year or eight, and whatever its callback risk turns out to be.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter a plan's preventive visits, technician hours per visit, loaded labor rate, travel, parts reserve, callback risk, annual overhead and target margin. It prices the labor, the reserve, and the overhead as their own lines and returns a break-even floor, a target-margin price, and the exposure of an early cancellation — for this account, not a flat number for every plan under the same name.

What a flat monthly fee doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same figure whether the account has one system on 4 visits a year or several systems on 8 or more. Prices preventive visits and technician hours at your own rate, so an account with more equipment and more visits costs more — on this account, not a plan-name average.
Has no line for callback risk — a plan with a history of return visits gets the same quote as one that never calls back. Adds a callback reserve as its own line, so repeat-visit risk is already funded rather than eating the plan's margin.
Treats the annual fee as spread evenly across the year, with no line for the fact that the first tune-up happens at signing. Prices an early cancellation directly, showing the cost already incurred against the revenue actually collected by that month.
Bakes an assumed overhead into the flat number rather than your shop's own rate. Applies your own overhead allocation to the account, so the price reflects your burden, not an averaged one.
Gives the salesperson a number to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the break-even floor and effective revenue per technician hour, so a shopped price can be scored against real cost before you offer it.

Two accounts, one flat fee

Using this calculator's own defaults, scaled to two account sizes:

A one-system home — 4 preventive visits a year, 2 technician hours each, 1 expected callback — costs $770 a year to service, and this calculator prices it at $80.21 a month ($64.17 break-even floor) at a 20% target margin. A two-system building — 8 visits a year, 2 callbacks, a larger overhead allocation — costs $1,500 a year and this calculator prices it at $156.25 a month ($125.00 break-even floor).

Quote both accounts a flat $50 a month instead and the one-system home brings in $600 a year against its own $770 break-even cost — $170 a year underwater before a dollar of profit. The two-system building brings in the identical $600 a year against a $1,500 break-even cost — a $900-a-year loss. Same flat fee, one account that is merely thin and one that loses money on every visit it gets.

The gap widens further on an early cancellation, because preventive visits are front-loaded: the first tune-up happens at signing, not spread evenly across the year. On the one-system home, a customer who cancels after month 3 has already received two visits worth $337.50 in cost. At the properly priced $80.21 a month, that customer has paid $240.63 by then — a $96.88 loss the contract has already absorbed, which is itself the cost of front-loading. At a flat $50 a month, that same customer has paid only $150 — a $187.50 loss, nearly double, because the lower fee was never checked against the front-loaded cost it has to cover.

Common questions

Why does a flat monthly fee get an HVAC maintenance contract wrong?

Because the cost driver is visit count, technician hours, and callback risk, not "has a maintenance plan." A one-system home on 4 preventive visits a year with a modest callback reserve costs $770 a year to service. A two-system building on 8 visits a year with double the callback reserve costs $1,500 -- almost twice as much -- for the same flat plan-fee marketing copy. A rate that never looks at visit count charges both the same because it was set on neither one.

What does a flat monthly fee usually leave out entirely?

Technician hours per visit, the callback reserve, overhead scaled to the account, and the fact that preventive visits are front-loaded -- the first tune-up happens at signing, before most of a year's fee has been collected. A flat number set once and quoted to every account has no line that moves when the account has more equipment, more callback risk, or cancels early.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

Using this calculator's own defaults: a one-system home on 4 visits a year costs $770 to service and this calculator prices it at $80.21 a month (20% target margin); a two-system building on 8 visits a year costs $1,500 and prices at $156.25 a month. Quote both a flat $50 a month instead and the one-system account brings in $600 a year against a $770 break-even cost -- $170 a year underwater before any profit -- while the two-system account brings in the same $600 against a $1,500 break-even cost, a $900-a-year loss. Same flat fee, one account that is merely thin and one that loses money on every visit it gets.

Why does an early cancellation hurt more than a flat fee assumes?

Because the first tune-up happens at signing, not spread evenly across the year, so a plan cancelled early has already cost more than a flat monthly fee has collected by then. On the one-system account, a customer who cancels after month 3 has already received two visits worth $337.50 in cost. At the properly priced $80.21 a month, that customer has paid $240.63 by month 3 -- a $96.88 loss the contract has already absorbed. At a flat $50 a month, that same customer has paid only $150 -- a $187.50 loss, nearly double, because the lower fee was never checked against the front-loaded cost it has to cover.

Does this calculator know my local labor rates or parts costs?

No. It has no price list and no data on your local labor market or parts supplier pricing. It takes your preventive visits, technician hours, loaded labor rate, callback reserve, parts, travel, overhead, and target margin, and turns that into a price and an early-cancellation exposure for this account -- the arithmetic a flat monthly fee skips, not a market survey.

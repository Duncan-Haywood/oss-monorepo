This calculator vs. doing the cost math yourself

Machine cost plus a bit for labor, divided by a typical order's weight, is the estimate most laundromat and pickup-and-delivery operators reach for when it is time to set or reprice a per-pound rate. It is not wrong on the order it was built around — it is wrong on every order that is not that size.

What each approach actually does

Doing the math yourself usually starts from one typical order: what does a wash and a dry cycle cost, roughly how many minutes of labor, add a bit for supplies, divide by the pounds. That is a fair estimate for an order close to that exact size. It breaks down at the edges: a small drop-off still runs a full wash and dry cycle even if the machine is half empty, so the cost per pound on that order is much higher than the average implies. Intake, tagging and checkout take roughly the same few minutes whether the order is 8 pounds or 30, so that fixed cost gets diluted correctly only on the order it was estimated from. A delivery stop's drive time and mileage do not show up in a per-pound guess at all. And overhead — rent, insurance, the base utility bill, the card processor — is easy to leave out of a mental number entirely.

This calculator costs machine cycles as whole steps, not a smooth rate, so a partial load costs what it actually costs. It keeps per-pound costs (labor minutes, supplies) and per-order costs (fixed intake minutes, delivery stops) separate, so a small order is priced on what it consumes rather than an average across every order size. And it turns a monthly overhead figure and monthly volume into a per-pound share automatically, which is the step a hand-built estimate most often skips.

Common questions

Isn't a rough price per pound close enough?

For an average-sized order with no delivery stop, a careful rough estimate can land close. What it tends to miss is everything that does not scale evenly with weight: a small order still runs a full wash and dry cycle even if the machine is half empty, intake and tagging cost the same whether the order is 8 pounds or 30, a delivery stop costs the same drive time and mileage either way, and overhead (rent, insurance, the base utility bill) has to be spread across pounds somehow. Each of those pulls the true cost per pound on a small order well above the average the rough guess used.

What does this calculator do that mental math can't?

It costs machine cycles as whole steps rather than a smooth per-pound rate, so a partially filled washer or dryer shows up as the full cycle it actually costs. It separates per-pound costs (labor minutes, supplies) from per-order costs (fixed intake minutes, delivery stops) so a small order is priced on what it actually consumes rather than an average across all order sizes. And it turns monthly overhead and monthly volume into a per-pound share automatically, rather than leaving that step out entirely, which is the most common gap in a hand-built estimate.

When is a rough estimate the better choice?

When every order runs close to the same size, uses close to a full machine load, and never involves a delivery stop, a rough per-pound number and this calculator will land close together — there is little for the per-order and stepwise-machine effects to bite on. The calculator earns its keep on the order that is not typical: a small drop-off that still eats a full cycle, or a delivery route stop whose drive time and mileage do not show up in a flat per-pound guess at all.

Why is your 1099-K higher than what you actually made?

Every filing season, resellers and gig-marketplace sellers open a Form 1099-K and don't recognize the number in Box 1a — it's often thousands of dollars more than what actually landed in their bank account after fees, refunds, and taxes. That gap is not a mistake on the form. It's how Box 1a is defined.

What Box 1a actually is

The IRS's own General Instructions for Forms 1099-K describe Box 1a as the gross amount of reportable payment-card and third-party network transactions for the year — reported "without regard to any adjustments for credits, cash equivalents, discounts, fees, refunds, or other amounts." In plain terms: it's the total dollar value of what moved through the platform's payment system tied to your account, before anything is subtracted.

Two specific things that inflate it

Sales tax the marketplace collected and remitted for you. Under marketplace-facilitator laws now in effect everywhere that levies sales tax, a large marketplace calculates, collects, and pays over sales tax on a seller's behalf automatically — the seller never sees that money and has no filing obligation for it. But the tax is still part of what the buyer paid, so it commonly counts toward Box 1a's gross total anyway.

Refunds. Because Box 1a is defined without regard to refunds, a sale you later refunded in full can still be counted in the gross figure, even though the buyer got their money back.

Neither of these is your income. Both need to be backed out before the number means anything about what you actually made.

What to actually do with it

  1. Pull the Box 1a total from each 1099-K you received.
  2. For each one, find (from the platform's own payout or tax summary, not a guess) how much of that total was refunds and how much was sales tax the platform remitted for you.
  3. Subtract your real costs — cost of goods sold, platform fees, shipping you paid — from what's left.
  4. What remains is your estimated net profit from that platform — the figure that actually belongs in a profit-and-loss calculation, not Box 1a itself.

The 1099-K reconciliation worksheet does exactly this arithmetic — for one platform or several at once — and exports the result as a CSV you can hand to whoever prepares your return.

Common questions

What does Form 1099-K Box 1a actually report?

The IRS's own General Instructions for Forms 1099-K define Box 1a as the gross amount of reportable payment transactions for the calendar year, "without regard to any adjustments for credits, cash equivalents, discounts, fees, refunds, or other amounts." It is the total dollar amount of transactions the platform processed for you, full stop — not a profit figure, and not even a figure net of money that was never yours to begin with.

Why would a platform include sales tax I never kept?

Under marketplace-facilitator laws now in effect in every state that levies sales tax, large marketplaces are required to calculate, collect, and remit sales tax on a seller's behalf — the seller never receives that money or has to file it themselves. But that tax is still part of the dollar amount the buyer paid and the platform processed, so it commonly ends up inside Box 1a's gross total. It was never the seller's income and does not belong in a profit calculation.

What about a refund I gave a buyer?

Box 1a is defined without regard to refunds, so a sale that was later refunded can still show up in the gross total even though the money went back to the buyer. Some platforms report refunds in a separate statement or a later box; not all break it out clearly on the 1099-K itself, which is exactly why it is worth reconciling by hand against your own payout history rather than assuming the form nets it out for you.

Why your overage fee might be losing you money

An overage rate looks like it protects your margin: the customer fills the container past the included allowance, you charge them a per-ton fee for the extra weight. But that fee only protects anything if it is priced above what disposal actually costs you. Set it below your own tipping fee, and every ton over the allowance loses money — on the exact jobs, heavy tear-offs and demo debris, where the most overage tons pile up.

Why this is easy to get wrong

The base rental price hides it. A well-priced base rental — say $351 for a 20-yard container with a 2-ton allowance, 7 days, and a healthy 30% target margin — looks perfectly profitable on paper. That price only has to cover disposal up to the allowance; it says nothing about whether the overage rate is safe.

Overage rates often get set by "what competitors charge," not by cost. A round number like $50/ton looks reasonable next to a $65/ton tipping fee, until you notice it's below the tipping fee, not above it — the fee is already losing $15 on every ton before your labor, mileage, or overhead are even counted.

The risk doesn't show up until the heavy job. A light cleanout that stays under the allowance never touches the overage rate at all. It's the shingle tear-off, the concrete tear-out, or the wet-dirt job — material that runs heavy for its volume — that pushes weight well past the allowance and turns a mispriced overage fee into a real loss.

Worked example

A container is rented at the price above: 20 yards, 2-ton allowance, 7 included days, a $65/ton tipping fee, driver labor and mileage totaling about $76, $20 equipment cost, 10% overhead, and a 30% target margin — quoting a base price of $351.01 with $105.30 of built-in profit. The overage rate is set at $50/ton, $15 below the tipping fee. A roofing tear-off comes back on the scale at 10 tons — 8 tons over the allowance:

A job that started with over $105 of built-in profit at the allowance ends up a small loss once the real weight ticket comes in — and it only takes about 7 overage tons for this specific rental's entire built-in profit to disappear (the calculator reports this number, maxOverageTonsBeforeLoss, for your own numbers). A heavier job than this one — a full container of shingles instead of mixed debris — would lose far more, because nothing about this overage rate has a floor.

Common questions

How do I know if my dumpster overage rate is priced correctly?

Compare it directly to your own tipping fee per ton, not to what competitors advertise. If your overage rate is lower than what the transfer station charges you per ton, every ton the customer goes over the included allowance costs you more to dispose of than you collect for it.

Why would a $351 rental job lose money?

Because the rental price only covers disposal up to the included weight allowance. A shingle tear-off or a concrete job can weigh far more than a light cleanout at the same container size, and if the overage rate charged for the extra tons is below the tipping fee, the loss on those tons can exceed the profit built into the base price.

Does raising the base rental price fix a mispriced overage rate?

Only up to a point. A higher base price gives the job more built-in profit to absorb losing overage tons before it goes negative, but the overage rate itself keeps losing money on every ton past that point — there is no ceiling on a heavy enough load. Fixing the overage rate itself, so it covers the tipping fee with room for margin, is the only fix that scales.

How do I estimate the weight before the job, to know if this risk applies?

By material, not container size: shingles, concrete, dirt, and brick run far heavier per yard than furniture, cardboard, or general household junk. A 20-yard container of shingles can weigh several times what the same container holds in light cleanout debris, which is why the same allowance and overage rate can be safe for one job type and a loss for another.

      FOR WINDOW REPLACEMENT CONTRACTORS

      Bid the opening, not the window.

      A 3040 double-hung is not a 6048 picture window, a second-floor bedroom is not a first-floor insert, and a rotten sill is a carpentry job hiding inside a “window” line. A flat price per window cannot see any of that. Price United Inches, the story, and the tear-out — then see the walk-away number before a $550-a-window special eats the week.

        Common questions

        Why price by United Inches instead of per window?

        Because United Inches (width + height, in inches) is the unit the glass shop actually prices, and a flat per-window number cannot see it. Each opening's material starts from its type's base cost and adds a per-inch rate for every United Inch past a 70 UI baseline — roughly a stock 3040 double-hung. Labor gets a size step too: nothing extra up to 90 UI, about 0.45 h more past that, and about 1.1 h more past 120 UI. A 6048 picture window at 108 UI is not the same job as the 3040 the "$550 a window" postcard was written around.

        What's the difference between an insert and a full-frame replacement?

        An insert (pocket) leaves the existing frame in place and is the baseline here — no extra hours, no extra parts. Full-frame tears out to the studs, which adds about 1.4 h and $55 of material per opening. New-construction nail-fin sits between them at about 0.35 h and $28. The installation method is chosen per opening, so a house where three openings need full-frame and nine take inserts prices as the mixed job it is.

        How do rot, masonry, and pre-1978 lead work change the bid?

        They are opening-level conditions, each with their own hours and parts on top of the unit: sill or jamb rot repair adds about 1.8 h and $65, a masonry or brickmold cut-in about 1.6 h and $40, and lead-safe work practices on a pre-1978 house about 0.9 h and $48. These are ballpark starting figures for your own estimate, not a code requirement, an RRP compliance determination, or a finding that any particular opening contains lead — that is your inspection and your certification, not this page's.

        Why does the second story cost more when the window is the same?

        The unit is the same; the carry, the ladder, and the setup are not. A second-storey opening adds about 0.75 h and a third storey or higher about 2.1 h, per opening. Scaffolding or a lift is a separate flat cost, because it is a job-level rental decision rather than something that scales per window. Frame material scales the hours too — wood and clad-wood run about 20% slower than vinyl, fiberglass about 5%, aluminum slightly faster.

        Why does a small job still come out at a few hundred dollars?

        Because a one-window stop is mostly not the window. Round-trip drive time is priced from the miles and speed you enter, setup is its own line, there is a per-opening disposal figure for the old unit, and a minimum on-site hours floor applies when the real work comes in under it — a crew that shows up has a half-day gone whatever the takeoff says. Add the flat trip or truck fee and permits, and the floor for showing up is visible instead of buried.

        Why isn't a 20% margin the same as adding 20% to my cost?

        Margin here is a share of the final price — price = job cost ÷ (1 − margin) — which is how the quote reads to the customer and how your books read it. Overhead is a separate markup on the job's direct cost, applied before margin and never to itself or to profit. A $8,000 cost marked up 20% is $9,600 and only a 16.7% margin; a 20% target margin is $10,000.

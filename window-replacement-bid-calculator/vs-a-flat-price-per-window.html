This calculator vs. a flat price per window

Window crews are shopped by a flat price per window. That rate cannot see the job — United Inches, story, frame, install method, and tear-out condition do not scale with a window count, and two jobs with the same number of openings can price over four times apart.

What a flat price per window is

A price per window is the fastest number to quote over the phone: count the openings, multiply by a rate, read back a total. It is also the unit homeowners already compare between competing bids, and the shape of the postcard special every crew gets shopped against.

The rate behind it was learned from a specific job — a given size, a given story, an insert into a clean opening — and then reused for the next window count that calls in, whether or not that next job shares any of those.

What this calculator is

This page keeps no account and sends nothing to a server. You enter a per-opening takeoff — width and height in inches (United Inches), quantity, story, frame, install method, and tear-out condition — plus your own wage, payroll burden, travel, scaffolding, disposal, overhead, and target margin. It prices each opening's material and hours from its own size, story, frame, install method, and condition, not a rate carried over from the last job.

What a flat per-window rate doesWhat this calculator does instead
Charges the same rate for a stock 3040 double-hung and a 6048 picture window, even though United Inches and material cost can differ several times over. Prices each opening's material from its type, frame, and United Inches past a 70 UI baseline, so a larger unit costs more without anyone eyeballing a surcharge.
Has no line for story — a first-floor opening and a second-floor opening get the same rate. Adds ladder and setup hours per story, and prices scaffolding or a lift separately when any priced opening is on the second story or higher.
Has no line for install method — an insert that leaves the frame in place and a full-frame tear-out to the studs get the same rate. Prices insert, full-frame, and new-construction install methods as their own hours and material adders, per opening.
Has no line for condition — sill or jamb rot, a masonry cut-in, and pre-1978 lead-safe work are invisible inside a "window" line. Prices each condition as its own hours and parts, on top of the opening.
Gives the crew a number to quote, not a number to check a quote against. Reports the price per opening and the walk-away price alongside the total, so a shopped rate can be scored against real cost before you match it.

Same window count, two different jobs

Using this calculator's own shipped cost inputs — $32 hourly wage, 28% burden, 12 miles one way, $150 scaffolding when needed, $18 disposal per opening, 12% overhead, 25% target margin:

8 stock first-floor double-hung windows (30″×40″, 70 United Inches), installed as vinyl inserts into clean openings, take 15.6 crew-hours and price at $4,095.30 for the job — $511.91 per window.

The same 8 windows, but oversized second-floor wood casements (48″×64″, 112 United Inches), replaced full-frame with sill rot repair, plus interior casing and exterior capping on every opening, take 71.6 crew-hours, cost $14,107.24 to run before a dollar of profit, and this calculator prices the job at $18,809.65 — $2,351.21 per window. Quoting the harder job at the easy job's learned rate of $511.91 a window gives $4,095.30 for the same 8 openings, which is $10,011.94 below the harder job's own break-even cost — before counting the $14,714.35 it falls short of this calculator's own price at a 25% target margin. Identical window count, more than four and a half times the labor, and a rate learned on the ground floor loses money the moment the crew opens the wall on the second.

Common questions

Why does a flat price per window get a replacement bid wrong?

Because a window count only measures how many openings there are, not what each one takes. 8 stock first-floor double-hung windows, installed as inserts in clean openings, take about 15.6 crew-hours. 8 oversized second-floor wood casements, replaced full-frame with sill rot repair plus interior casing and exterior capping, take about 71.6 crew-hours -- the same count of windows, more than four and a half times the labor, because United Inches, story, frame, install method, and condition do not scale with a window count.

What does a flat per-window rate usually miss entirely?

United Inches (a 6048 picture window is not the same material cost as a 3040 double-hung), story access (a second-floor opening adds ladder and setup time a first-floor opening does not), frame material (wood and clad-wood run slower and cost more than vinyl), install method (full-frame tear-out to the studs adds far more than an insert that leaves the frame in place), and condition (sill or jamb rot repair is a carpentry job hiding inside a "window" line). A flat rate has no line for any of them -- it is one number learned on one job and reused for the next window count that calls in.

How big is the pricing gap in practice?

Using this calculator's own shipped cost inputs: 8 stock first-floor double-hung inserts take 15.6 crew-hours and price at $4,095.30 for the job -- $511.91 per window. 8 oversized second-floor wood casements, full-frame with rot repair plus interior casing and exterior capping, take 71.6 crew-hours, cost $14,107.24 to run before a dollar of profit, and this calculator prices the job at $18,809.65 -- $2,351.21 per window. Quoting the harder job at the easy job's learned rate of $511.91 a window gives $4,095.30 for the same 8 openings, which is $10,011.94 below the harder job's own break-even cost -- before counting the $14,714.35 it falls short of this calculator's own price at a 25% target margin.

Is a flat price per window ever a reasonable shorthand?

For a narrow band of similar openings -- the same size class, the same story, the same frame, an insert install, no rot -- a per-window number can land close. The risk is quoting an oversized unit, a second or third story, a full-frame tear-out, or sill rot the same way sight unseen, because the rate does not know any of that until the crew opens the wall, and by then the per-window number has often already been quoted.

Does this calculator know my local labor rates or unit prices?

No. It has no data on your local labor market or your supplier's unit pricing. It takes a per-opening takeoff with width, height, quantity, story, frame, install method, and condition, plus your own wage, payroll burden, travel, scaffolding, disposal, overhead, and target margin, and turns that into crew hours and a price for this job -- the arithmetic a flat per-window rate skips, not a market survey.

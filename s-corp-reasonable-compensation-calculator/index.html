Is your S-corp salary defensible — and what does closing the gap cost?

A single-shareholder S-corp that also performs services for the business must pay a "reasonable" W-2 salary before taking any profit out as a distribution. There is no IRS safe-harbor percentage — the test is a comparable-wage comparison: what an unrelated employer would pay someone else to do your job, for your hours. Common questions

What is "reasonable compensation" for an S-corp owner?

A shareholder of an S-corp who also performs services for the business must be paid a reasonable salary as W-2 wages before any profit is distributed to them. The IRS and the courts test this by comparing your pay to what an unrelated employer would pay someone else to do the same job, for the same hours — not by any fixed percentage of profit.

Is there an IRS safe-harbor percentage, like paying yourself 60% of profit as salary?

No. The IRS has never published a safe-harbor percentage, and the "60/40" or "1/3" rules of thumb circulating online are informal guidance from advisors, not an IRS rule. This calculator does not use a percentage rule; it compares your planned salary to a comparable-wage figure you supply for your own role and hours, which is the test actually applied in litigated cases.

What happens if I pay myself too little salary and take the rest as distributions?

The IRS can recharacterize part of your distributions as wages, assess back payroll tax (both the employer and employee shares of FICA), plus penalties and interest. Watson v. Commissioner (8th Cir. 2012) is the leading case: a $24,000 salary against roughly $200,000–274,000 of annual profit was recharacterized to $91,044.

Can my S-corp pay me more salary than it actually earned this year?

A corporation is not generally expected to pay out more in salary than it earned in a lean year — the reasonable-compensation rule targets disguised distributions from profit that exists, not a shortfall when there isn't any. If your comparable-wage figure exceeds what the business made, that is a signal to talk to your own preparer about the options, not a hard floor this calculator enforces.

Why does raising my salary cost more in FICA tax?

W-2 salary is subject to combined Social Security and Medicare tax (FICA) — 15.3% combined below the annual Social Security wage base, 2.9% above it, plus an extra 0.9% employee-only Medicare tax above a fixed income threshold. Distributions from an S-corp are not subject to FICA. That tax difference is exactly why the IRS scrutinizes a low salary paired with a large distribution — it is the incentive to understate salary, not a loophole this calculator is built to help you exploit.

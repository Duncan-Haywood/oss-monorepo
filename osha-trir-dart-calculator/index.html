        Common questions

        What is TRIR?

        TRIR (Total Recordable Incident Rate) is the number of OSHA-recordable injuries and illnesses per 100 full-time workers per year: recordable cases times 200,000, divided by total hours worked by all employees. 200,000 is OSHA's own base rate — the hours 100 full-time employees work in a year (40 hours a week, 50 weeks) — so the rate is comparable across companies of any size.

        What is DART rate?

        DART rate uses the same formula as TRIR but counts only cases involving Days Away from work, Restricted duty, or job Transfer — the recordable cases serious enough to keep someone from doing their normal job. It is always less than or equal to TRIR, because every DART case is also a recordable case, but not every recordable case is a DART case (a case needing only medical treatment beyond first aid is recordable but not DART).

        Does this tool decide whether an injury is OSHA recordable?

        No. Recordability is a judgment call made against OSHA's own recordkeeping rule (29 CFR 1904.7) — first aid vs. medical treatment, work-relatedness, and the general recording criteria — and it stays the employer's own determination, case by case. This tool starts from the counts already on your OSHA 300 log and does the rate arithmetic; it does not review, or reach a conclusion about, any individual case.

        Why does a GC prequalification form or insurance renewal ask for this?

        Many general contractors set a TRIR ceiling (often 3.0, or their trade's own published industry average) below which a subcontractor cannot bid, and workers' comp carriers use the same rate as one input to a renewal premium. A company that only calculates its rate once a year, when the OSHA 300A summary is due, is often seeing the number for the first time on the same form that is about to gate a bid or a renewal.

        How is the injury-free hours figure calculated?

        It holds your current recordable case count fixed and solves for how many more hours worked, with no new recordable case, would dilute the rate down to your target: recordable cases times 200,000, divided by the target rate, minus the hours already worked. It is not a prediction and it does not assume anything about future incidents — it is the arithmetic answer to 'if nothing else happens, how much more injury-free work does it take to reach that number.'

Why did our HOA get hit with a special assessment?

A board that watches its reserve balance grow every year can still be blindsided by a special assessment, because the balance in dollars is not the number a reserve study actually warns on. The warning is "percent funded" — the balance measured against what the components already in service currently owe toward their own next replacement — and it can cross the danger line a year or more before the balance itself turns negative.

A fund that looks fine one year and is flagged the next

Take an association with a $150,000 starting balance, a $65,000 annual contribution, and a typical component list — roof, repaving, exterior painting, pool resurfacing, elevator, and common-area HVAC — each already partway through its own service life. Run it forward year by year:

YearBalancePercent fundedSpecial-assessment risk?
1$218,000.0031.6%Above the 30% target
2 (after exterior painting is replaced)$185,593.6327.3%Below the 30% target

The balance is still six figures and still higher than several years ago. The board has not changed its contribution. What moved is the denominator: exterior painting's replacement pulled cash out of the fund in the same year that every other component's own prorated share of its (inflating) replacement cost kept climbing. Percent funded fell from just above the board's 30% target to just below it — the exact trigger a reserve study flags as special-assessment risk, a year before the balance itself would have shown any sign of trouble.

What it actually costs to fix, one way or the other

For this same association, clearing every year of a 30-year horizon at this contribution rate is not possible — the minimum contribution that avoids a shortfall works out to about $118,760.06 a year, against the $65,000 currently being collected. Expressed per unit across 120 units, the board has two ways to close that gap:

  • Phase it in as a dues increase: about $37.33 per unit per month, starting now, spread across every remaining year of the horizon.
  • Wait and pay it as a special assessment: about $151.28 per unit, due in one lump sum the year the shortfall actually hits (year 2, in this projection).

Both numbers describe the same underlying gap. The difference is entirely about who is asked to plan for it and when — a board that never runs the projection only ever sees the second option, and only once it is no longer a projection.

Common questions

If the balance was still six figures, why did the board get a warning at all?

Because the warning a reserve study issues is not about the balance in isolation — it is about the balance against what the components already in service currently owe toward their own replacement, which is what "percent funded" measures. A fund can grow every single year in dollar terms and still fall as a percentage, the moment the components' own prorated obligation grows faster than the balance does — which is exactly what a single mid-sized replacement, like exterior painting, can do the year after everything looked fine.

Why does one component replacement move the percentage so much?

Two things move at once. The replacement itself pulls cash straight out of the balance, and every other component's currently-owed share keeps climbing whether or not anything was replaced that year, because it is prorated against how much of that component's life has already elapsed. So the numerator drops and the denominator rises in the same year — which is why percent funded can swing several points in a single year even though no single number in the board's own contribution changed at all.

Is inflation the reason the numbers get worse than a board expects?

It is a real part of it, and it compounds on every component every year, not only the one being replaced that year — a roof due in year eight is priced at year-eight replacement cost, not what it would have cost today, and every other component's currently-owed share is revalued at its own inflated replacement cost the same year. A board that mentally prices its roof at today's contractor quote is underestimating the obligation the moment more than a year or two stands between now and the replacement.

What actually determines whether a board sees this coming?

Whether the projection is run at all, and how far out. A single current-year balance and a single current-year contribution say nothing about which future year a cluster of components lands in, or whether this year's contribution rate still clears every year of a multi-decade horizon once every component's replacement is inflated to the year it actually happens. That is a year-by-year simulation, not a snapshot, and it is what this calculator runs before the board is the one finding out the hard way.

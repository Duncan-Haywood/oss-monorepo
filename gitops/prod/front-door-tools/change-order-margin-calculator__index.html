Common questions

What is the difference between the walk-away price and the target price?

The walk-away price is your cost floor — loaded labor plus materials, subcontractor cost, and overhead, with zero profit. The target price divides that floor by one minus your target margin, so it already has the margin built in. On the defaults (8 hours at $28/hr, 25% burden, $350 materials, 10% overhead, 20% target margin), the floor is $693.00 and the target price is $866.25 — the gap between them is exactly the profit a 20% margin requires.

Why is loaded labor $280 for 8 hours at $28/hour, not $224?

Because payroll burden is applied to the wage before multiplying by hours: hours × wage × (1 + burden). At the default 25% burden that adds $56 — the employer-side taxes and insurance cost of that labor, not profit. Change-order pricing built on the $224 bare-wage number would be quietly working at a loss on every hour billed.

Why does overhead apply to materials and subcontractor cost, not just labor?

Overhead is a percentage of the whole direct cost — loaded labor plus materials plus subcontractor cost — because the business absorbs its overhead on every dollar that flows through a job, not only the payroll dollars. On the defaults that direct cost is $630.00, and 10% overhead on that full figure is $63.00.

What does “above cost, but below your target margin” mean?

It means the proposed price covers your walk-away floor and leaves some profit, just not as much as your target margin asks for. On the defaults, proposing $750 against the $693 floor leaves $57 of profit — but margin is profit divided by price, not by cost, so $57 / $750 is 7.6%, short of a 20% target by 12.4 points. A price can clear cost by a comfortable-looking dollar amount and still miss the margin you were aiming for.

My contract caps change-order markup. Does that change what I can charge?

Yes, and it is the field most likely to change your answer. Enter the cap and the calculator shows the most the contract permits you to bill — direct cost plus that percentage — next to what is left of it after your own overhead. The two come out of the same allowance, so a 10% cap against 10% overhead means the change order earns exactly nothing: on the defaults the cap and the $693.00 walk-away floor are the same number. Below that, at a 5% cap, the most you may bill is $661.50 and every change order on the contract is a loss. Leave the field at zero for a contract with no cap and nothing changes.

The added work pushes the schedule. Where does that cost go?

Into "days added" and "extended general conditions ($/day)" — the supervision, site trailer, and rented plant the added scope keeps on site. It is treated as a direct cost of the change order, so overhead and margin carry on it like any other, which is the point: general conditions absorbed by the contractor are absorbed because they were never in the change order to begin with. Three days at $450 turns a $630 change order into $1,980 of direct cost and moves the target price from $866.25 to $2,722.50. Leave both at zero for work that fits inside the existing schedule.

Why does the cap warning appear before I enter a proposed price?

Because the cap is a fact about your contract rather than about the price you are considering. If the markup the contract allows is below your overhead rate, every change order under it loses money whatever you propose, and waiting for a proposed price would keep that silent on exactly the page you have not finished filling in. The other messages still wait for a proposed price, because they are comparisons against one.

Why is target margin capped at 99%?

The target price divides the cost floor by (1 − target margin). At 100% that division is by zero; the calculator caps the input at 99% so a mistyped margin produces a very large, clearly wrong price instead of an error or an infinite one.
